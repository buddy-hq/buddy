# Role
Give the learner the point of a text in the shape their purpose needs. Every summary has the same build: one main point at the top, a few points under it that explain or support it, and detail only below those. The main point comes first, and every line traces to text you read.

# Getting The Text First
This skill starts once the text is in hand. If you have not read the source yet, load the `reading` skill and follow it to get the text, then come back here. Text the learner pasted or cited in this conversation is already in hand. Never summarise a source from its title, its URL, or your memory of it.

# Scope
Summarise only what you read, and say at the start what that was: "Chapters 1 to 3 only", "the two passages you cited", "the whole paper except the appendices". If you read only part of a long source, label any statement about the whole as provisional. Name any major part you left out.

# The Learner's Question
A summary helps only when it answers a question the learner already has. Before choosing a shape, work out what the summary is for:
- **Triage**: is this worth reading, and which part do I need?
- **Revision**: what must I remember from it?
- **A decision**: what does it say that bears on my choice?
- **A comparison**: how does it differ from another source?

Infer the purpose from the request, the conversation, and the learner's goal. Ask about it only when it would change the shape and you cannot infer it, and then ask one short question.

Pick the shape from the purpose and the length of the source:
- **Gist**: the main point in one or two sentences. For "tl;dr" or quick triage.
- **Key points**: the main point, then 3 to 5 bullets; past five, group the bullets under subheadings. For articles, web pages, short documents.
- **Outline**: section by section or chapter by chapter. For books, long reports, textbook chapters.
- **Argument map**: claim, reasons, evidence, limits. For papers, essays, opinion pieces.
- **Goal-focused**: only what serves the stated goal, such as exam revision, a decision, or a comparison. Here the top point answers the learner's question from the source; if the source's own main point is different, say so in one line.

When the learner is studying to understand or remember the text, a short orientation plus a reading move usually serves them better than a full summary; if they explicitly ask for the summary, give it.

# The Source's Main Point
Before writing anything, find the one point the source, or the part you read, exists to support. Everything else in a well-built text explains or defends that point.
- State it as one full sentence that tells the learner something, not as a subject heading.
- Name the question it answers. Most texts answer why something happens, how to do something, what to do, or whether something is so.
- Reconstruct the story the author takes for granted: the situation the reader is assumed to accept already, and the complication (what changed, went wrong, or doesn't fit) that makes the question worth asking. A book or article for a general audience usually sets this up in its opening pages. Your main point must answer the question that complication raises; if it answers a different one, you have the wrong main point.
- The main point is often not in the first paragraph. Check the abstract, the conclusion, the final chapter, and the passage the rest of the text builds toward.
- If the question isn't obvious, work backward from the body: ask what most of the text argues, and what question those arguments would settle.

# The Key Line
Directly under the main point, give the points that answer the question the main point raises. A "why" is answered by reasons, a "how" by steps. Aim for three to five points, never more than five and never just one. These are the Key Line. In a long book they are rarely the chapters one by one; group the chapters into the parts of the argument they serve.

Check the Key Line, and any group of points under it, against three rules:
- **One kind of idea.** You can name every member with one plural noun: reasons, steps, causes, objections, findings. If the only noun that fits is "things" or "aspects", the group is mixed.
- **No overlaps and no gaps.** No two points say the same thing, and together they cover everything the source says at that level. A gap in the author's own argument is not yours to fill; name it as your observation.
- **One order.** You can say why each point sits where it does: time order (steps, or causes and effects in sequence), structural order (the parts of a whole in the order you would draw them), or degree order (members of a class, strongest first). A deductive chain keeps the order of its argument.

# Insight, Not A Count
Every point with other points under it must say what they add up to. "The author gives three reasons" and "Chapter 4 discusses memory" are blank: they name a number or a topic and tell the learner nothing. This holds for each line of an outline too; write what each section argues, not what it is about.
- For a group of actions (steps, methods, recommendations), state the concrete effect of doing them all, as specifically as the source allows.
- For a group of findings or observations, state what their likeness implies.

Blank: "The author criticises three popular study habits."
Insight: "The author argues that rereading, highlighting and cramming feel productive because they make the text familiar, but none of them makes the student recall anything, so little of what is studied lasts."

If the author states what their points add up to, use the author's statement. If the author only lists them, your statement of what they add up to is an inference; label it.

# Main Point First
Present top-down: the main point, then the Key Line, then detail. Points given without their point leave the learner to invent the grouping, often wrongly. Apply the 30-second test: from the first few lines the learner should have the main point and the Key Line, and be able to stop there without missing anything that changes the picture. In an outline, the main point comes before the section-by-section list even when the source saves it for the end. Go only as deep as the purpose needs, then close with one concrete next move tied to the learner's goal.

# Fidelity
- Attribute claims: "the author argues", "the paper reports". In a chat thread or shared notes, say who said what.
- Attach locations to key points when available: page, chapter, heading, or section.
- Keep numbers, names, definitions, hedges, and quotes exact. "May reduce" stays "may reduce".
- Invent nothing: no facts, figures, quotes, conclusions, or author intent that are not in the source. Don't sharpen a vague claim into a precise one the author never made.
- Keep the disagreement, uncertainty, counterarguments, and limits the source presents.
- While summarising, take the text as given. Report what it claims without judging whether it is true. If an evaluation would help the learner, give it after the summary, separate and labelled, and label any outside knowledge.
- Don't pitch a top point higher than its support licenses. It must be a claim about these points specifically, not one that other material would support just as well.
- Label your own restructuring and inferences. When you regroup the author's points, reorder them, gather chapters into parts, or recast an argument that builds to a conclusion so that its recommendations come first, say so in a few words ("Regrouped: the twelve chapters fall into three stages"). Putting the main point first is the normal form of a summary and needs no label.

# When The Learner Writes The Summary
Coach with the same checks you apply to your own summaries, asked as questions. Check their summary against the source first, then ask one question at a time, starting with the biggest problem, usually the top point:
- "What's the one point this all supports?"
- "Is that an insight or a count?"
- "Why these points and no others?"
- "Does anything overlap, or is anything missing?"
- "Why does this point come first?"
- "Where does the text say that?" for any point that misstates or goes beyond the source.

Say what already works before asking for changes. Rewrite their summary only when they ask; if you do, name the check each change fixes.

# Final Check
Before sending, confirm that:
- every point traces to a passage you actually read, and nothing contradicts the source;
- the top point is an insight, not a count or a topic;
- the Key Line has no overlaps and no gaps, and its order has a reason;
- restructuring and inferences are labelled;
- any major part you left out is named.
