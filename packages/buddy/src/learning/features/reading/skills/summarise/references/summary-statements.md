# Summary Statements

Use this when a top point comes out blank ("three reasons", "several findings", "Chapter 4 covers memory") or you can't see what a group of points adds up to. Stating what a group adds up to is where the thinking gets finished; a group left under a count or a topic has not been understood yet.

# Before You State Anything
Make two checks first.
1. **Is the group sound?** It holds one kind of idea, has no overlaps or gaps, and has an order. You can't summarise a group that isn't; fix it with grouping-and-order.md.
2. **Are these action ideas or situation ideas?** Action ideas tell someone to do something: steps, methods, recommendations, objectives. Situation ideas say that something is the case: findings, causes, problems, observations. Sum up actions by the effect of doing them; sum up situations by what their likeness implies.

For a deductive group, none of this applies: the top point is the chain's conclusion, with a "because".

# Action Groups: State The Effect
A complete set of actions produces one effect, and that effect is the summary. You can only judge whether the set is complete once the effect is stated, and you can only state the effect once the actions are clear, so break the circle this way:
1. Pare each action to its barest form: who does what to what.
2. Separate the levels. An action is at the same level as the next if it is done before it; it sits below the next if it is done in order to produce it.
3. Word each action by its end product: what the person would be holding when it is done.
4. Read the effect off those end products, and check it: if every lower result were in place, would the stated effect follow? What would fail to happen without these actions? Reject an effect so broad that a little progress on any one action would satisfy it.

Example: a chapter on reading papers advises reading the abstract, then the figures, then the conclusion, then deciding whether the methods are worth reading. The effect is a decision, made before any full read, on whether the paper deserves one. "The author describes a four-step method" says nothing; "The author's method lets a reader decide whether a paper is worth a full read before committing to one" says what the steps are for.

Levels in the same chapter: "skim the headings" and "read the chapter summary" are done in order to preview the chapter, so they sit under "preview"; "preview", "read", and "recall" are each done before the next, so they sit side by side.

Keep end-product wording faithful. State the effect as specifically as the source allows, with its numbers and deadlines if it gives them. If the source's actions are vague, say what it does promise and don't invent a measurable target it never set.

For long or tangled action statements, strip each sentence to its real subject and the action applied to it. Then ask what the subjects have in common and what the actions have in common; the shared kind of subject and the shared kind of action together give the effect.

When a source sorts its actions into separate lists of tasks, objectives, and benefits, the lists usually repeat each other at different levels. Reduce every item to its essence, find the repeats and the levels, and summarise around the end products.

# Situation Groups: State What The Likeness Implies
Situation ideas need three steps, and most summaries stop after the first:
1. Classify the items: problems, reasons, findings. This only names what kind of list it is.
2. Find the link that makes these items belong together and sets them apart from everything else of that kind.
3. State what that link means. That statement is the summary.

Look for the link in the structure of the sentences. Each item is a subject and a predicate, so items that belong together usually share the same kind of subject, the same kind of predicate (the same action or the same object), or the same kind of implied judgement. If the subjects are the same, compare the predicates; if the predicates are the same, compare the subjects; if neither, compare the judgement each item implies.

Example: a paper reports that students who tested themselves remembered more a week later; that students who reread felt more confident but remembered less; and that students predicted rereading would work better. The shared thread is the gap between how learning feels and what it produces. Summary: "Students' sense of how well they are learning points the wrong way: the method that feels weaker works better."

Strip polished wording and named frameworks down to their bare subjects and predicates before looking for the link. Fluent phrasing hides weak connections, and a point being true doesn't make it part of the group.

# Closer Links In Long Lists
In a long list, once you know where the similarity lies, sort the items into subgroups by it. Then ask why these subgroups and no others; the answer is the top point. With the subgroups named and ordered, check whether that order implies a subgroup the source never covers, and name it as your observation if so.

Example: twenty reasons students drop an online course fall into reasons at enrolment (wrong expectations), reasons mid-course (pace, workload), and reasons when stuck (no one to ask). The top point is about where in the course students are lost, not about the twenty reasons.

# Making The Leap
When you can see the group is sound but not what it implies, sketch where the points come from: the system, process, or situation they describe, as a few boxes and arrows. Read conclusions off the sketch. Then check whether those conclusions are alike in some way; if they are, state what their likeness means. If they share nothing, they relate deductively, so carry the reasoning through to its "therefore". Stopping short of either leaves a blank statement.

# Actions In Disguise
Some lists look like situations but are really steps: "factors that affect retention", "variables to manage". Treat such a list first as a situation group. If you can picture the combined effect the items would produce when acted on, restate them as actions and summarise by that effect. Situation ideas about an activity can also take time order, following when each part of the activity happens.

# Why These And No Others
Ask of every group: why did the author put these ideas together, and no others? Either they share a trait that only they have, and the summary is what that trait means; or they are all the actions needed to reach one effect, and the summary is that effect. If you can't answer either way, the group isn't sound yet.

# How Precise
Match precision to need. The top point and the Key Line deserve full rigour. Lower groups in a long outline can carry a looser summary when the reasoning is sound and the learner doesn't need that level in detail.

Whatever you state, keep to the fidelity rules in SKILL.md: use the author's own statement of what the points add up to when there is one, and label your own when there isn't.
