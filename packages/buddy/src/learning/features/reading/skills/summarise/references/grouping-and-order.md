# Grouping And Order

Use this when the source's points won't sit in clean groups, a list runs long, or you can't say why one point comes before another. Fix the grouping and its order first; only then state what the group adds up to (see summary-statements.md).

# Deduction Or Induction
The points in a group relate in one of two ways, never a mixture.
- **Deductive**: each point builds on the one before and the group ends in a "therefore". The first point states something that is the case; the second states something else that is the case and comments on the subject or the predicate of the first; the third states what follows from both.
- **Inductive**: the points are separate ideas of the same kind, nameable by one plural noun, and the point above states what their likeness means.

To tell them apart, look at what the second point does. If it comments on the subject or the predicate of the first, the group is deductive. If it doesn't, it should be another member of the same class as the first, and the group is inductive.

Deductive: "Memory for a text fades within days unless it is retrieved. Most students only reread, which involves no retrieval. So most of what students read is soon lost." The second point comments on the first point's condition, "unless it is retrieved".

Inductive: "Rereading doesn't improve recall a week later. Highlighting doesn't. Cramming doesn't." The predicate stays the same while the subject changes; the plural noun is "study habits that don't improve lasting recall".

Two traps:
- A list whose items actually cause one another in sequence is a deductive chain dressed as a list. Its real top point is the conclusion the chain leads to.
- A point resting on a single piece of evidence can only be argued deductively; there is nothing to group.

# Summarising A Deductive Chain
Lift the last step to the top and fold the earlier steps into a "because": "Because memory fades without retrieval and most students only reread, most of what they read is soon lost." When the source's chain is long, keep only the steps the learner couldn't readily fill in for themselves.

When a source argues its way up to recommendations over many pages, a summary that puts the recommendations on the Key Line, each with its reasoning beneath it, is usually easier for the learner to take in. Keep the author's argument order instead when the conclusion is surprising enough that the learner needs the case before the claim, or when the recommendations make no sense without the reasoning. Either way, recasting the argument is a restructuring choice; label it.

# Size Limits
- A deductive group holds no more than four points, and chains no more than two "therefore" steps.
- An inductive group holds no more than five points.

Past five, some items are almost certainly more closely related to each other than to the rest. Find those subgroups and show them; a flat list of nine hides the source's thinking.

# Dividing Without Overlaps Or Gaps
When a group divides a whole (the stages of a process, the parts of a system, the kinds of evidence), the parts must not overlap and must together cover the whole.
- Set out the whole the source is dividing, then place each point on it.
- A point that lands nowhere doesn't belong in the group.
- Two points on the same spot overlap; merge them.
- An empty spot is a gap. If the gap is in the source, say so as your observation ("The author covers how students start and stall in the course, but not how they finish"). Don't fill it.

When a group is a class (three problems, four objections), define the shared trait precisely enough that you could say of any other item whether it belongs.

# Choosing The Order
Every inductive group needs an order. Finding it is also how you check that the members belong together and that none is missing. The order comes from the kind of thinking that produced the group:
- **Time order** when the group came from working out causes and effects or the steps of a process: put the steps in the sequence they happen. A group of actions always takes time order.
- **Structural order** when the group came from dividing a whole into parts: follow the parts as you would draw the whole on a page. To explain how something works, give its working parts in the order they act.
- **Degree order** when the group came from classifying things that share a trait: rank them by how strongly they have it, strongest first.

How the source divided its subject sets the order of the parts. Divided by activity or stage, use time order; by place or component, use structural order; by kind, use degree order ranked by the measure the source cares about.

Ask of each point, "Why is this first, and could it be second?" If no order shows, identify where the group came from (a process, a structure, or a class) and use the order that source gives. If you still can't find one, the grouping itself is wrong; regroup.

Order signals importance to the learner. If the author built deliberately toward the strongest point, you may keep that order to stay faithful, or rank strongest first and label the change.

# Long Lists And Hidden Subgroups
A long list of reasons, problems, or tips usually holds several different kinds.
1. Say what type of point each item is making.
2. Group the items of the same type.
3. Order the groups. The right order depends on the question the list answers: a list of why students drop a course orders differently from a list of how to keep them.

Also clear out items that don't belong at the same level:
- An item that refers to all the others rather than being one of them is a summary or a framing remark. Lift it out; it may be the top point.
- An item that is a result of another item, or merely a feature of it, sits under that item, not beside it. Keep actions and their outcomes apart.

# Pseudo-Classes And Hidden Sources
Authors often group items as if they shared a trait when really they come from the same process or the same structure: "five reasons the course fails" that are in fact the five stages of taking it. This is legitimate once you see it; use the order that process or structure imposes.

To find the hidden source, reword each item as a result or a stage. If most of them turn out to be stages of one process, switch to time order; the top point usually gets sharper. An item that fits no stage doesn't belong in that group. Leave it out of the group, and mention it separately if the source gives it weight.

The same move works on a list of conclusions that hints at a point without stating it: try to justify the order of the items, and the process behind them usually shows. If the author covers some stages of that process and not others, name the missing stage as your observation.

# An Inference Pitched Too High
Test every group statement from the bottom up: do these points, and only these, support it? If you can name other points, not in this group, that would support it just as well, the statement is too broad. Narrow it until it is about these points specifically.

Too high: "The author argues that study habits matter."
Right: "The author argues that the habits students trust most all skip retrieval, which is why they fail."
