# Science and Mathematics

A scientific text reports findings or conclusions from experimental or observational research. The scientific problem is always to describe phenomena as accurately as possible and to trace how kinds of phenomena are connected. Science looks for general truths that hold regardless of time and place, and rests on special experience beyond ordinary daily life ([../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md), Rule 1).

This file covers the classic works of science and mathematics and popular science. It often applies to specialist papers too, but specialist science is written by experts for experts and assumes knowledge a lay reader lacks; a lay reader relies on popularisations, must tell good ones from bad, and must read the good ones with understanding. Where the learner has the background, read a paper by the general rules, with the adjustments here (adaptation: the notes stop short of specialist papers).

## Reading the classics
- **Try them; none is impossibly hard.** Until roughly the end of the nineteenth century, major scientific works were written for the intelligent general reader. Even the hardest is manageable with effort.
- **Read them for the history and philosophy of science.** A lay reader does not read a scientific classic to master its subject as it stands today, but to understand how science developed and how it thinks. The reader's first obligation is not competence in the subject but understanding the problem. Following how facts, assumptions, principles, and proofs connect engages reason where it has worked best, dispels the sense that science is unintelligible, and frees the mind through wonder.
- **Rule 4 matters most here.** State as clearly as possible the problem the author set out to solve, and its background.
- **You need not read a classic through to gain from it.** A preface, or selected parts of a superseded work, can convey its method and lasting ideas.

## How scientific texts differ
- **Separate what the author assumes from what they establish.** Good scientific works contain no persuasion or propaganda, though they may carry bias in their starting assumptions. Allow for it by distinguishing what is assumed from what is shown by argument. The more objective the author, the more openly they ask the reader to grant things. Scientific objectivity is not the absence of starting bias but its frank admission.
- **Key terms are technical and easy to spot; key claims are general.** Through the technical words the reader reaches the claims, which are always general statements about how things are and behave.
- **Hard sciences declare their usage.** They state at the outset, in definitions, postulates, and axioms, which terms are essential and how they will be used, giving the field something like the rules of a game. Do not dispute these stipulations; accept them and go on. Because terms and claims are easy to find and assumptions are explicit, such texts are in an important sense easier to read than most exposition.
- **Inductive arguments need the evidence.** Science is mainly inductive: its typical argument establishes a general claim from observable evidence, a single experiment or many cases. It also uses deductive proof, where it differs little from philosophy. To understand an inductive argument, the reader must be able to follow the evidence, which the text alone may not allow ([../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md), the aids to finding arguments).
- **If the text is not enough, get the experience.** See a demonstration, handle similar equipment, look at specimens or models. There are classic experiments as well as classic texts; the texts become clearer to those who have seen and done what they describe. Experiments described clearly, with illustrations and diagrams, can sometimes be repeated at home.

## Mathematics
- **Mathematics is a language.** Much fear of it comes from not being told early that it is a language like any other, with its own vocabulary, grammar, and syntax, learned by memorising symbols and relations. It is almost entirely written, so it is learned only once. Learning it is an elementary-reading problem, no harder in principle than learning a natural language, perhaps easier.
- **It carries no emotional charge.** Ordinary language is largely about feelings and relations that two people never fully share. Mathematics concerns things outside both writer and reader, and its terms, propositions, and equations, used properly, carry no emotional connotation. This is one reason it can be understood.
- **It is beautiful and satisfying.** Starting with a clear classic of elementary geometry, and a few evenings with it, can overcome much fear; the other classical mathematicians are not very hard, and the reader may skip.
- **Two kinds of proposition in elementary geometry.** Some require that something be done (constructions); some require that something be proved (theorems). Constructions are needed to prove later theorems, and resemble postulates: both assert that an operation can be performed, but a postulate assumes it and a construction proves it from the postulates.
- **A theorem proves an if-then connection only.** It shows that if the hypothesis holds, and the definitions, postulates, and earlier propositions hold, then the conclusion holds. It asserts neither the hypothesis nor the conclusion by itself, only their connection, and only once proved. This limited, fully logical problem is its beauty.
- **Contrast reasoning about real things.** An argument about the world assumes something about the world that may be false, so its assumptions must be examined. A mathematical proposition does not depend on whether its objects exist: if they exist as defined, the conclusion follows absolutely. Philosophy rarely limits its problems or uses logic this cleanly.

**Two ways to read texts with mathematics:**
- **A mathematics text, read for itself:** read it from beginning to end, pencil in hand. Writing in the margins and on scrap paper is more necessary here than for any other kind of text.
- **A scientific text that contains mathematics:** skip strategically, especially on a first reading.
  1. Read the statement of each proposition and glance at the proof to see how it goes.
  2. Read the statements of the lemmas and corollaries.
  3. Read the discussions of how propositions relate to each other and to the whole.
  4. Go through the whole text this way, skipping diagrams that trouble you and glancing over the connecting material.
  5. Find and read the passages where the author makes the main points, especially the concluding summaries that set out problems for later work.
  This shows how the system is built, what comes first, and how the parts fit.
- **Diagrams may illustrate experiments rather than carry mathematics.** Read the descriptions of experiments, the statements of results, and the closing discussions where the author sums up findings and consequences.
- **Scientists use mathematics for precision, clarity, and limits.** The reader can usually grasp something of the matter without going deep into the mathematics. Its absence can make a scientific text harder, as when science is set out as dialogue, a form suited to the stage and to philosophy but not to science. Some classics need no mathematics at all.

## Popular science
- **Popularisations avoid the two main difficulties.** They describe few experiments (reporting results instead) and contain little mathematics (unless they are about mathematics). A reader who can manage some classics should find them manageable. Popular articles are usually easier than popular books.
- **The reader depends on reporters.** Even good science journalism filters information through writers; if they are good, the reader is lucky, and if not, the reader has almost no recourse ([current-events.md](current-events.md)).
- **Popular science still demands active reading.** It is never easy the way a story seems easy; even a short article without experiments, diagrams, or formulas takes real effort. Apply the rules with particular force: find the subject, relate whole and parts, come to terms, find the claims and arguments, and understand before judging or weighing significance.
- **Short articles are mostly informational; good popular books ask more.** With a short article, following the author's account is often enough. A good popular book asks the reader to see its implications too.
- **Theoretical science can have practical weight.** A book on a subject that affects the reader, such as public health or a new technology, has consequences beyond theory: if we are involved, action or restraint may follow. There is also a practical need to understand the major achievements of one's age, such as what mathematics is and how mathematicians think.

**Buddy as reader.** When explaining a scientific text, state the problem first, separate what the author assumes from what the evidence or proof establishes, keep the author's defined terms exactly, and say where understanding an argument depends on evidence or mathematics you have not been able to check from the text (adaptation).

## Questions to put to the learner
- What problem was the author trying to solve, and what was known before?
- What does the author assume, and what do they establish? Have they stated their starting assumptions openly?
- What are the technical terms, and what general claims do they form?
- Which arguments are inductive? Can you follow the evidence, or do you need to see it done?
- Is this a construction or a theorem? What if-then connection does it establish?
- Are you reading a mathematics text for itself (every step, pencil in hand) or a scientific text with mathematics in it (statements first, skip strategically)?
- For popular science: who filtered this? What follows from it for how we should act?
