# Analytical Reading, Stage 3: Criticism (Rules 9 to 15)

The third stage judges the text: is it true, and why does it matter? Reading is a conversation in which the author has had their say and the reader has the last word. Because the author cannot interrupt to ask for a full hearing or to correct a misreading, an undisciplined reader can turn that conversation into a quarrel. The rules keep it civil and make it profitable: the only gain from conversation with a teacher, living or dead, is what you learn.

These rules are for exposition. Imaginative literature is judged by other standards ([../kinds/imaginative-literature.md](../kinds/imaginative-literature.md)).

## Why criticism is part of reading
- **Reading is completed by judgment.** Understanding what a text says is not the end. An expository author tried to teach and to convince; their effort deserves a considered response. If you cannot say "I agree", have grounds for disagreeing or suspending judgment. The passive reader fails here even more than at interpretation, dismissing a text by forgetting it, which condemns it without consideration.
- **No text is beyond criticism once understood.** No text is so good that no fault can be found in it. A reader who has understood a text has risen nearly to the author's level and owes the author a peer's response; failing to give one is unfair to the author.
- **Teachability is active, not submissive.** A teachable reader is not passive or pliable. They hear the teacher out fully, understand before judging, and then use independent judgment. The most teachable reader is the most critical one, who finally makes the greatest effort to make up their own mind. Effort is not enough; the reader must also know how to judge.
- **Rhetoric governs this stage.** Every serious communication wants to be understood and also agreed with: to convince on questions of fact and theory, to persuade on questions of action or feeling. For the reader, the skill is knowing how to respond to someone trying to convince or persuade you; the grammatical and logical work of the first two stages prepares for it.
- **Be responsive and responsible.** Responsive: follow what was said and the intention behind it. Responsible: take a position that is yours, not the author's. Holding someone else responsible for your judgment is surrendering it.

## The general maxims (Rules 9 to 11)

### Rule 9: Understand before you criticise
Finish the outline and the interpretation first. Be able to say, with reasonable certainty, "I understand", before saying "I agree", "I disagree", or "I suspend judgment". These three are the only critical positions. Once you have honestly finished the first two stages, you have earned the right to criticise, and the duty.

- **Criticism always comes last.** The first two stages overlap, and a skilled reader combines them, but expert and beginner alike must wait until they understand before they judge.
- **Criticising is not only disagreeing.** Agreeing is as much a judgment as disagreeing, and can be as wrong. Agreeing without understanding is empty; disagreeing without understanding is presumptuous.
- **Suspending judgment is a position too.** It says that something has not been shown, that you are convinced neither way. Doubt is no more intelligent than agreement or denial in a reader who does not know what they are doubting.
- **Test understanding by restatement.** Criticism not based on understanding is irrelevant. A critic who cannot restate the position they attack, in their own words, does not understand it. Apply the same test to yourself before criticising an author, and to the learner before accepting their criticism.
- **Hesitate before claiming to understand a good text.** The presumption is that work remains. You must judge yourself here, which makes the responsibility heavier.
- **"I don't understand" reflects on the text only after your best effort.** If you have done all that can be expected and still do not understand, the text may be unintelligible. But the presumption favours a good text; failure to understand it is usually the reader's. So stay with the first two stages a long time, and say "I don't understand" in a way that allows the fault may be yours. To turn it into a criticism of the text, locate the trouble: show that the structure is disorderly, that the parts do not hang together, that some of it is irrelevant, or that the author equivocates with key words and falls into the confusions that follow. As far as you can support the charge, you have no further critical obligation.
- **Take extra care with partial reading and dependent texts.** If you have read only part of a text, be slower to claim understanding and slower to criticise. If a text depends on other works by the same author for its full meaning, be more careful still until you have read those works.

### Rule 10: Disagree reasonably, not to win
There is no point in winning an argument you know or suspect you are wrong about. Put truth above winning.
- Treating the conversation as a battle means reading only to find something to disagree with, and a quarrel can always be picked. Alone with a text, a reader can seem to win after a glance at the first pages. Be as ready to agree as to disagree, moved only by the evidence and the argument.
- Admit a point when you see it. Feeling beaten by having to agree marks a disputatious reader whose problem is emotional, not intellectual.
- **Treat disagreements as resolvable.** Disagreement is pointless agitation unless undertaken in the hope of settling an issue. People can agree insofar as they are rational; passion, prejudice, and a language clouded by emotion and interest cause most disagreement, and to the extent people are rational these can be overcome.
- **Know the kinds of disagreement.** Apparent disagreement comes from misunderstanding and is cured by coming to terms. Disagreement from unequal knowledge, where the less informed wrongly dispute with the better informed, is cured by instruction; the better informed may rightly correct errors due to lack of knowledge. Some disagreements may lie deeper, but they are hard to identify, and most can be resolved by removing misunderstanding or ignorance, though often with difficulty.
- **Be as ready to be changed as to change the author.** Keep in mind that you may misunderstand or lack knowledge on some point. Disagreement is a chance to be taught as well as to teach.
- **Reject "it's all a matter of opinion".** If every view were private and beyond argument, conversation could never increase knowledge. Where knowledge is at stake, disagreements are either apparent (removed by coming to terms) or real, and real ones can be settled in the long run by fact and reason. Argue on the assumption that understanding reached by reason, in light of all the relevant evidence, can settle the issue.

When you find yourself disagreeing:
1. Check that you understand; make sure the disagreement is not a misunderstanding.
2. Decide whether the matter is one of knowledge or of mere opinion.
3. If it is knowledge, treat the issue as resolvable and stay open to being taught by the author. If you are not, your criticism may be justified, and you may, in effect, instruct the author.
4. Give your reasons.

### Rule 11: Give reasons for every judgment
Respect the difference between knowledge and mere personal opinion by giving reasons for any critical judgment you make.
- **Knowledge is defensible opinion.** Little human knowledge is absolute; self-evident truths are the exception. Most of what we know, we hold subject to correction, because all or most of the evidence supports it and new evidence could overturn it. Knowledge in this sense is opinion that can be defended with evidence other reasonable people are likely to accept. Mere opinion rests only on feeling or prejudice.
- **Apply the distinction to the author and to yourself.** An author's claims that come without reasons are only the author's opinions. A reader who does not separate reasoned claims from flat opinions is not reading to learn; they are judging the person, not the text.
- **When you agree, share the author's reasons actively. When you disagree, give your own grounds.** Otherwise you treat a matter of knowledge as opinion. Giving reasons defines the issue rather than just stating it, and that is where all hope of settling it lies.

### Conditions for fair controversy
These are ideals, hard to meet in full:
1. **Acknowledge your emotions.** Admit the feelings you bring to the dispute or that arise in it; otherwise you vent feelings and mistake them for reasons.
2. **Make your own assumptions explicit.** Know your prejudgments, or you will not admit that the other side may be entitled to different ones. Good controversy is not a quarrel about assumptions: if the author asks you to grant something, grant it, even though the opposite could also be assumed.
3. **Try to be impartial.** Try to take the author's point of view. If you could not read the text sympathetically, your disagreement is probably more quarrelsome than reasoned.

Because these are hard, even careful readers attack straw men, denounce what they cannot refute, and air prejudices. Keeping adverse criticism to the four specific points below makes that less likely.

## The specific points of adverse criticism (Rules 12 to 15)
Understanding is one kind of agreement: agreement about how the words express the ideas. It does not make disagreement impossible, and not every disagreement is a misunderstanding. The author makes judgments about the world that can be right or wrong, and holds good only as far as they are true or probable on the evidence. Once misunderstanding is cleared away, a remaining disagreement is a real issue; agreement about words is the condition for real agreement or disagreement about things. Authors argue and expect readers to argue back, so a good reader must meet the arguments, not just follow them.

After "I understand, but I disagree", there are four things a reader can say. They are probably exhaustive, certainly the main ones, and not mutually exclusive.

Every point must be specific and supported. No text is uninformed, misinformed, or illogical about everything: say in what respect, and give reasons.

### Rule 12: Show where the author is uninformed
The author lacks knowledge relevant to the problem they are trying to solve. Make this point only if the knowledge would have been relevant. Support it by stating the missing knowledge and showing how it would change the author's conclusions. In science and history, missing information is usually revealed by later research; in philosophy, knowledge can be lost as well as gained, so an earlier author may know what a later one does not.

### Rule 13: Show where the author is misinformed
The author asserts what is not the case: presents as true, or more probable, what is false, or less probable, claiming knowledge they lack. Point it out only if it bears on the author's conclusions; an error of limited relevance may not affect the main account. Support it by arguing for the truth, or greater probability, of a contrary claim.

These two points are related. Lack of information can cause false claims, and anyone misinformed on a point is also uninformed on it. The difference still matters: missing knowledge leaves some problems unsolvable or some conclusions unsupported; false beliefs lead to wrong conclusions and untenable solutions. Together they charge the author's premises with being insufficient, in quantity or quality.

### Rule 14: Show where the author is illogical
The author has made an error in reasoning. The two main kinds are the non sequitur (the conclusion does not follow from the reasons given) and inconsistency (two things the author says cannot both be true). An author may also fail to draw conclusions that their own principles imply. Show exactly where the argument fails to follow. It matters only as far as the major conclusions are affected; a text may be weak in irrelevant places. Good texts rarely make obvious slips; their errors are usually hidden and take a penetrating reader to find.

The most important case is bad reasoning from sound premises. Weak reasoning from false premises or thin evidence is interesting but matters less. Keep error from bad reasoning distinct from error from insufficient knowledge.

### If you cannot make points 12 to 14, you must agree
Having said you understand, if you cannot show the author uninformed, misinformed, or illogical on a relevant matter, you must agree as far as the author has gone. You do not get to choose. Conceding that the premises and the reasoning are sound while rejecting the conclusion only means you dislike the conclusion: that is emotion or prejudice. If you have been convinced, say so. If you honestly remain unconvinced, perhaps you should not have claimed to understand.

The first three points judge the soundness of the materials (terms, claims, arguments) the author used. The fourth judges how completely the author carried out the plan.

### Rule 15: Show where the author's analysis is incomplete
The author has not solved all the problems they began with, has not made the best use of their materials, did not see all the implications, or failed to make distinctions their undertaking needed. Saying a text is incomplete is empty, since every finite work is, unless you define the gap precisely, from your own knowledge or with the help of other texts.

- **Incompleteness grounds suspended judgment, not disagreement.** It marks the limits of the author's achievement. A reader who agrees in part (finding no grounds under 12 to 14) may still suspend judgment on the whole because the account is incomplete.
- **Rule 15 closes the chain.** The structure stage ends with the author's questions (Rule 4); interpretation ends with which were answered (Rule 8); criticism ends with how completely, which judges both how well the questions were posed and how well they were answered.
- **Compare related texts by the four points.** In one field, a text is better in proportion as it says more that is true and makes fewer errors. One author may know less, believe more that is false, or reason less well than another. The deepest comparison is by completeness, measured by the number of valid and significant distinctions a text makes. Since the number of distinct terms tracks the number of distinctions, a good grasp of each author's terms helps here.

If you understand and agree completely, the analytical reading is done: you have been taught and convinced.

## Is it true? Why does it matter?
Rules 9 to 15 answer the third and fourth basic questions.

- **Truth applies to every kind of writing, in its own sense:** mathematical, scientific, philosophical, historical, poetic. Praising a work for the truth it reaches is the highest praise; criticising it for failing in truth takes a serious work seriously. Do not substitute other standards (novelty, sensation, force, charm) for truth, clarity, and power to enlighten.
- **If it is true in some sense, ask why it matters.** If the text only informed you, ask why the author thinks this is worth knowing, and whether it matters to the learner. If it enlightened you, ask what else follows and what it implies or suggests. Facts rarely come without interpretation, especially in digests, which select facts by some view of what matters. When reading for understanding, this question has no end; it comes back at every stage.
- **The four questions follow from the nature of communication.** If communication were simple, outlining would be unneeded; if language were perfect, interpretation would be unneeded; if error and ignorance did not limit knowledge, criticism would be unneeded.

For how the questions of truth and significance change by kind (practical texts imply action, stories are judged by likelihood and beauty, histories by plausibility and use of sources), see the files in [../kinds/](../kinds/).

## Buddy as reader
When the learner asks you to judge a text:
1. Show understanding first: state briefly, in your own words, what the author claims and on what grounds, with locations (Rule 9).
2. Take one of the three positions (agree, disagree, suspend judgment), on the specific claim, not the whole text, unless you have read the whole text (adaptation).
3. Support each adverse point under one of the four headings, with the specific respect and your reasons. Where your point depends on knowledge from outside the text, label it as outside knowledge ([../outside-aids.md](../outside-aids.md)).
4. Where you cannot fault the premises or the reasoning, say the argument holds as far as it goes.
5. Keep your judgment separate from your account of the author's position, and labelled as yours.
If you read only part of the text, say so and treat your judgment as provisional.

## Buddy as coach
- When the learner criticises a text: "Before we judge it, state the author's position in your own words." Check the restatement against the text before discussing the criticism.
- When the learner disagrees: take them through the four steps above. "Is this a misunderstanding? Is it a matter of knowledge or of taste? What are your reasons?"
- Ask the learner to name which point they are making: "Is the author missing something, wrong about something, reasoning badly, or stopping short?" Then: "Where exactly, and how would it change the conclusion?"
- When the learner says "I can't fault it, but I still disagree": ask what in the premises or reasoning they reject; if nothing, point out that they are obliged to agree as far as the author goes, or to reconsider whether they understood.
- When the learner says "I don't understand it, so it's badly written": ask them to show where the structure breaks down or which key word shifts meaning.
- Ask them to name their own assumptions and feelings about the subject, and whether they read the text sympathetically.
- Close with the fourth question: "Suppose it's true. What follows, and does it matter to you?"
