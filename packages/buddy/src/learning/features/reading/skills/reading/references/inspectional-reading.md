# Inspectional Reading

Inspectional reading gets the most out of a text in a limited time. It answers, from the surface of the text: What kind of text is this? What is it about as a whole? How is it built, and what are its parts? Does it deserve more time? It is systematic, not casual browsing, and it takes full alertness: read with a question in mind, looking for evidence of the main theme and following one thread.

It has two steps, systematic skimming and superficial reading. They are one skill. A beginner should do them separately; an experienced reader does both at once.

Inspection needs elementary reading first: the reader must be able to read the text steadily, without stopping at many words or stumbling over grammar, and make some sense of most sentences. If the learner cannot, handle that first (SKILL.md, Elementary reading).

## When to use it
- **Buddy as reader.** Use it to answer orientation and triage questions ("What's this about?", "Should I read it?", "Which chapter covers X?"), to classify a text before analysing it, and, in `scoped-reading` mode, to choose the scoped target. In `whole-full-text` mode you have the whole text, so do not skim the pack instead of reading it; ask the skimming questions of the text you ingested (adaptation).
- **Buddy as coach.** Teach it when the learner is about to start a book or long document, is choosing between texts, or starts at page one and ploughs through. Reading straight from the first page without looking at the contents makes the learner learn the book's surface and its substance at the same time, which compounds the difficulty.
- Skim when it is not yet known whether a text is worth a careful reading and time is short. Skim even a text you already plan to read carefully, to see its form and structure first. What skimming yields may be all a text is worth, now or ever; even then, you know its main contention and its kind.

## Systematic skimming
Skimming takes minutes, at most an hour. For a prepared pack, each step maps onto a pack file (adaptation for the mapping; the steps themselves are the method).

1. **Title, subtitle, and preface.** Read `00-resource.md` for title, author, and source, then the preface or introduction (usually the first chunk after front matter). Read each quickly. Note the subtitle and any sign of scope, aim, or the author's angle. By the end of this step, know the subject, and pause to place the text in a category: what kind of text is it, and what else is like it?
2. **Table of contents.** Study `10-toc.md` as a road map before anything else. A detailed contents page is especially valuable, and even a thin one is worth studying. If the pack has no TOC or the structure is weak, list the headings in the full text or chunks instead (adaptation).
3. **Index.** If the text has an index, check it: estimate the range of topics and the kinds of works and authors cited, and for terms that look crucial (from your sense of the text, or from how many references they have), read at least some of the passages cited. They may hold the point on which the whole text turns. With no index, search the pack for the candidate key terms and see how often and where they occur (adaptation).
4. **Blurb or abstract.** Read any publisher's description, back-cover text, or abstract. It often states the main points, frequently in the author's own framing. Pure puffery is obvious at a glance, and may itself suggest the text has little to say. For a paper, the abstract plays this role; for a web page, the standfirst or lead paragraph (adaptation).
   After these four steps you may already know that the text deserves a careful reading, or none. Either way you may stop. Otherwise go on.
5. **Pivotal chapters.** From what you now know, look at the chapters or sections that seem central to the argument. If they open or close with summary statements, read those carefully.
6. **Dip in, and read the ending.** Read a paragraph or two here and there, sometimes several pages in sequence, never more, looking for the main contention. Above all, read the last two or three pages of the main body (before any epilogue, appendix, or back matter), where authors usually state what they think is new and important, even if their judgment of that is wrong.

After skimming, you should be able to place the text accurately and know whether it has more to give.

For a web page, article, or paper without a pack, the same steps apply in miniature: title and subtitle, headings as the contents, abstract or lead, section openings and closings, and the conclusion (adaptation).

## Superficial reading
When tackling a difficult text for the first time, read it straight through without stopping to look up or puzzle over what you do not understand right away. Attend to what you can understand and pass over paragraphs, footnotes, comments, and references that stop you; you will soon reach parts you do understand. A reader who stalls at a hard passage is lost, and sticking at it rarely solves it.

This is the most important rule of reading. Do not try to understand every word or page of a difficult text the first time through, and do not fear being or seeming superficial.
- The mistake is not trying a hard book but expecting too much of the first pass. No text written for general readers, however hard, is cause for despair.
- A first reading prepares the second. What was understood on the way through, even half or less, makes the skipped passages far easier on return, but only if the text was read through once. Understanding half a hard book beats understanding none of it.
- Help used before a first reading-through gets in the way. Looking up everything as you go makes the reader forget the beginning by the end and lose the whole. Dictionaries, encyclopedias, footnotes, and commentaries come later (see [outside-aids.md](outside-aids.md)).
- The rule holds for literature and exposition alike. Read a play or story through, and talk about what the first reading gave, before studying it closely. With an expository text, insisting on every fine point before going on loses the big points.

**Buddy as reader.** When you read a long text in several scoped parts, keep going through the target rather than stopping to reason out every obscure passage mid-read, then return to the passages that matter to the learner's question. Do not reach for your background knowledge of the text before you have read it (adaptation).

**Buddy as coach.** When a learner says they are stuck early in a hard text, tell them to keep reading to the end of the chapter or section and mark what stopped them, then come back. On the second pass, narrow to the passage that stopped them and work on it together (adaptation: the narrowing step). If they are reading a play, poem, or story, have them finish it before discussing detail.

## Reading speed
The aim is not simply faster reading but reading at the right speed for each thing, and knowing which speed fits.
- Read every text no more slowly than it deserves, and no more quickly than you can read it with satisfaction and comprehension.
- Vary speed within one text, even in analytical reading. Every text, however hard, has connecting material to read quickly, and every good text has passages to read very slowly. Full comprehension of a short passage may take days; rhetoric and supporting lists can go fast.
- Slow down over the sentences that puzzle, not the ones that merely interest.
- Inspectional reading is fast for three reasons: it reads faster, it reads less of the text, and it reads with different goals.
- Speed is not the main obstacle to understanding a difficult text. Great speed is valuable only for material not worth much. Readers fail at hard texts because they do not know the rules, what to look for, or when they have found it.
- Concentration is not comprehension. A reader can attend closely and still be able to answer only simple factual questions; higher comprehension needs analytical reading.

**Buddy as reader.** For you, speed is where to spend close attention. Read the passages that carry the learner's question closely and move through the rest (adaptation).

**Buddy as coach.** If a learner's real problem is slow word-by-word reading, the usual cause is stopping on each word or short phrase, going back over what was just read, and silently sounding out each word; the mind can take in a sentence at a glance once these habits ease. Practice pacing the eyes with a pointer moving slightly faster than comfortable reduces both. Treat this as an elementary-level fix that does not by itself improve understanding.

## What inspection yields, and its limits
Inspection answers the structural questions: what kind of text it is, what it is about as a whole, and the order in which the author develops the subject. It prepares analytical reading: skimming prepares the structure stage, and a first read-through is the first step of interpretation. It also plays a central part in syntopical reading, where every candidate text is inspected before any is read closely.

From inspection alone you can report kind, subject, and structure with some confidence, and you may form ideas about the author's views. Do not judge whether the text's claims are true until you have read it carefully; label anything more than kind, subject, and structure as provisional.

## Deciding whether a text deserves more time
- **Most texts need only skimming.** The great majority of books can be read only for amusement or information; nothing important will be learned from them, and inspection is enough.
- **Good texts deserve one careful reading.** A small fraction are carefully made and give real insight on subjects of lasting interest. They deserve one analytical reading, after which a skilled reader has what they can give. Return to check points or refresh memory; the reader's notes are most valuable for these. A reader knows such a text is used up when it has stretched them and they sense it will not change them further; on rereading, it seems to contain less than remembered, because the reader has grown and the text has not.
- **A very few texts are inexhaustible.** After the best reading, the reader suspects there is more, cannot forget it, and returns; on return the text seems to have grown too, and earlier understanding stays true but is true in more ways. Each reader's own set of these is small and personal, since minds and tastes differ.
- **To get better at reading, read texts beyond you.** Texts well within the reader's powers do not improve their reading. Texts that only amuse, or only add facts, make the mind fuller, not better. Texts that seem beyond the reader are within reach if the rules are applied, though not instantly.
- **The hardest texts are often imaginative, not scientific.** Good scientific and philosophical texts help the reader find terms, claims, and arguments; poems and novels offer no such help and deal with things harder to write about, so they may be the most demanding reading of all.
- **Bad texts are hard in an unrewarding way.** A bad text slips away when pinned down, because there is nothing to pin, and it repays no effort.
- **Good texts reward twice.** The reader's skill grows from tackling them, and, more important, they teach about the world and the reader. Some questions, in science and philosophy and in human relationships, have no final answer; the best texts help because their authors thought about them better than most.

**Buddy as coach, choosing what to read.** When a learner asks what to read or is choosing between texts:
- Start from what interests them most. The aim is to read well, not widely; a handful of demanding books in a year is not a failure, and a reading list is an invitation to start where they feel at home.
- Recommend some widely acclaimed works in their area before they build their own list.
- Judge recent works more cautiously than older ones, since distance makes detachment easier.
- Suggest they learn to read well in their own tradition before tackling works from a very different one, which often baffle partly for lack of that practice.
- Read related works in relation and in a sensible order (see [outside-aids.md](outside-aids.md)).
- To find which works matter most to them, ask which ten they would keep if they could keep only ten.
- Not every text needs the full method. Match the level to the text's merit and the learner's purpose.

## Questions to put to the learner
- Do you need to read all of this, or does skimming answer your question?
- What kind of text is it, and what is it about? What does the title, read carefully, promise?
- What are its main parts, from the contents?
- Which terms look crucial, and where does the text seem to turn?
- What is the author's main claim, from the opening and the last pages?
- Is this text over your head in a way that will stretch you, or will it only inform or amuse you?
- What speed does this text, or this passage, deserve?
