# Philosophy, Theology, and Authoritative Texts

Philosophy looks for general truths, like science, but asks different questions and appeals only to common experience, the kind everyone has ([../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md), Rule 1). The analytical rules assume a reader alone with a text and nothing but their own mind, which is exactly a philosopher's situation, so the rules apply more directly to philosophy than to any other kind of text.

## The questions philosophers ask
- **Philosophy asks the questions children ask.** The great philosophical works ask profound questions that sound simple: why, not just whether. Adult curiosity often shrinks to asking for information. A mind not stirred by good questions cannot appreciate the best answers. To follow the philosophers, look, wonder, and ask as a child does, and answer with adult care.
- **Theoretical questions** concern what is or happens. About being: what distinguishes existing from not existing, the ways things exist, what exists in the mind or outside it, whether everything is physical, whether anything is unchanging or necessary, whether the possible is wider than the actual. About change: what persists through change, the kinds of change, its causes, and whether they are also causes of being. And about necessity and chance, the material and immaterial, freedom and determinism, the powers of the mind, the nature and limits of knowledge, and freedom of the will. They are easy to state and understand and enormously hard to answer.
- **Normative questions** concern what ought to be done or sought: good and evil, and whether some things are always good or bad; right and wrong, and how they differ from good and evil; the order of goods, which depend on which and which conflict; duties and obligations; virtues and vices; happiness and the purpose of life; justice and rights; the state and the individual; the good society, a just government and economy; war and peace.
- **The branches.** Theoretical philosophy: metaphysics (being and existence), philosophy of nature (change and its causes), and the theory of knowledge (its causes, extent, limits, and certainties). Normative philosophy: ethics (the good life and individual conduct) and political philosophy (the good society and the individual in it). Normative philosophy concerns the ends everyone ought to seek and prescribes means only in the most general terms, unlike how-to texts, which assume the reader already wants the end ([practical-books.md](practical-books.md)). Classify a philosophical text by the questions it asks.
- **First-order and second-order questions.** First-order questions ask what is and happens, or what people ought to do or seek. Second-order questions ask about our first-order knowledge, the content of our thinking about it, and the language we express it in. Much recent academic philosophy asks only second-order questions, for specialists, and is as hard for a lay reader as specialist science; the method gives no special guidance for it. The first-order works, including the classics, were written for general readers.

## Method and style
- **Philosophy's method is thinking about common experience.** Scientific and historical questions need research; philosophical ones cannot be answered by experiment, special observation, or documents. There is nothing to do but think. Good philosophy is not speculation cut off from experience: its answers are tested against the experience everyone has. Great philosophers differ from others not by having more experience but by thinking about it more deeply and asking and answering more carefully.
- **Separate truly philosophical questions from scientific ones.** Philosophers have sometimes treated as philosophical questions that later science should answer, often because they could not have known. Their scientific errors are mostly irrelevant, apart from the confusions they cause, because they are read for their philosophical questions. The reverse error also happens: a normative question such as how one ought to live cannot be answered by surveys or research.
- **Philosophy explains; science describes.** Theoretical philosophy tries to explain the nature of things, reaching the underlying causes and conditions of what happens, not just the connections among events. Its problems are handled well only when answers rest on clear argument and analysis.
- **There is one method but several styles.** Know each style's strengths and weaknesses:
  - **Dialogue.** Leads the reader to discover things for themselves; very hard to write well.
  - **Treatise or essay.** Develops a view by straightforward exposition with a beginning, middle, and end, often reporting and answering others' views; the most common form.
  - **Question and objections.** Poses a question, gives the wrong answer and its supporting arguments, sets an authority against them, states the author's own answer, and replies to each objection; it embodies the idea that truth emerges from open conflict of views.
  - **Systematic, in mathematical form.** Definitions, propositions, proofs, and commentary; it suits philosophy poorly.
  - **Aphorism.** Short, suggestive statements that leave the reader to supply the connections and arguments; it makes the reader discover, but it is barely exposition, since the author touches a subject and moves on without defending it.
- **Style decides what may be skipped.** A lot can be skipped in the systematic style, as in mathematical physics. Nothing can be skipped in a continuous treatise, because the reasoning is continuous, or in a dialogue, any more than in a play or poem.
- **Form can defeat communication.** Mathematical form for philosophy, and dialogue for science, both seem to have hindered understanding.

## How to read a philosophical text
1. **Find the question or questions it tries to answer,** stated or implied. This is the most important thing to discover.
2. **Find the author's controlling principles.** How an author answers is shaped by their fundamental principles. Great philosophers state their definitions and postulates more clearly than others, but their controlling principles may be stated in this text, reserved for another, or never stated and instead running through all their work, or assumed here because stated elsewhere. Finding them may take many readings over years; it is the ideal goal of a thorough reading and finally required for understanding the author. Discovering hidden assumptions, the author's and your own, matters most in philosophy.
3. **Do not shortcut by reading about the philosopher.** What you discover yourself is worth far more than someone else's account ([../outside-aids.md](../outside-aids.md)).
4. **Put most effort into the terms and the opening propositions.** Philosophers' technical terms are usually common words used in a very special sense. Resist reading familiar words in familiar ways, or the text becomes nonsense ([../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md)).
5. **Do not be put off by abstraction.** Philosophy's basic terms are abstract, as science's are; general knowledge cannot be expressed otherwise. The senses grasp the concrete and particular; the mind grasps the abstract and general. To understand an abstract word is to have the idea it expresses, a general aspect of things you experience concretely. That aspect cannot be seen, touched, or imagined, and trying to picture it only confuses.
6. **Attend most closely to the principles.** As inductive arguments are the focus in science, principles are the focus in philosophy. They are either assumptions the author asks the reader to share or propositions the author calls self-evident.
7. **Grant the assumptions to see what follows.** Take the author's assumptions, even against your own, to see where they lead; pretending to believe something is good exercise. The clearer you are about your own prejudgments, the less you misjudge others'.
8. **Test self-evident principles against common experience.** They are drawn directly from experience, not proved, and the experience is the common one. Understanding and testing them needs no special experience, only your own common sense and observation. After understanding a philosophical text, ask: Is this true? Have I felt this? Have I always thought it without realising? Is it obvious now though it was not before? Is the author's view actually simpler than the muddled ideas I had before? If you understand and cannot disagree, accept it as your own common sense of the matter.
9. **Check whether the author keeps to their principles.** Even the best philosophers are often inconsistent, and inconsistency is serious. When it occurs, decide which the author really means: the principles as stated, or conclusions that do not follow from them. Or decide that neither holds ([../analytical-reading/3-criticism.md](../analytical-reading/3-criticism.md), Rule 14).
10. **The author's personality does not matter.** Good theoretical philosophy, like good science, contains no persuasion, and the reader need not investigate the author's life or circumstances (unlike practical texts).
11. **Listen to the conversation before judging.** Read other major philosophers on the same problems; they have argued with each other for a long time. Reading related texts in relation, and in order, matters more for philosophy than for any other kind. Listen before making up your mind about any of them.
12. **Do not be troubled that philosophers disagree.** Lasting disagreement may mark a great unsolved or unsolvable problem, and it is good to know where the real mysteries are. Others' disagreements matter less than the reader's own responsibility to decide what is true and false in light of the conversation.
13. **Answer the questions for yourself.** Adopting others' opinions evades philosophical questions rather than answering them. Answers must rest on argument. Unlike in science, the reader cannot rely on expert testimony.

**Buddy as reader.** When explaining a philosophical text, lead with the question it answers and the principles it starts from, keep its special terms in its sense, and do not supply your own answer to the philosophical question unless asked; if asked, give it separately with its grounds (adaptation).

## Theology
- **Natural theology is philosophy.** It reaches its conclusions (for example, about whether a god exists) by the unaided working of reason, as the last part of metaphysics. Read it as philosophy.
- **Dogmatic theology starts from articles of faith,** held by members of a religion on the authority of their church. A non-believer can read it well: treat the articles of faith with the respect given to a mathematician's assumptions, while remembering that for the faithful they are not assumptions but the most certain knowledge. Accept them as true while reading, then read with the care any good exposition deserves.
- **Two mistakes.** Refusing to grant the articles of faith even for the reading, and so fighting the first principles and never attending to the text. Or assuming that because the principles are held on faith, the reasoning and conclusions are too. If the principles are granted and the reasoning is sound, the conclusions follow as far as the principles go; but bad reasoning gives invalid conclusions even from acceptable principles. So judge the reasoning.

## Authoritative ("canonical") texts
- **What they are.** A text that an institution with a teaching role, a body of doctrine, and faithful, obedient members treats as authoritative: scripture read as revealed truth, but also a movement's founding texts, a school's master texts, a professional code, or a text that must be read the teacher's way to pass the course. Members cannot question the approved reading or find error or nonsense in the text.
- **There is one right reading.** Any other reading carries a penalty, from a lost grade to much worse. The faithful reader must make sense of the text and find it true in some sense; if they cannot alone, they must go to someone who can (a religious teacher, a superior, a professor) and accept the resolution. They read without freedom but gain a satisfaction perhaps found in no other reading. This is the one case where turning to someone else for help with a hard text is expected rather than a last resort ([../outside-aids.md](../outside-aids.md)).
- **Scripture read as revealed truth is, for a believer, the hardest and most important reading of all,** and more has been written on how to read it than on everything else about reading. The method gives no rules for it.

**Buddy as reader and coach.** Find out whether the learner reads such a text as a member of the tradition or from outside, and respect that stance. For a member, do not contest the tradition's first principles; explain the text within its approved reading where you know it, label that reading and its source, and point them to their tradition's own teachers for authoritative resolution. For an outside reader, read it as dogmatic theology is read above: grant the principles while reading, then judge the reasoning. When the "canon" is a course text that must be read the instructor's way, help the learner understand that reading and, separately, what the text itself supports (adaptation throughout this paragraph).

## Questions to put to the learner
- What question is this text trying to answer?
- Is it theoretical or normative? Which branch? Is it asking about the world or about our knowledge and language?
- Is this a philosophical question, or one for science or research?
- Which style is this, and what can you skip?
- What are the author's starting principles? Which are assumptions to grant, and which are claimed to be self-evident? Does your own experience bear the self-evident ones out?
- Which ordinary words is the author using in a special sense?
- Does the author stay consistent with their principles? If not, which do they really mean?
- What have others said on this question?
- What do you think, and on what grounds?
