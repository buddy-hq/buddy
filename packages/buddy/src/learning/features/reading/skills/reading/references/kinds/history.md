# History and Biography

History here means the written record, not the events themselves: a more or less formal narrative of a period, event, or series of events in the past. Its essence is narration, a story; even a collection of documents, as a collection, implicitly tells one. History concerns particular things at particular times and places and how they changed, often with comment on what they meant. A history's title or front matter usually shows what it is.

Read a history as exposition, with the four questions and the general rules ([../analytical-reading/overview.md](../analytical-reading/overview.md)), adjusted as below. For news and writing about the present, see [current-events.md](current-events.md).

## What makes history different
- **Historical facts are elusive.** Reconstructing even a recent event from living witnesses under strict rules of evidence leaves room for doubt. A historian's witnesses are mostly dead, cannot be cross-examined, and were free to guess and suppose. Some facts, such as dates, are fairly certain but teach little; their limits, causes, and meaning are disputed, and a fact's meaning depends on whether its consequences matter to us.
- **History sits nearer fiction than science.** A good historian does not invent the past and is bound by accuracy, but must always supply something: either find or impose a general pattern on events (a theory of history), or, while claiming only to report, assign causes to events and motives to actions. The only alternative is to hold that there is no discoverable pattern at all.
- **Recognise how your historian works:** fitting events to a theory, assigning causes and motives while claiming only to report, or denying any pattern.
- **A history is a story and must meet the demands of good storytelling,** as any narrative must, fact or fiction. Its unity is a single thread of plot; state it as such, extending the author's own statement after reading if needed ([../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md)).
- **History needs both kinds of experience.** As story it calls on the common experience that novels and plays draw on; as science it rests on the historian's special experience of documents, remains, and interviews ([../outside-aids.md](../outside-aids.md)).
- **History is read for truth.** Unlike a novel, which need only be true to its own world, a history is expected to be true in some sense, and readers may rightly complain when it is not.

## Two rules for reading history
1. **Read more than one account.** Historians' theories differ and shape their accounts, and every narrative is written from some point of view. To get at the truth of an event or period that matters, especially one with practical significance for the reader, look at it from more than one side. Reading related texts in relation matters more for history than for science or fiction ([../syntopical-reading.md](../syntopical-reading.md)).
2. **Read to learn how people act, in all times and places, especially now.** A lay reader stands between the professional historian, who must weigh every source, and someone reading for fun. With only one account, the reader has little chance of learning what really happened, but that is not the only reason to read history. Any good history also shows something universal about how people behave. A history can matter because it shaped later history, as later leaders read it and took it as justification or pattern. History tells what led up to now, so it says something about the future too.

## The four questions, adapted
1. **What is it about? Find the subject and its limits.** Every history has a particular, limited subject. Note exactly what it covers and what it does not, including limits the author sets. Knowing what it is not about is necessary for judging it: an author cannot be blamed for not doing what they did not attempt.
2. **What does it say, and how? Find how the story is told.** Time fixes the broad outline, but a story can be told in more than one way. Note how the historian divides the work: by years, decades, or generations, or by themes, each in its own chapters. Find which aspect the historian treats as most fundamental; you may disagree and still learn from them.
3. **Is it true? Two kinds of criticism, only after understanding.**
   - **Plausibility.** Does the account ring true to how people behave? A historian can misread or misjudge even well-documented, relevant sources through a poor grasp of human nature and affairs.
   - **Use of sources.** Especially with special knowledge, ask whether the historian misused sources, missed a relevant work, or got facts wrong. A historian is expected to be informed.
   Plausibility is the more important of the two. A good historian combines storyteller and scientist, knowing what probably happened as well as what witnesses said happened.
4. **Why does it matter? History leads to action.** Perhaps no writing affects action more. It shows what is possible, because it describes what has been done, which may be done again or avoided. The main answer to the fourth question lies in practical and political action, which is why history must be read well: people act on some knowledge of history, often not enough.

**Buddy as reader.** When asked what happened, report what this account says, as this author's account, with its scope and point of view; do not merge it with other accounts or your background knowledge without labelling them (adaptation, applying the first rule to Buddy's answers).

## Biography and autobiography
A biography is a story about a real person or group: usually a narrative of a life, with many of the problems of history. Ask the questions for any history, and also: what is the author's purpose, and what are their standards of truth? Biographies are special histories, often more like stories than like arguments. Do not argue with one until you fully understand what it says.

Know which kind you have, because each is read differently:
- **Definitive.** Meant to be the final, exhaustive, scholarly account of an important life; it cannot be written about a living person and usually follows several inadequate earlier lives. The author works through all the sources and much of the period's history. Read it as history: the history of a person and their times, seen through their eyes.
- **Authorised.** Commissioned by the subject's heirs or friends, it shows errors and achievements in the best light. It can be very good, since its author has access to everything, but cannot be trusted as a definitive one can. Read it as how the subject's circle wants the subject seen. It shows much about the time it was written in (its manners, and which attitudes were acceptable and, by implication, which were not), but not the real private life. Most biographies of living people are of this kind; the reader must still read all sides and make sense of them.
- **Ordinary.** Neither definitive nor authorised. Expect the author to know the facts and, above all, to convey what it was like to live that life in another time and place. Less reliable than a definitive life, often very good reading.
- **Instructive.** Written to teach a moral: to help readers become great and avoid the errors of the great. Read it less for information about the life than for its view of life in general. Such works can shape later history when people model their lives on them.

Autobiography:
- **No autobiography is wholly true, and none wholly false.** Knowing one's own life is harder than knowing another's, and every autobiography is written about an unfinished life. The temptation to say less, or more, than the truth, with no one to contradict, is almost irresistible; everyone has secrets and illusions about themselves. But every autobiography reveals something about its author, if only what they want to hide. Read all of them with caution.
- **Much of all writing is autobiographical.** No author keeps themselves wholly out of their work; even impersonal texts reveal their authors, and two authors who agreed on everything would still write different books. Within reason, read any text partly to discover its author's character.
- **But do not make the author's character the main concern.** Hunting for an author's secrets can make the reader miss what the author says plainly.
- **To know the truth about a life, read as many lives of the person as you can,** including their own account if there is one.
- **Why does it matter?** Like history, biography can lead to practical and moral action. It tells of a life, usually a more or less successful one, and the reader has a life to lead too.

## Questions to put to the learner
- What exactly does this history cover, and what does it leave out?
- Does the historian fit events to a theory, assign causes and motives while claiming only to report, or deny any pattern?
- From whose point of view is it told? What other accounts should you read?
- How is the story organised, by period or by theme? What does the historian treat as most fundamental?
- Is it plausible that people acted this way?
- Has the historian used the sources well? Is anything relevant missing or wrong?
- What does this show about how people act, then and now? What does it suggest is possible, or should be avoided?
- For a biography: which kind is it? What is the author's purpose? What might an autobiographer be leaving out, or adding?
- What does this life suggest for how you lead yours?
