# Social Science

Social science has a central core of anthropology, economics, politics, and sociology, and includes much of law, education, and public administration, some of business and social work, and much of psychology (whose place is disputed, since it studies individuals as well as societies). "Behavioural sciences" once meant fields that study observable, measurable behaviour and is now nearly a synonym. History supplies social science with data and examples but is not itself one, since it does not by itself yield systematic knowledge of patterns or laws. The definition is loose but workable. Social science's ideas and vocabulary also run through interpretive journalism, social criticism (on crime, poverty, education, welfare, war, government), and fiction that builds model societies to explore their consequences.

Read it by the general rules ([../analytical-reading/overview.md](../analytical-reading/overview.md)), with these adjustments.

## Why it is harder than it looks
- **It seems easy to read.** Its data often come from familiar experience, as in poetry and philosophy; its style is often narrative, as in fiction and history; its jargon is used in everyday talk; and words like "society" and "social" carry many familiar meanings. Jargon, metaphor, and strong feeling make for deceptively easy reading.
- **The ease is false.** Familiar phrases can be empty: readers and writers often do not know what they mean by a phrase like "social cohesion". Test your understanding of such familiar terms. Technical ideas used widely in popular writing are often made to do too much work without being understood, and if a writer is confused about a key term, the reader will be too.
- **Set your opinions aside to understand.** Readers usually have strong commitments on social questions, more than on most philosophical ones, and may feel it disloyal to stand back and question impersonally. But that detachment is required to answer the first two questions: a reader who refuses to hear what a text says cannot understand it.
- **Terms are the main difficulty.** Hard sciences declare their usage at the start and readers accept it. Social science does this less, partly because it has been less mathematical and partly because terms like "inequality" or "wellbeing" are much harder to define than a triangle, and readers tend to question the definitions. So authors struggle with their terms throughout, and coming to terms can be very hard, though some shared understanding of the key terms is usually possible ([../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md)).
- **It is a shifting mixture.** Social science mixes science, philosophy, and history, often with some fiction. Unlike history, whose mix is familiar, the mix changes from text to text and within one text.

## The rules, adjusted
- **Rule 1 is the hardest and most important step.** Saying what kind of text this is is as hard as defining social science. Identify the strands that make it up (scientific, philosophical, historical, fictional) and where the mix shifts. Doing so goes a long way toward understanding it ([../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md)).
- **Outlining, claims, and arguments are ordinary.** In a good text they pose no special problem; the terms are the difficulty.
- **The fourth question needs restraint.** This is where readers most often concede they cannot fault the author's case and still reject its conclusions, out of prejudgments about the approach or the conclusions. If the reader cannot show the author uninformed, misinformed, or illogical, they must agree as far as the author goes ([../analytical-reading/3-criticism.md](../analytical-reading/3-criticism.md)).
- **Read the literature, not a single text.** In social science it is usual to read several works on a problem rather than one text for its own sake: the field has few classics, readers usually come with a problem rather than an author in mind, there is typically no single authoritative work, and works are revised and superseded quickly. The need is more urgent here than in philosophy or history. Apply the analytical rules to each work, and the syntopical method to the set ([../syntopical-reading.md](../syntopical-reading.md)).

**Buddy as reader.** When a learner asks about one social-science text, answer from it, and say when the question is one the wider literature disputes; offer to bring other sources to bear rather than treating one study as settling it (adaptation).

## Questions to put to the learner
- Which strands make up this text: science, philosophy, history, fiction? Does the mix change within it?
- What exactly does the author mean by this familiar term? What do you mean by it?
- Has the author defined their terms, or do they struggle with them as they go?
- What are your own commitments on this subject? Have you set them aside long enough to hear the text?
- If you can't fault the conclusions but still disagree, is that a prejudgment?
- What other work on this problem should be read alongside this one?
