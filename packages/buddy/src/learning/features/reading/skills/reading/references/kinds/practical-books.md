# Practical Texts

A practical text is about action in a field where people can do better or worse: how to do something, or what ought to be done. It includes how-to books, manuals, guides, cookbooks, texts on an art or craft, advice, speeches and exhortations, and texts on ethics, politics, and economics that say what should be done. For how to recognise one, see Rule 1 in [../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md). The practical/theoretical line cuts across every field: theoretical texts divide into history, science, and philosophy, but practical texts can appear in any field.

General rules must be adapted to particular texts; no one reads a text in general. For every kind, the key is which of the four questions come first and what kinds of answers to expect.

## What makes practical texts different
- **A practical text cannot solve its own problems.** A theoretical text can. A practical problem is solved only by action, and action happens in the world, not on the page. A text on how to do something can only help; the reader must do it.
- **The reader supplies the particular judgment.** Action is always particular: here, now, in these circumstances. The judgment just before acting is almost never in a text, because the author cannot foresee the reader's situation. A practical text gives general rules for situations of a sort; the reader adds knowledge of their own situation and judges how the rule applies.
- **Rules and principles.** Any text with rules (prescriptions, maxims, directions) is practical. It may also state the principles that explain the rules; those are usually theoretical knowledge, the theory of a practice.
- **Two kinds of practical text.** Some are mainly rules (manuals, cookbooks, guides), and everything else serves the rules. Others are mainly about the principles that generate rules (most major works in economics, politics, and ethics). The difference is one of emphasis.
- **A text of practical principles can look theoretical.** Its claims and arguments look just like a theoretical text's, saying that something is the case. Its problems give it away: it always concerns a field of human conduct where people can do better or worse.

## Reading a rule book
- **The leading claims are the rules.** A rule is most directly an imperative, but may be stated as a plain statement. Recognise it by its recommending something as worth doing to reach an end; words like "good", "best", and "should" signal it.
- **The arguments try to show the rules are sound,** by appeal to principles, by examples of the rules working in concrete cases, or both. Look for both. Appeals to principle usually persuade less than examples but explain the rule better.

## Reading a text of practical principles
Read it as practical, not theoretical. Read between the lines: work out the rules that follow from its principles, even where it does not state them, and how those rules would apply in practice. Otherwise the reader has not really understood it and cannot judge it. Rules are made intelligible by principles; practical principles matter because of the rules and actions they lead to.

## Persuasion
- **Every practical text persuades, and legitimately.** Since the reader's verdict turns on accepting the author's ends, the author must win the reader to them, appealing to feeling as well as reason. No one makes serious practical decisions without being moved. A writer who ignores this is ineffective; a reader who ignores it is likely to be sold something without noticing.
- **The defence against propaganda is recognising it.** Only persuasion that goes unnoticed is really dangerous: what reaches the feelings without passing through the mind can put the mind out of action. A reader who knows the text's terms, claims, and arguments can spot the persuasive passages and the emotive use of words, and weigh them.
- **Keep some resistance, but do not be deaf.** Resistance is good when it prevents hasty acceptance. A reader who wants to be deaf to every appeal should not read practical texts.
- **The author's character and circumstances matter more here.** Nothing about the author of a mathematics text needs knowing. To understand and judge a moral, political, or economic text, know something of the author's character, life, and times.

## The four questions, adapted
1. **What is it about as a whole? Find out what the author wants the reader to do.** The outline is still needed, but Rule 4 dominates: knowing the author's objectives amounts to knowing what the author wants the reader to do.
2. **What does it say in detail, and how? Find out how the author proposes the reader do it.** Terms, claims, and arguments are still needed, but Rule 8 dominates: find and understand the means the author recommends.
3. **Is it true? Judge the ends and the means against what is right.** A rule is practically true if it works and if its working leads to the right end, one the reader rightly wants. Theoretical truth is agreement with how things are. With a theoretical text, compare the author's principles and assumptions with your own; with a practical text, the main judgment is about ends, not means. Even sound means will not win agreement if the reader rejects the end; there is no practical interest in the best means to an end one disapproves of or does not care about.
4. **Why does it matter? Agreement implies action.** A theoretical text that convinces calls for adjusting one's view of things, not necessarily for action. A reader convinced that a practical author's ends are worthy and the means likely to work can hardly refuse to act as the author wishes. A reader who does not act did not really accept the ends or the means.

- **Universal and selective ends.** Some ends apply to everyone, so the call to act applies to every reader. Some apply only to a class of people; the reader must decide whether they belong to it, and if they do, they are more or less obliged to act. Watch for fooling oneself about belonging, or accepting the end and means while wanting someone else to act.

**Buddy as reader.** When explaining a practical text, lead with what it wants the reader to do and the means it proposes, mark the persuasive passages as persuasion, and note where applying a rule depends on the reader's particular situation, which the text cannot know (adaptation: ordering an explanation by the adapted questions).

## Questions to put to the learner
- Is this mainly a text of rules or of principles?
- What does the author want you to do? How do they propose you do it?
- For a text of principles: what rules follow from these principles, even unstated, and how would they apply to your situation?
- Are the rules argued from principles, shown in examples, or both?
- Do the rules work? Do they lead to an end you rightly want?
- Do you accept the author's ends? If you disagree, is it with the ends or the means?
- Where is the author persuading you? Which words are there to move you rather than inform you?
- What do you need to know about the author and their times to judge this?
- If you accept the ends and the means, are you acting on them? Is the end meant for everyone, or for a group you belong to?
