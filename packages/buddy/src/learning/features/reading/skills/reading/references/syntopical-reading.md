# Syntopical Reading

Syntopical reading brings several texts to bear on one question and builds an analysis of the subject that may not be in any of them. Mere comparison is not enough. It is the most active and demanding level, demanding even when the texts are easy, and the most rewarding. It includes inspectional and analytical reading.

## What changes at this level
- **The reader and the reader's problem come first.** Analytical reading aims to understand one text. Syntopical reading reads each text for the light it throws on the reader's question, which may be far from the author's purpose; an author can help without meaning to.
- **The reader is in charge.** In analytical reading the reader stands to the author as student to teacher. Here the reader must master the situation and make the authors serve the question.
- **The question of significance matters most.** Why the discussion matters, and what follows from it, is probably the most important question here, and it can be answered only after the first three questions have been answered about the discussion.

## Before starting
Two things must be known first: that more than one text bears on the question, and, roughly, which texts. The second is much harder.
- **"The same subject" is hard to pin down.** Apart from a single event or period, what counts as the same subject is rarely clear. Two novels set in the same city are not therefore about that city. Texts on one idea may use its name across a huge range, raising the question whether there is one subject at all.
- **The paradox.** You must know the subject to choose the texts, but you can identify the subject only by reading them, after which half may turn out to be off-subject. Finding the relevant texts and passages may take longer than everything else together.
- **Inspection is the way through.** Inspectional reading comes into its own here.

## Stage I: Survey the field
1. **Build a tentative list of sources.** In a library this comes from catalogues, advisers, and the bibliographies in texts. For Buddy, the starting list is what the learner has: prepared resources, workspace files, pasted or cited text, and URLs you can fetch. You may suggest other sources from your background knowledge, but label them as unread suggestions and do not describe their positions as if you had read them (adaptation).
2. **Inspect every source on the list before reading any analytically.** Inspection shows whether each says something important on the question (one to come back to) or, however good, nothing that bears on it. It also sharpens your idea of the subject and cuts the list to a workable size. For a prepared pack, inspect through `10-toc.md`, the opening and closing chunks, and a search of the pack for the question's key words (adaptation; see [inspectional-reading.md](inspectional-reading.md)).

The two steps affect each other: inspection changes the list, and the list changes what you inspect. But inspecting all the sources and reading some closely cannot be done at the same time, by any reader. Researchers who try end up reading everything at one rate, too fast or too slow for most texts.

Relevant works are often found by accident or educated guess. Many do not use the subject's name, especially older ones, so a list grows through inspecting many more works than end up on it. An abstract can help decide whether a work is relevant, but never replaces reading it.

## Stage II: The five steps
### Step 1: Find the relevant passages
Re-inspect the sources that proved relevant to find the passages that bear most on the question. A whole text is rarely on your question; even when it is, read it quickly. Keep finding relevant sources and finding relevant passages as separate jobs at first: until you understand the question, which may take inspecting many sources, looking for passages while still judging sources leads to mistakes you must later undo. Once the question is clear, the two can merge.

For Buddy: search each pack for the question's key words and their likely synonyms, then read the matching chunks or pages in scoped reading. When the sources are too large to ingest together, this is the normal route (adaptation).

A topical guide to where subjects are discussed helps, if one exists: it points to passages without prejudging what they mean. It helps the reader start in the middle of great texts on a subject they care about, leads from one topic to related ones and into an author's wider context, and teaches three things: that a topic gives direction but not meaning, so the reader must work out exactly how a passage bears on it; that reading many passages on one topic from different authors sharpens the reading of each, by sequence, contrast, or amplification; and that finding one passage under several topics shows how much meaning a rich passage carries. Help from people can hinder: an expert who knows the literature may tell you not only where to look but how to read it, which gets in the way.

### Step 2: Bring the authors to terms
Authors will not share the same words or terms. Build a neutral terminology and make each author speak your language rather than theirs. This is probably the hardest step, because the habit, and the analytical reader's deference to an author, is to accept each author's terms.
- **Refuse any single author's vocabulary.** Adopting one author's terms lets you understand that text and not the others, and sheds little light on the question. A borrowed term drags in others that get in the way. Expect that no author's terminology will suit.
- **This is translation.** You impose common terms on authors who may never have faced your question and so never made terms for it. Build terms that help you understand every author and help answer the question.
- **Reserve a disputed word for the majority.** If most authors use a word for one idea and some for another, keep the word for the majority's idea and describe the minority's view in other words, even though they used that word.
- **See through the words.** Authors often say the same thing in different words, or different things in almost the same words. A reader who cannot see through language will mistake verbal differences for disagreement and miss real differences behind verbal likeness ([analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md)).

### Step 3: Get the questions clear
With your terms set, set neutral propositions too, by framing questions that bear on your problem and that each author can be read as answering, even if they never saw them as questions.
- **Order the questions.** Usually: first, questions about whether the thing exists or what it is; then, for authors who say it exists, how it is known or how it shows itself; last, questions about the consequences of those answers. The exact order depends on the subject.
- **Record silence.** If an author gives no answer, record them as silent or undecided on that question. Sometimes an implicit answer can be found: had the author faced the question, they would have answered thus. Do not put words in authors' mouths, but do not rely only on their explicit statements either.

### Step 4: Define the issues
When a clear question gets different answers, there is an issue between the groups of authors. With two answers the issue is simple; with more, order the answers in relation to each other and group the authors by view.
- **Join the issues well.** An issue is truly joined when two authors who understand a question the same way answer it in opposite ways. Differences often come from different conceptions of the question as much as from different views of the subject. Frame questions so that issues are joined as well as they can be, even if no author posed the question that way.
- **Group issues into controversies.** Issues around a closely connected set of questions make a controversy about that aspect of the subject. A useful structure: a general controversy, on which everyone with something to say takes a side (for example, whether the thing exists or happens at all); a special controversy, among those who say it does, about its nature or properties; and subordinate issues, among those same authors, about where and in what respects it holds. Arrange them in order even if no author did.
- **Issues can be constructed.** An issue does not always exist openly between authors; sometimes it must be built by interpreting their views on matters that were not their main concern.

### Step 5: Analyse the discussion
Steps 1 to 4 correspond to the structure and interpretation stages of analytical reading: they say what the discussion says and how. Step 5 asks of the discussion whether it is true and why it matters.
- Order the questions and issues to throw the most light on the subject: more general issues before less general ones, with the relations between issues shown.
- Ask the questions in an order you can defend, show how the answers differ and try to say why, and point to the texts that support how you classified each answer. Only then have you understood the discussion.
- For questions that have been argued for a long time, do not simply answer them yourself. Whatever truth can be found lies in the ordered discussion of opposing answers, many of them backed by strong reasons, more than in any one set of claims about it. A thorough analysis clears away dead ends and can prepare the way for someone else's breakthrough by making the shape of the problem visible.
- **Report agreements as well as disagreements.** Opposed views are usually each shared by several authors.
- **Know what agreement and disagreement imply.** Agreement among authors creates some presumption that the shared view is true; disagreement creates a presumption that none of the conflicting views is wholly true. One may be wholly true, each may hold part of the truth, all may be false (as may a view everyone shares), and a view not yet stated may be nearer the truth.

## Objectivity
A syntopical analysis does not aim at final answers. A report that tried to prove any view on the main issues would be one more voice in the discussion, not an analysis of it. Its contribution is detachment: look at all sides and take no side.
- Taking no side is achievable; looking at every side is not fully achievable, since not all sides can be listed, but try.
- Partiality creeps in even without explicit verdicts: through how an argument is summarised, what is stressed or neglected, the tone of a question, a passing remark, and the order in which answers appear. Balance question against question, avoid loaded comment, and check for over- and under-emphasis.
- Keep going back to the actual texts. When presenting results, summarise each author's position in the neutral terms and pair it with the author's own words, quoted without wrenching them from context, so others can judge the interpretation.

**Buddy as reader.** When the learner asks you to compare sources on a question, present: the question and its sub-questions; each author's answer in neutral terms, with a short quotation and a location; where authors are silent; the issues and how authors group on them; and where they agree. Take no side unless the learner asks for your own view; then give it separately, labelled, with reasons, after the analysis (adaptation). Only include sources you actually read, and name any you inspected and set aside as not relevant.

## Cautions
- **Prefer expository texts.** Including imaginative literature is risky: a story's substance is its plot, not positions on issues; characters rarely take clear positions; and when one does, it may not be the author's view (it may be ironic). Placing a story on one side of an issue takes heavy interpretation with doubtful results.
- **Social science usually needs this level,** because it has few authoritative works and many that are quickly revised ([kinds/social-science.md](kinds/social-science.md)). History does too: read more than one account of any event that matters ([kinds/history.md](kinds/history.md)).

## Objections, and why the method still works
- **"A neutral terminology distorts the authors."** Like translation between languages, it is difficult, not impossible.
- **"Each author is a separate world; they cannot be treated as in conversation."** A thinker from any age could, through an interpreter, soon discuss shared problems with us. The syntopical reader is that interpreter, and must know each text well.
- **"How an author says something matters more than what."** That would make all communication emotional. Communication has a rational content that can be right or wrong and understood apart from its manner.

## Notes and coaching
Notes at this level record the shape of the whole discussion, usually kept separately from any one text because they span them: an ordered set of questions and statements about the subject, with page references into several texts ([study.md](study.md)).

Questions to put to the learner:
- What exactly is your question? Has inspecting the sources changed it?
- Which sources bear on it, and which did you set aside, and why?
- Which passages in each source bear on the question, whatever the author's own purpose?
- What neutral words will describe every author's view? Where must you avoid an author's own word?
- What questions, in what order, can every author be read as answering? Where is an author silent?
- Where do the answers differ? Is the difference about the subject or about how the question is understood?
- Where do the authors agree?
- Is your write-up tilted by summary, emphasis, tone, or order? Did you quote each author next to your summary?

For a syntopical exercise, see [study.md](study.md).
