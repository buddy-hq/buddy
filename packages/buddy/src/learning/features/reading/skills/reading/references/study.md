# Study: Notes, Habit, and Exercises

This file serves Buddy as coach: how the learner should mark and take notes at each level, how the reading habit is built, and how to design questions and exercises at each level. The questions for each stage of analytical reading are in the stage files; this file is about the form of study.

## Why the learner should write while reading
- **Asking is not enough; the learner must try to answer, and writing makes that easier.** Writing while reading is the sign and the means of staying alert.
- **Writing keeps the reader awake, makes thought articulate, and helps memory.** Active reading is thinking, and thinking tends to come out in words; a reader who cannot put a thought into words probably does not have it yet.
- **The notes are a conversation with the author.** They record questions, agreements, and disagreements. Marking a text is taking it seriously enough to argue with it once it is understood.
- **A text becomes the reader's own when it becomes part of their thinking,** and writing about it as they read is the best way there.
- **The goal decides whether the reader stays awake.** Reading to grow in understanding means reading as actively as possible and expecting the effort to repay; letting the mind drift during time set aside for such reading defeats the reader's own purpose.
- **People fail at reading for understanding for lack of skill more than will.** Many know which texts would profit them and still fail, because they do not know how to make their minds do the work. What tires a reader is not stretching but stretching without success.

## Marking a text
The marking devices, and where they live in Buddy (the mapping is an adaptation: the learner's own notes live in their notebook, and Buddy's citations with locations stand in for marks in the margin):
1. **Underlining** major points and forceful statements. In Buddy: the learner's highlights or cited passages.
2. **A line in the margin** to stress an underlined passage or mark one too long to underline. In Buddy: a quoted block in the learner's notes.
3. **A star or other mark, used sparingly,** for the ten or dozen most important passages in the text, so they can be found again. In Buddy: a short "key passages" list with locations.
4. **Numbers in the margin** for a sequence of points in an argument. In Buddy: a numbered list of an argument's steps, each with its location.
5. **Cross-references** to other pages where the author makes the same, related, or contradictory points. In Buddy: "compare p. N" links in the notes.
6. **Circling key words and phrases.** In Buddy: a list of key terms with the author's sense of each.
7. **Writing in the margin or at the top or bottom of the page:** questions (and perhaps answers) a passage raises, a complicated discussion reduced to a simple statement, the sequence of major points through the text.

Two larger notes: a personal index of the author's points in the order they appear, and, after finishing, an outline of the whole text as a structure (a basic outline and the order of its parts, not page by page). That outline measures the reader's understanding.

Do not ask the learner to mark everything; marks mean something only when they select (kept from the current skill). Write a notes file for the learner only when they ask; otherwise suggest what to write and where (adaptation).

## Notes by level
The kind of notes depends on the level of reading.
- **Structural notes (inspectional).** Record the answers inspection gives: what kind of text it is, what it is about as a whole, and the order in which the author develops the subject. Write them while fresh, especially if the careful reading will come days or months later. These notes are about structure, not detailed substance. Defer any judgment of truth until the careful reading, even if inspection gave insights into the author's views.
- **Conceptual notes (analytical).** At this level the reader answers questions of truth and significance. The notes concern the author's concepts and the reader's own, as the reading deepened or widened them.
- **Cross-text conceptual notes (syntopical).** Still conceptual, but a note may point to pages in other texts as well as this one.
- **Dialectical notes (syntopical, for an expert reader).** Notes on the shape of the whole discussion among the authors, even though they never addressed each other. They usually live apart from any single text, because they span texts, and form an ordered set of questions and statements about one subject ([syntopical-reading.md](syntopical-reading.md)).

## Building the habit
- **A skill is a habit formed by practising rules.** An art as taught is a set of rules; as possessed, it is the habit that comes from working by them until it is second nature. Knowing the rules is a condition of the skill, not the skill.
- **Learn the acts separately, then fuse them.** Reading well is a complex skill, like driving. Each separate act needs full attention while it is learned; after practising the parts, the reader does each with less attention, and they merge into one performance. The acts must be learned separately before they can be forgotten as separate acts.
- **Many rules, one habit.** The number of rules reflects the complexity of one skill. When the parts become nearly automatic, attention can move from technique to the text itself.
- **Be patient with the rules.** Learning to read well is at least as complex as learning a physical skill, and more mental: the beginning analytical reader must think about their own mental acts. Relearning something that feels familiar can be humbling; a long list of rules at the start is to be expected.
- **The habit is remembering to ask the four questions while reading; the art is answering them precisely.**

**Buddy as coach.** Practise one act at a time on real texts: a session on classifying and stating the unity, another on finding key terms, another on reconstructing arguments. Combine them once each is easy (adaptation of "learn the acts separately").

## Designing questions and exercises
### General principles
- **Use texts worth reading.** Typical tests use unrelated fragments or trivia chosen to suit the questions. Choose practice texts that are worth reading for their own sake, so the exercise teaches something as well as building skill. In Buddy, the best practice text is usually the one the learner is already studying (adaptation).
- **Mix question types, and mix difficulty.** Essay questions (an extended written answer); choosing the statement that best states the main idea; choosing the one valid interpretation of a detail, or the one wrong interpretation among right ones; recognising a verbatim quotation; completing a statement with the best phrase; true or false; tables sorting items as likely or unlikely, or major or minor. Mix the types rather than grouping them, and range from easy to very hard; the hard ones can be the most enjoyable.
- **Vary where the answer comes from.** Most questions can be answered directly from the text. Some need knowledge from outside it that the reader should have. Some are inferential: pick the inferences the text reasonably supports, and reject spurious ones with no basis in it. Ask the reader to select all valid answers, whether stated, implied, true by logic, or true from background knowledge.
- **Questions should make the reader reflect, not just check.** Frame hard questions to make the reader think about what they read.
- **Answers should teach.** Give fuller answers than a bare key, especially at the higher levels. Accept more than one option when both are defensible, explain why a tempting option is wrong or only partly right, distinguish precisely worded options, say when a question turns out to be meaningless, and raise follow-up questions.
- **Unanswerable questions should send the reader to the text.** A question the reader cannot answer yet should not irritate but lead them to look for a better answer in the work itself, and to more questions.
- **Outside knowledge helps at every level.** Even at the lower levels, questions can draw on history and other background; the better informed the reader, the better they read.
- **Tests have real limits.** Criticisms of reading tests are largely valid, especially when big decisions rest on them alone: they may not be standardised, may be culturally biased, may not predict well, may allow more than one right answer, and may be graded arbitrarily. Many still distinguish degrees of skill, and learners facing them benefit from knowing how they work.

### Elementary level
Speed does not matter. The learner reads a short text (about 1,000 to 1,500 words) at a comfortable pace, roughly six to twelve minutes, marking phrases and sentences of interest and making a few notes, then answers questions. Questions check literal content and detail, draw on background knowledge, include inferences about the subject, and end with a short written comment on a statement from the text (noticing, for example, a metaphor it uses).

### Inspectional level
Speed is essential. Use a detailed table of contents as the text, treated as if it were the whole work; in Buddy, the pack's `10-toc.md` (adaptation).
1. The learner reads a short note about the author, in a few minutes, as background.
2. They skim the table of contents in under ten minutes.
3. They answer a first set of questions, on structure: how many major parts, what they are called, how each is divided and into how many subdivisions, which way the work moves, where a given section falls.
4. They read the table of contents superficially, in about twenty minutes.
5. They answer a second, harder set, on substance: what the work is about, what kind of work it is, the ideas it depends on, the author's concerns, and questions about its significance or symbolism that may be hard or impossible to answer from the contents alone. Ask these because they might be answerable, and because trying reveals something central about the work. Let the learner guess and see how close they come.

For an expository work, harder inspectional questions ask for definitions of key terms (with true-or-false checks), whether factors are major or minor, what a phrase in the contents means and why it matters, the author's problem stated exactly in a sentence or two, and the whole theory in no more than 100 words. Some of these are unfair from the contents alone; their purpose is to send the learner to the work for better answers. Half an hour of inspection can teach a lot about a great work and may lead the learner to inspect all of it or read it closely.

### Analytical level
Use a text the learner has actually read. The learner answers the four questions (what is it about as a whole; what does it say in detail, and how; is it true; why does it matter), using the rules. There is no answer key: analytical reading is solitary work, with only the reader's mind, and the answers are in the text.

Prompts to give:
- Classify the text.
- State what it is about as a whole, more briefly than the text does anywhere.
- Outline it, and explain why some parts get more space than others.
- State the author's problem precisely, not as a vague aim but as a specific question with specific goals.
- Find the key terms, the key claims, and the arguments, and construct the arguments yourself.
- Judge which problems were solved and which were not.
- Say where the text's account is incomplete, as precisely as you can.

**Buddy as coach.** Buddy does not hand over a key, but checks each of the learner's answers against the text, with locations, and sends them back to the passage where an answer does not hold (adaptation: Buddy acts as the check the text alone provides).

### Syntopical level
1. Treat the preparatory survey as done: define the subject and choose the texts.
2. Narrow the inquiry to one question.
3. Use short passages from two or more authors on that question.
4. The learner reads carefully with all the time they need (speed never matters here), marks, takes notes, and returns to the texts as often as needed.
5. A first set of questions identifies each author's categories, what they share and how they differ, how they rank, and one remark that raises a difficulty, then the other author's main problem and founding idea. This usually yields a first interpretation, often an apparent disagreement.
6. Ask the learner whether that interpretation is right, prompted by the difficult remark and by passages not yet considered.
7. A second set reveals what the first missed: often a deeper agreement, a shared key word that does not mean the same for both authors, and key terms one author raises that the other never mentions, so that agreement on the main question is not agreement on everything.
8. The learner states the conclusions the reading justifies and documents each answer, and any disagreement with the suggested answers, from the authors' own words.

## Coaching moves by level
- **Elementary:** "Which words or sentences stopped you? What does this sentence say, plainly?"
- **Inspectional:** "From the contents, how many parts are there and what does each do? What is the author's problem? State the whole in one sentence." Then have the learner write structural notes.
- **Analytical:** the questions in [analytical-reading/1-structure.md](analytical-reading/1-structure.md), [analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md), and [analytical-reading/3-criticism.md](analytical-reading/3-criticism.md), one at a time.
- **Syntopical:** the questions in [syntopical-reading.md](syntopical-reading.md), with the two-set exercise above.

When the learner writes a summary of the text and wants it checked, load the `summarise` skill for the checks.
