# Current Events, Digests, and Condensations

This covers news, magazine and opinion pieces, current-affairs books, and digests or condensations of other writing. Much of it does not need the full effort of analytical reading, and not every rule must be applied. But the four questions must be asked of anything read ([../analytical-reading/overview.md](../analytical-reading/overview.md)).

## Why it needs care
- **There is a duty to understand the present,** even though little current writing matches the best histories.
- **Current events pose the problem history poses.** We cannot be sure what is happening now any more than what happened before, yet we must try. We depend on reporters, and what we can know depends on them.
- **The reporter's mind is a filter, not a clear window.** Ideally a report lets reality show through. In fact the reporter filters out what they judge untrue, which is proper, but they can be wrong.
- **Know who is writing.** The most important thing to know about any report of current events is who wrote it: less the person than the kind of mind.
- **Be wary.** With the great authors of the past, this kind of wariness is rarely needed. But the author of a current piece, or their sources, may have an interest in the reader understanding it a certain way. Know that interest and allow for it.

## The five questions for any report of current events
These adapt the four questions to writing about the present.
1. **What does the author want to prove?** Assume nearly every current-events text wants to prove something. The blurb, standfirst, or preface often states the main contention.
2. **Whom does the author want to convince?** Insiders, the few who can act quickly, or everyone? If the reader is outside the intended audience, the piece may not be worth their time.
3. **What special knowledge, opinion, or prejudice does the author assume?** Many authors write only for readers who already agree; a reader who rejects the assumptions may only be irritated. Shared assumptions are hard to see. It is hardest to notice one's own habitual assumptions, the beliefs so settled they feel like facts; those of another age are easier to see. With writing about our own time we lack that distance, so the reader must look through the filter of their own mind as well as the author's.
4. **What special language does the author use?** Some words set off strong reactions in readers of a given time. Try to control those reactions, or at least notice them. Learning an author's special language is coming to terms with them ([../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](../analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md)).
5. **Does the author really know?** Probably the hardest question. Does the reporter know the facts, including the possibly secret thoughts and decisions of the people involved, and everything needed for a fair account? Bias is not the only risk: reporters of good will may themselves be kept uninformed by those who control information, knowingly or not, and the unknowing case is the more dangerous for the reader.

Some reading is done for information about how others understand things: to learn how other people have interpreted the facts. That is a legitimate aim of its own.

## Digests and condensations
- **A digest is skilled reading done for the reader, but the reader must still read it well.** Good digests reduce large amounts of material to the essential information; their makers are expert readers for information first and clear writers second. For an ordinary informational article, a condensation is adequate and often better than the original. The best writing cannot be condensed without loss; a summary of it is useful only if it sends the reader to the original. A digest saves the reader the length of reading, not the work of reading.
- **The greater the condensation, the more the reader must know the condenser.** Condensing is selecting. Cutting a little matters little; cutting a thousand pages to one makes what was left out decisive. Know the character of whoever did the cutting, and be more wary still. Since the original is not at hand, infer what was left out from the condensation itself, reading between the lines. This makes digests sometimes the most demanding reading of all.
- **Facts in digests come already interpreted,** selected by someone's view of what matters. Ask why it matters of informational reading too.

**Buddy as reader.** When the learner shares a news piece or opinion article, apply the five questions before answering questions about its claims, and separate what the piece reports from what it argues and from the language it uses to move the reader (adaptation). When the source is itself a summary or digest, say so and note that what was left out cannot be seen. When the learner asks you to summarise such writing, load the `summarise` skill.

## Questions to put to the learner
- What does the author want to prove, and whom are they trying to convince? Are you in that audience?
- What does the author take for granted? What do you take for granted?
- Which words here trigger a strong reaction in you?
- How could the author know this? What might they not know?
- What interest might the author or their sources have in your reading it a certain way?
- For a digest: who condensed it, how much was cut, and what might be missing? Should it send you to the original?
