# Outside Aids

Intrinsic reading reads a text in itself, apart from other texts; the analytical rules are intrinsic. Extrinsic reading reads it in light of outside aids. Outside aids help, and are sometimes needed for full understanding. In practice the two fuse: every reader brings other reading and life experience to outlining, interpreting, and judging, and no text should or can be read wholly in isolation.

There are four kinds of outside aid: relevant experience, other texts, commentaries and abstracts, and reference works. For Buddy, add your own background knowledge and the web (adaptation, below).

## When to use outside help
- **Do the work on the text first.** Seek outside help when a text stays unintelligible, in whole or in part, after you have done your best with it by the rules of intrinsic reading. Readers who act on this need less and less help over time.
- **Do not lean on aids.** Reading with a dictionary in one hand is a mistake (though strange words may be looked up), and running to a commentary at every puzzle is usually ill-advised. Help used before a first read-through gets in the way ([inspectional-reading.md](inspectional-reading.md)).
- **One exception.** A reader who belongs to a tradition that holds a text authoritative, and who cannot make sense of it alone, is expected to turn to the tradition's teachers and accept their reading ([kinds/philosophy.md](kinds/philosophy.md)).

**Buddy as reader.** Your background knowledge of a text, including what commentaries and summaries have said about it, is an outside aid. Read the text first, answer from the text, and bring in background knowledge only for what the text leaves unclear or for facts outside it; label it as outside knowledge. Never let it stand in for reading the source (adaptation, applying this rule to Buddy; see the grounding rules in SKILL.md).

**Buddy as coach.** A tutor is a present teacher who can answer questions, which a text cannot. That makes Buddy an outside aid too, and the same rule applies: when the learner is studying, let them work on the passage before you explain it, and then explain what is still unclear (adaptation).

## Relevant experience
- **Common and special experience.** Common experience comes to people just by living (it need not be universal, only shared by most people in the ordinary course of life: growing up, friendship, work). Special experience must be sought out and is available only to those who go to the trouble: laboratory work, fieldwork, expeditions.
- **Match the experience to the kind of text.** Common experience matters most for fiction, where judging whether a story is plausible rests almost wholly on it, and for philosophy, where the author appeals to it and the reader tests the author's principles against their own common sense and observation. Special experience matters mostly for science: to follow and judge an inductive argument, the reader must follow the reported evidence, helped by descriptions, illustrations, and diagrams. History needs both, because it is part story and part science based on documents, remains, and interviews.
- **Test your use of experience with an example.** The surest sign you have used experience properly is that you can give a concrete example of a point you think you understand, beyond the author's own examples. If you cannot, reread.

## Other texts
- **Read related texts in relation, and in a sensible order.** Many important texts respond to earlier ones. Reading the earlier text first can make a later one intelligible; many readers give up on a hard classic because they expect to understand it without the texts it depends on.
- **Related texts are a larger context.** As the whole text is the context for its parts, related texts are the context for the text.
- **Respect chronology.** Important authors were readers of earlier ones and wrote in conversation with them. Read them in an order that respects this, from past to present (more natural) or from present to past.
- **This matters most for philosophy, then history,** less for science and fiction; good novels and plays can be read on their own.

## Commentaries and abstracts
- **Use commentaries sparingly.** Commentators are not always right, and study guides often mislead (though they help with exams). Even a correct commentary is not exhaustive, and a confident one tends to confine your understanding to its points.
- **Read the author's own preface and introduction before the text; read anyone else's commentary after.** Read after doing your best with the text, and consult the commentary for the questions still open. Read first, it distorts the reading: you see only the commentator's points, and are at their mercy. Read after, you meet the commentator as an equal and can tell where they are wrong.
- **Dependence makes a worse reader.** A reader who relies on commentaries is lost when none is available, and may understand one text with help but read worse in general.
- **Abstracts and plot digests have two uses only:** to jog the memory of a text already read (best if the reader made the abstract during analytical reading), and, when comparing sources, to judge whether a work is likely to be relevant. An abstract never replaces reading the text.

## Reference works
A reference work cures ignorance only in a limited way. It cannot cure total ignorance or do the reader's thinking, and it cannot guide a reader who does not know what they are looking for. Using one takes four kinds of knowledge:
1. **Know what you want to know.** Have some idea, however vague, so you can ask an intelligible question; you need enough knowledge around the gap to ask about it.
2. **Know which reference work answers it.** Know what kind of question you are asking and which kind of work answers that kind; this needs a general acquaintance with the main types.
3. **Know how to find it in that work.** Know how the work is organised; read its introduction and directions before using it.
4. **Know that its compilers treat the matter as knowable.** Reference works hold only what is generally agreed: dates, definitions of words and things, histories of almost anything, much about the physical world. Moral questions, questions about the future, and deep questions about human life are not answered there, and unsupported opinion does not belong there. What a reference work contains reflects what its era thinks can be known.

### Dictionaries
- **Know what a dictionary is for.** It is mainly a tool for reading: for texts that use technical words, archaic words, allusions, or familiar words in old senses. It records the history of the language, so attend to the order and relation of a word's meanings, not only their variety.
- **Do not look words up on a first reading unless they matter.** Looking up too many words early loses the text's unity and order. Look up a technical or wholly new word only if it seems important to the author's overall meaning.
- **Do not settle arguments about large ideas with a dictionary.** Dictionary makers are authorities on usage, not on fairness or the good life. And do not memorise lists of words cut off from experience; a dictionary is about words, not things.
- **Ask it four kinds of question.** Words as physical things (spelling, pronunciation); as parts of speech (grammatical role, which can shift); as signs (their meanings and how they relate; synonyms and antonyms; proper and common names; concrete and abstract names); and as conventions with a history (origin, changes of form and meaning, and whether a sense is current, archaic, obsolete, idiomatic, colloquial, or slang). Read the dictionary's explanatory notes and abbreviations first.

**Buddy as reader.** When a learner asks what a word means in a text, give the author's sense from the context first, and the dictionary sense only as a contrast or when the context does not settle it ([analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md](analytical-reading/2-interpretation.md)) (adaptation).

### Encyclopedias
- **Use them for understanding the order of knowledge, not only for isolated facts.** Do not try to read one through or memorise it.
- **Find the hidden structure.** Alphabetical order masks an underlying arrangement of knowledge. Understanding comes from seeing facts in relation to other facts, so follow the editors' directions, often starting from the index or topical guide, whose grouped entries reveal that arrangement. (A contents page is arranged by topic; an index, by letter.)
- **Use them to settle questions of fact, not differences of opinion.** Facts should not be argued about. But encyclopedias disagree about facts too, so on anything that matters, check more than one, preferably written at different times. Do not rely on a single one.
- **Ask four things about any fact.** A fact is a claim, so ask what it means as well as what it says. A fact is a claim generally agreed to be true, not an opinion; honest reference works label reported opinions and treat unsettled matters only by reporting the disagreement. A fact reflects reality: a particular event or a widely accepted generalisation, not a theory until the theory is generally accepted. And facts are partly conventional: what counts as fact shifts as observation improves, especially in science, and is partly shaped by culture, though some facts everyone must accept.
- **An encyclopedia is not where understanding is pursued.** It contains no arguments (beyond accepted or historically interesting ones) and no imaginative literature, and understanding needs both reason and imagination. It is an adjunct to reading good texts; bad texts seldom need one.

## The web (adaptation)
The notes do not treat the web; the rules above apply to it by type (adaptation throughout this section).
- Buddy reads web pages only from what the learner cites or pastes, or a URL fetched with `webfetch` when available (SKILL.md, Getting The Text).
- A web page used to explain a text is a commentary: consult it after reading the text, for what is still unclear, and name it as a source outside the text.
- A web page used to check a fact is a reference work: use it for matters that are generally agreed, check more than one source when the fact matters, and do not use it to settle a difference of opinion.
- A web page on a current matter is current-events writing: ask who wrote it, what they want to prove, and whether they know ([kinds/current-events.md](kinds/current-events.md)).

## Questions to put to the learner
- Have you done all you can with the text itself before looking this up?
- Does this text appeal to ordinary experience or to special experience? Can you give your own example of this point?
- Which earlier texts does this one answer or depend on? Would reading them first help?
- Have you read the text before opening that commentary or study guide?
- What exactly do you want to know? Which kind of reference work answers that, and is it the kind of thing such a work treats as settled?
- Does this word matter enough to the author's meaning to look up now?
