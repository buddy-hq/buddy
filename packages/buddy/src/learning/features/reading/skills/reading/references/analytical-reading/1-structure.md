# Analytical Reading, Stage 1: Structure (Rules 1 to 4)

The first stage finds what a text is about: its kind, its unity, its parts, and the questions it answers. Every text worth reading has a skeleton, a unity with parts organised around it; a text without one is a mess. The reader's job is to see that skeleton under the surface without tearing the text apart. Together, Rules 1 to 4 answer most of the first basic question, "What is the text about as a whole?"

Inspect the text first ([../inspectional-reading.md](../inspectional-reading.md)); a good inspection does much of this stage.

## Rule 1: Classify the text
Know what kind of text you are reading, as early as possible, preferably before you begin. The rule applies to every text but matters most for exposition.

Asking "What kind of text is this?" unlocks the other questions. A reader who cannot answer it, or never asks it, will fail at many later questions, because each kind teaches differently, tackles different problems, uses different methods, and argues in its own style. Asking it is also nearly the best way to open a discussion of any text.

### The categories
You need the categories in mind before the signals can help; grouping texts as alike is not enough without knowing what the kind is.
- **Imaginative literature or exposition.** Imaginative literature (novels, stories, plays, epics, lyric poems) communicates an experience. Exposition conveys knowledge: anything made mainly of opinions, theories, hypotheses, or speculations offered as true in some sense. The line can blur: some novels carry a lot of science or social science, and some social science carries fiction. Ask rather than assume. With exposition, the main task is recognising which kind of exposition it is.
- **Theoretical or practical.** A theoretical text teaches that something is the case. A practical text teaches how to do something the reader wants or ought to do. Knowledge becomes practical when it is turned into rules for acting.
  - Any text that says what you should do or how to do it is practical: manuals in any field, guidebooks, texts on an art or craft, and texts on moral, economic, or political problems (normative texts, which say what ought to be done). Speeches and exhortations are practical too. See [../kinds/practical-books.md](../kinds/practical-books.md).
  - A practical text is practical whether or not it is true or you agree with it. A false practical text is a bad one, not a theoretical one.
  - A study that only reports how people actually behave, without judging it, is theoretical.
  - Every practical text persuades as well as advises, but persuasion is not the same as practical writing: a political harangue differs from a treatise on politics, and propaganda from analysis.
- **Within theoretical texts: history, science, philosophy.** Rough distinctions are enough at first.
  - **History** tells about particular events or things at a particular time and place, and how they changed; it narrates, often with comment on what the events meant. See [../kinds/history.md](../kinds/history.md).
  - **Science** looks for general truths about how things happen always or for the most part, regardless of time and place, and rests on special experience: laboratory work, controlled observation, or field research others could recheck with similar effort. See [../kinds/science-and-mathematics.md](../kinds/science-and-mathematics.md).
  - **Philosophy** also looks for general truths, but asks different questions and appeals only to common experience, the kind everyone has in daily life, and refers the reader to it for support. See [../kinds/philosophy.md](../kinds/philosophy.md).
  - The science-or-philosophy test applies only to texts that are one or the other, and may need some reading to apply. A text that does both is classed by its main emphasis. Neither is mere thinking or mere observing: both observe and think, differing in the experience they think about and in how they prove conclusions.
  - Social science mixes these strands and needs its own handling ([../kinds/social-science.md](../kinds/social-science.md)).

### Signals
- **Title and front matter.** Read the title and subtitle carefully and think about what they mean; readers often misread titles and so misjudge the text. A title can tell you where the account must begin and what it must cover. Authors usually make titles, subtitles, prefaces, contents, and chapter headings descriptive precisely so that readers can classify the text; heed them. For a prepared pack these are in `00-resource.md`, `10-toc.md`, and the opening chunks (adaptation).
- **Title words.** "How to", "the art of", "a guide to", or the name of a practical field (ethics, politics, engineering, business, and often economics, law, medicine) mark a practical text.
- **Words in the text.** A practical text soon keeps saying "should" and "ought", "good" and "bad", "ends" and "means"; its typical statement is that something should be done, that this is the right way, or that one thing is better than another as an end or a means. A theoretical text keeps saying "is": that something is true, that these are the facts.
- **Kind of question.** Questions about whether something is certain or valid are theoretical; questions about what something is for, or what end it serves, are practical.
- **When the front matter is not enough,** read the summary passages and the beginnings and ends of the text and its major parts, watching the vocabulary with the categories in mind. You can usually classify without reading far.
- **Mistrust the signs.** Some economics is purely theoretical; some texts claim to teach how to think; some authors confuse theory and practice; many texts are partly one and partly the other. It still pays to detect how the author approaches the problem, and the better you understand the distinctions, the better you use the signs.

### Procedure
1. Inspect the text: title, subtitle, contents, preface or introduction, index, blurb or abstract.
2. Read the title and subtitle closely and ask what they imply about scope and starting point.
3. Decide: imaginative literature or exposition? If imaginative, which form?
4. If exposition: theoretical or practical?
5. If theoretical: history, science, or philosophy? Or a mixture, as in social science?
6. If the front matter does not settle it, read summary passages and the beginnings and ends of the text and its major parts.
7. Hold the classification loosely and revise it as the author's approach shows.

## Rule 2: State the unity
Say what the whole text is about in one sentence, or at most a short paragraph.

- **"About" means the theme, not the kind.** Rule 1 says what kind of text it is. Rule 2 says what the author is up to: the main point.
- **Say it definitely, in few words.** You have grasped the unity only if you can state it briefly to yourself or someone else. If it takes many words, you have seen many things, not the one thing. A felt unity that cannot be put into words has usually not been grasped.
- **There is no single right statement.** A unity can be stated in many ways. One statement beats another in proportion as it is brief, accurate, and comprehensive, and quite different statements can be equally good. Your statement may differ from the author's. But not anything goes: the text is the same for every reader, so any statement can be checked against it.
- **Use the author's help, but do not rely on it.** Titles and prefaces often state the unity, and an expository author has no reason to keep the reader in suspense; the text may state its point in its first paragraph. Use that as a guide. The obligation to find the unity still belongs to the reader, as the obligation to have one belongs to the writer, and it can be met honestly only by reading the whole text. You may need to extend the author's statement once you have. Many readers cannot state a unity even when the author gave it: they cannot write concise sentences, neglect the rule, or skip the author's opening words.
- **Better texts have a more pervasive unity.** The better the work, in any art, the more nearly its unity pervades it.
- **Stories and histories have a unity of plot.** A good story, novel, or play has one main thread of action that ties everything together and can be told in a few sentences; the rest is episode. Many stories share the same essential plot; what the author does with it makes the difference. The unity of a history is also a thread of plot. See [../kinds/imaginative-literature.md](../kinds/imaginative-literature.md) and [../kinds/history.md](../kinds/history.md).

**Buddy as reader.** If you read only part of the text, say that your statement of the unity is provisional and name what you read (adaptation, applying the grounding rules). A statement of the unity is also the core of any summary; for summary requests, load the `summarise` skill.

## Rule 3: Outline the parts
List the major parts in order, show how each relates to the others and to the unity of the whole, and outline each part in turn as a whole with parts of its own.

- **A complex unity is one and many at once.** The parts must be organised, not merely collected; parts with no organic relation make a heap. Like departments in an organisation, each part has some independence and its own internal plan, but is connected to the others and helps make the whole intelligible.
- **The best texts have the most intelligible structure.** They are more complex than poor ones, but their complexity is simpler to grasp because the parts are better organised. That is one reason the best texts are the most readable. Lesser texts are more bothersome; to read them as well as they can be read, look for their plan, since if they hang together at all, they have one.
- **The outline formula (an ideal to approximate).**
  1. The whole text is about such-and-such (Rule 2). The author develops it in N major parts: the first about X, the second about Y, and so on.
  2. The first major part divides into sections: the first considers A, the second B, the third C.
  3. In the first section of the first part, the author makes points 1, 2, 3, 4. And so on, down to the smallest unit worth identifying.
- **Approximate it to fit the text and purpose.** A good reader does this habitually, may never write it all out, but could reconstruct it if asked. A rough notion of the structure is enough for most texts. An exhaustive outline, sentence by sentence, can be longer than the text; that is what the great commentaries do.
- **Outline the text, not the subject.** No text deserves a perfect outline, because none is perfect. Do not put in what the author left out. The outline is of the author's treatment, which goes only so far, not of the subject, which could be extended without end.
- **Make your own outline; use the author's divisions as guides.** Chapter and section divisions are the author's plan for writing; your outline is your plan for reading, and the two coincide only for a perfect writer and a perfect reader. Do not ignore headings; use them actively. There is often more plan in a good text than its divisions show. A pack's `10-toc.md` gives the author's divisions, not the outline (adaptation).
- **Rule 3 checks Rule 2.** A unity stated after a glance may be right, but you would not know it; it would be a lucky guess. Show how and why you stated the unity as you did by outlining the parts and showing how they develop it.
- **Reading and writing mirror each other.** The writer starts from a structure and builds the text on it; the reader recovers the structure from the text. Good writing neither hides a weak structure under padding nor leaves it bare; the structure shows in how the parts connect. The rest of the text is part of the work too and gives it the life an outline lacks. Good writing has unity, clarity (distinct parts), and coherence (an orderly arrangement); find the unity, and appreciate the clarity and coherence by finding the distinctions and order among the parts.
- **Rules 2 and 3 show whether a text is well made.** If, with enough skill and effort, you cannot find a text's unity or see its parts and their relations, it is probably a bad text, whatever its reputation. Do not decide this quickly, since the fault may be yours, but do not always assume it is: most texts are badly made in this sense.
- **Apply the structural rules to substantial parts.** A relatively independent part of an expository text (a chapter, a section of a report) has its own unity and parts; find them. Parts of expository texts are far more self-contained than parts of poems, plays, and novels. You can get the idea of an expository text by reading some parts carefully, though you then cannot follow Rule 3 for the whole. You cannot do this with a good novel, whose idea is in the whole. In scoped reading, apply Rules 2 and 3 to the part you read and say that is what you did (adaptation).
- **Rules 2 and 3 apply to every kind of text, differently.** The unity of a novel is not the unity of a treatise, and its parts are of other sorts in another order. See the kind files.

## Rule 4: Find the author's questions
State the question or questions the author set out to answer. The author began with questions, and the text holds the answers. Whether or not the author states them, formulate them as precisely as you can: the main question and, if it is complex, the subordinate questions, in an intelligible order (which are primary, which secondary, which must be answered first so that others can be answered later).

- **Rule 4 helps with Rules 2 and 3.** It does their work again in another light, and helps most with difficult texts. Readers who skip it misconceive the main point and produce chaotic outlines, because they do not see why the text has the unity it has or what its structure is for.
- **Do not claim to read the author's mind.** You cannot discover from the text what was in the author's head, especially in literary works; do not psychoanalyse an author from a play. Still, saying what the author was trying to do is often helpful with imaginative works and plainly valuable with exposition.
- **Know the kinds of questions anyone can ask about anything.** They make an author's problems easier to detect. The lists are not exhaustive, and must be adapted for imaginative literature, where they remain useful.

Theoretical questions:
- Does the thing exist?
- What kind of thing is it?
- What caused it, under what conditions can it exist, or why does it exist?
- What purpose does it serve?
- What follows from its existence?
- What are its characteristic properties and typical traits?
- How is it related to other things, alike or different?
- How does it behave?

Practical questions:
- What ends should be sought?
- What means should be chosen to a given end?
- What must be done to reach an objective, and in what order?
- Under these conditions, what is the right thing to do, or the better rather than the worse?
- Under what conditions would it be better to do this rather than that?

For practical texts, Rule 4 becomes "find out what the author wants the reader to do" ([../kinds/practical-books.md](../kinds/practical-books.md)). In science and mathematics it matters more than anywhere else ([../kinds/science-and-mathematics.md](../kinds/science-and-mathematics.md)).

## Coaching moves
Have the learner do each move, then check it against the text with locations.
- "What kind of text is this? What in the title, contents, or wording tells you?"
- "Does it keep saying 'is' or 'should'?"
- "State what the whole text is about in one sentence." Then: "Is that brief, accurate, and complete? Which parts of the text develop it?"
- "What are the main parts, in order? What does each do for the whole? What are the parts of the first part?"
- "What question is the author trying to answer? Which questions must be answered before it?"
- If the learner cannot find a unity or a structure: "Is the fault yours or the text's? What would you need to see to decide?"

Ask the learner to write the kind, the one-sentence unity, and the outline as structural notes (see [../study.md](../study.md)).
