# Analytical Reading, Stage 2: Interpretation (Rules 5 to 8)

The second stage finds what a text says: its terms, its claims, its arguments, and its answers. It answers the second basic question, "What does it say in detail, and how?", and prepares the last two.

These rules are for exposition. Poems and stories do not deal in terms, claims, and arguments in this sense; their parallel rules are in [../kinds/imaginative-literature.md](../kinds/imaginative-literature.md).

## Language and thought
Every interpretive rule has two steps: one that deals with language (words, sentences, paragraphs) and one that goes through the language to the thought behind it (terms, propositions, arguments). Both are needed. Language used without thought communicates nothing, and thought cannot be communicated without language.

Language is an imperfect medium, which is why interpretation is needed at all. A good writer works to reach the reader through it, and the reader must meet the writer halfway; communication needs skill on both sides.

The greatest error in applying these rules is to assume that the units of language and the units of thought match one to one. One word can carry several terms and one term can be carried by several words; one sentence can hold several claims and one claim can be put in several sentences; an argument can occupy part of a sentence or several paragraphs. Keep this in mind throughout.

A working grasp of grammar is needed: how nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and modifying clauses work, and how to take a sentence apart by its syntax, informally. Until words, sentences, and paragraphs have been analysed, they are a barrier rather than a medium.

## Rule 5: Come to terms with the author
Find the important words, then settle precisely what the author means by each.

A term is a word used in one definite sense. Almost every word has several meanings, but a writer can use it in one sense at a time. When writer and reader use a word with the same meaning, they have come to terms. When the author means one thing and the reader takes another, words have passed between them but not terms, and communication has failed. Terms are the basic unit of communicable knowledge; without them nothing passes from author to reader. Terms are not in the dictionary, though their materials are: they exist only in communication, when a writer avoids ambiguity and a reader follows the writer's use.

### Finding the important words
Most words in a text are not important; they are used as everyone uses them, and context shows any shift, as in conversation. Importance is a matter of degree. The words to find are those the author uses in a special way.
- **The words that trouble you.** You find key words by trying to understand the passage, which is circular: if you understand a passage, you know its important words; if you do not, it is probably because of how the author uses certain words. Mark the words that give trouble; ordinary words should not, so these are likely the special ones. Words important to the author that do not trouble you mean you have already come to terms; work is needed only where you have not.
- **Words the author stresses.** Quotation marks, italics, a discussion of a word's senses and how it will be used, a definition of the thing it names. Words in the author's explicit starting points (definitions, axioms, postulates) are basic.
- **Technical vocabulary.** Every field has one. Some authors state theirs at the start; otherwise find it from prior knowledge of the field, or recognise words that must be technical because they are not ordinary. Knowing the kind of text, its unity, and its parts (and its title, headings, and preface) helps separate technical words from ordinary ones. One technical word leads to others.
- **Ordinary words made technical.** Where a field lacks a settled vocabulary (philosophy is notorious), authors coin words or take a common word and give it a special sense. This misleads readers who think they already know the word. Good authors usually warn you.
- **Words the author argues over.** When an author explains how others use a word and why they use it differently, that word matters to them.
- **The special vocabulary.** A small set of words carries an author's leading ideas and the weight of the analysis and argument. Some are used in a very special way, some in the field's traditional way. These, plus any word you do not understand, are the ones to work on.
- **Older texts.** Ordinary usage depends on time and place. Recent authors use most words as we do; in older texts it is harder to tell which words were used in the ordinary way of their time, and deliberate archaism and translation make it harder still.

The outstanding fault of the passive reader is inattention to words and so failure to come to terms. A text that can raise the reader's understanding will contain words they do not fully understand; reading those as ordinary words, as in a newspaper, gets nowhere. Note deliberately the words you must work on.

### Finding the meanings
For each important word, in the text's order:
1. Decide whether the word has one meaning in the text or several.
2. If several, see how the meanings are related.
3. Note where it is used in one sense or another, and whether the context shows why the meaning shifts, so you can follow the word as flexibly as the author does.

- **Use the context you understand.** Find the meaning of a word you do not understand through the meanings of the surrounding words you do. A definition works this way. Since most words in any text are familiar, the reader has the materials; "I can't understand any of it" usually means the reader has not tried.
- **Work by trial and fit.** There is no rule of thumb. A word in place is a term, fixed by a meaning you and the author share. Fit the rest by trial and error; each word placed makes the next easier. Mistakes show because the picture will not complete, and need readjusting. With practice you can check yourself and stop thinking you understand when you do not.
- **Several senses are not ambiguity if they are distinguished.** Ambiguity is using a word in several senses without distinguishing or relating them. An author who separates the senses of a critical word, and lets the reader do the same, is offering terms. Authors normally use important words in several senses.
- **A word can stand for a combination of its senses.** If the senses are related, the word can carry all of them, some, or one at a time; each definite use is a term.
- **Synonyms carry one term in several words.** Good authors vary their words. Do not assume every change of word is a change of term, or that every repetition keeps the same term.
- **Phrases can be terms.** A phrase working as one unit carries a term just as a word does, and is less likely to be ambiguous because its words restrict each other.
- **Separate the vocabulary from the terminology.** List the important words in one column and their meanings in another. One word will map to several terms, and one term to several words.
- **Bad texts resist.** No text fits perfectly. The better the text, the better its terms fit and the more interpretation yields. If an author uses words ambiguously, you cannot find what they mean; you can only find that they were not precise.
- **Questions that come up with practice:** literal against metaphorical use; abstract against concrete words; proper against common names; defining words against defining things, and words that cannot be defined yet have definite meanings; emotive use of words (to stir feeling or move to action rather than to inform); and how rational speech differs from disordered speech. Study grammar and logic on these when your own reading raises them.

**Buddy as reader.** When you explain a passage, use each key word in the author's sense, and say so when it differs from the everyday sense or from a dictionary definition. Do not let a synonym in your explanation suggest the author changed terms, or a repeated word hide a change of sense (adaptation, applying Rule 5 to explanations).

## Rule 6: Find the leading propositions
Mark the most important sentences and find the claims they contain.

A proposition is a declaration of the author's judgment: something affirmed as true, denied as false, or asserted as fact. It declares knowledge, not intentions. A preface may promise to teach; the propositions show whether the promise was kept. Terms usually come first, then the claims built from them.

- **Not every sentence is a proposition.** Questions state problems; propositions answer them. Sentences of wish or intention may reveal the author's purpose but do not carry the knowledge being set out.
- **One sentence can carry several propositions, or different ones.** A compound sentence ("and", "if ... then", "not only ... but also") can hold two or more claims, sometimes a whole argument. Even a grammatically simple sentence can say several things, some true and some false. And the same sentence expresses a different proposition if its terms shift.
- **Separate them before judging.** You may accept one claim in a sentence and reject another; without separating them you cannot judge with discrimination. Interpretation of an important sentence is not finished until all its distinct claims are separated out. Practise by stating each claim in your own words, numbering them, and relating them.
- **One proposition can be put in different sentences.** Recognise equivalent sentences as the same claim.
- **Which sentences are important.** Every sentence must be understood, but most, like most words, cause no trouble and can be read quickly. For the reader, the important sentences are those understood just well enough to know there is more to understand; read them much more slowly. They are likely the author's most important ones too, since the most important things are the hardest. For the author, the important sentences state the judgments the argument rests on: the main affirmations and denials and the reasons for them. A text contains much else (how the author reached the view, why it matters, remarks on words, comments on others); the main sentences should stand out as if in relief.
- **Use the author's signals.** Some authors say when a point is important, use typography, or set leading sentences apart (numbered definitions, postulates, and propositions; a question followed by objections and the author's answer). A reader who treats everything as equally important, at one speed, misses even these.
- **Be puzzled, and notice it.** The difference between passages understood at once and those that are not locates the sentences carrying the load. Noticing that you are puzzled is where learning from a text begins. A reader who never asks what a passage means gets no insight they did not already have.
- **Slow down over what puzzles, not what interests.** Readers who vary speed often pause over the wrong sentences. In older texts, surprising facts are a distraction; curiosity about the author, their language, or their world is not the same as trying to understand their ideas.
- **Key words and key sentences lead to each other.** Marked words point to sentences worth attention, and a puzzling sentence points to its words. The rules come in order but need not be followed in order: knowing the terms gives you the proposition, and understanding the proposition gives you the terms.
- **Leading propositions belong to the main argument.** They are premises or conclusions. Sentences forming a sequence with a beginning and an end are probably the important ones.
- **Find the propositions through context.** Interpret all the words of a sentence, especially its principal ones, and bring the surrounding sentences to bear, working from what you understand to what you do not.

### Two tests of understanding
1. **Say it in your own words.** If, asked what a sentence means, you can only repeat the author's words in a new order, you probably do not know what they mean; only words have passed to you, not thought. Ideally, say the same thing in completely different words. Translating it into another language is the strictest version of this test, since only the thought survives translation. A reader who cannot restate a claim would not recognise it if the author put it another way, and a reader who treats the author's equivalent sentences as different claims has not seen through the words.
2. **Give an example.** Point to an experience the claim describes or bears on, or a particular case of the general truth. An imagined case is often as good as a real one. If you cannot illustrate a claim in any way, suspect that you do not know what it says. Some scientific claims need special experience to illustrate. Claims refer to the world; without acquaintance with the facts they concern, actual or possible, you are only playing with words.

Using words without regard to the thoughts they carry or the experiences they refer to is the besetting fault of readers who do not read analytically: they hold what they read as a verbal memory they can recite but not use. Seeing through words to claims also matters when comparing authors, who often say the same thing in different words, or different things in similar words ([../syntopical-reading.md](../syntopical-reading.md)).

**Buddy as reader.** Explain a claim by restating it in different words from the author's and giving an example, and use the same two tests to check a learner's understanding (adaptation: applying the tests to explanations).

## Rule 7: Find the arguments
Find the passages that state the text's important arguments; where no single passage does, assemble each argument from sentences scattered across paragraphs until you have the sequence of claims that makes it up.

Knowing the author's claims is not enough. Claims without reasons are only the author's opinions; find out why the author thinks the reader should accept them.

An argument is a set of statements in which some give reasons for another. Words like "because", "since", "therefore", "if ... then", and "it follows that" signal one. Some arguments claim truth; some claim only probability. Premises are the source of the conclusion even when stated after it. A valid argument's conclusion follows from its premises, but it is not therefore true, since the premises may be false.

- **Arguments do not match paragraphs.** There is no fixed convention for paragraphs. An argument may be one complicated sentence, part of a paragraph, a paragraph, or several. Many paragraphs hold no argument at all (evidence, how evidence was gathered, digressions); read those quickly.
- **Authors differ in how plainly they argue.** Good expository writers try to show their thinking. Writers in a mathematical style come close to one argument per paragraph; most others put several arguments in one paragraph or spread one over several. The looser the text, the more you must search a whole chapter to assemble one argument, and some texts make the search vain.
- **An argument is a movement of thought.** It may start with the conclusion and then give reasons, or start with evidence and reasons and arrive at the conclusion.
- **Mark the steps.** Write out an argument's claims if needed, but usually marks and numbers showing which sentences belong together in sequence are better. For Buddy, cite the location of each step (adaptation).
- **Use the author's summaries after the work, not instead of it.** A good text sums itself up as its arguments develop. When an author summarises at the end of a section, look back for the material the summary draws on. A reader who has worked through the text can use a recap; one who has not cannot. Inspection shows where the summaries are.
- **Make every step explicit.** Omitted steps mark a bad or loose text. Some omissions are harmless because readers supply the step from common knowledge; others mislead, sometimes deliberately (leaving unsaid a relevant point that would be challenged if stated is a propagandist's trick). Making every step explicit is a sound maxim of careful reading.
- **Put every argument in a nutshell.** Arguments may be built on arguments, but the unit is the single argument; find those and you will not miss the larger sequences.
- **No training in logic is needed.** Texts that teach are written for general readers. A mind that works while reading, comes to terms, and reaches the claims will see the arguments.

Three aids:
1. **Conclusion and reasons.** If you find the conclusion first, look for the reasons; if you find the reasons first, see where they lead.
2. **Induction and deduction.** Tell apart arguments that offer particular facts as evidence for a generalisation (inductive) from those that offer general statements to prove a further generalisation (deductive). The ability to tell them apart matters more than the names. In science this is the difference between proof by reasoning and establishment by experiment; some claims can be supported either way, some only one way.
3. **Assumed, proved, self-evident.** Note what the author says must be assumed, what can be proved or otherwise supported, and what needs no proof because it is self-evident. Not everything can be proved, as not everything can be defined; proofs need a starting point. Every line of argument starts either from assumptions writer and reader agree to (which can be anything, so long as they are agreed) or from self-evident claims neither can deny. Not every self-evident claim is a tautology: a tautology is true only by the definitions of its words and teaches nothing, while a genuinely self-evident claim expresses an understanding of how things are related, its opposite is seen at once to be false, it rests on common experience, and it instructs. Do not dismiss all of them as tautologies.

## Rule 8: Find the solutions
Decide which of the author's questions (Rule 4) were answered and which were not, and which failures the author knew of. After coming to terms and grasping the claims and arguments, check:
1. Which of the problems the author set out to solve did they solve?
2. In solving them, did they raise new problems?
3. Of the problems not solved, old or new, which did the author know they had failed to solve?

A good writer, like a good reader, should know whether a problem has been solved. Rule 8 ties the interpretation back to the structure.

When Rules 5 to 8 are done, you can be reasonably sure you understand the text. Until then, follow the author with eyes and mind open and objections held back. From here on you may argue with the author ([3-criticism.md](3-criticism.md)).

## Coaching moves
Resolve a learner's confusion in this order: words, then claims, then arguments, then what follows (kept from the current skill; it follows the order of the rules). Apparent disagreement with a text usually comes from misunderstanding it, and misunderstanding often starts with a word taken in a sense the author did not mean.
- "Which words here are you unsure of? Which does the author stress, define, or argue about?"
- "Is this word used the same way throughout? Where does its meaning shift?"
- "Which sentence here do you understand only well enough to know there's more to it?"
- "How many separate claims are in this sentence? Number them."
- "Say that in completely different words." Then: "Give me an example, real or made up."
- "What is the conclusion here, and what are the reasons? Is anything left unsaid that the argument needs?"
- "Is this argument from particular cases to a general claim, or from general claims to another?"
- "What does the author ask you to assume? What do they prove?"
- "Which of the author's questions did the text answer, and which did it leave open?"

Do not move on while the learner cannot say what a passage was trying to do (kept from the current skill). Do not ask the learner to mark everything; marks mean something only when used sparingly ([../study.md](../study.md)).
