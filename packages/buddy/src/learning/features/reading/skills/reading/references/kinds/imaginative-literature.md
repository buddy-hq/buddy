# Imaginative Literature

Imaginative literature (stories, novels, epics, plays, and poems, prose or verse) communicates an experience and gives pleasure. Exposition conveys knowledge about experiences the reader has had or could have; imaginative writing tries to give the experience itself, one the reader can have only by reading, and if it succeeds, something to enjoy. It appeals mainly to the imagination, where science and philosophy appeal mainly to the intellect; this is a matter of emphasis.

The fifteen analytical rules are for exposition. The four questions apply to any text, and imaginative literature has parallel rules for them, below. The general rules are in [../analytical-reading/overview.md](../analytical-reading/overview.md); this file says how each changes.

## Why it is harder than it looks
Reading imaginative literature well is harder than reading exposition, though more people seem to do it. Readers who enjoyed a novel often cannot say what they liked or why, which suggests they did not read below the surface. It is easier to be pleased than taught, but harder to know why one is pleased; beauty is harder to analyse than truth. Over time, great poems and novels may be the most demanding reading there is, because they offer none of the help exposition gives with terms, claims, and arguments, and they deal with things hardest to write about well.

## What not to do
- **Do not resist the work's effect.** This is the most important negative rule. Reading fiction is active in a different way: not poised to pounce, but open. Let the story act on you and move you. The aim is a deep experience; if the reader escapes, it should be into a deeper reality, their own inner life and view of the world.
- **Do not look for terms, claims, and arguments.** Those are logical devices. Imaginative writing uses the ambiguity of words for richness and force, building with metaphor as exposition builds with words sharpened to one meaning. A poem or story may carry several distinct but related meanings and relies as much on what it implies as on what it says; the whole says something none of its sentences say. Exposition tries to leave nothing between the lines.
- **Do not judge it by the standards for knowledge.** The "truth" of a story is its plausibility: it must be a likely story, true to the world the author created and re-created in the reader, not a verifiable report. Errors of geography or history should be criticised only when a work presents itself as a treatise on those subjects; a misstatement of fact does not spoil a story that surrounds it with plausibility. (History, by contrast, is read for truth; [history.md](history.md).) A philosophical text can be tested against the reader's common experience; a great imaginative work cannot, because its characters and situation are unique.

## How it teaches
Imaginative literature teaches indirectly. People learn from experience, including the imagined experience a story creates. Exposition teaches directly, by commenting on experience; imaginative writing teaches by creating experience to learn from. To learn from a story, the reader does their own thinking about the experience; to learn from a scientist or philosopher, the reader first understands the thinking the author did.

## The parallel rules
**Structure**
1. **Classify the work by its kind.** A lyric tells its story mainly through a single emotional experience. Novels and plays have complex plots with many characters, their actions, reactions, and feelings. A play tells its story entirely through action and speech; the playwright rarely speaks in their own voice, as a novelist can. Each calls for a different kind of openness, so recognise the kind at once.
2. **Grasp the unity, which is always in the plot.** Test yourself by stating it in a sentence or two, as a brief narration of the plot, not as a claim or argument. The plot is not the experience itself but its framework, standing for the unity as a logical summary stands for an argument. Even a lyric has a plot in this sense.
3. **Find how the whole is built from its parts in time.** The parts are the steps by which the plot develops: details of character and incident. They follow a time order, beginning, middle, and end, not a logical order. Know where the narrative really begins (not necessarily on the first page), what it passes through, and where it comes out; the crises that lead to the climax; where and how the climax comes; and what happens after it within the narrative. Parts of fiction make little sense taken out of the whole, unlike parts of good exposition.

**Interpretation**
1. **Get to know the elements.** Episodes, incidents, characters, and their thoughts, speeches, feelings, and actions play the role of terms. The reader has not grasped a story until they know its characters and have lived through its events.
2. **Become at home in the imagined world.** The scene or background connects the elements, as a claim connects terms. Know the world as someone living in it would; be willing to befriend the characters and share their fortunes with sympathy, as with a friend's. Then the characters and events stop being a list and become a community the reader knows.
3. **Follow the plot as it unfolds.** As the argument is the movement of an expository text, the unfolding plot is the moving connection of a story's elements. Having joined the characters in their world and accepted its laws, follow them through what happens; the plot drives everything else.

Do not press these parallels too hard; they are analogies. Their purpose is to make the reader progressively aware of the author's achievement, enriching the pleasure, so the reader knows what they like and why.

**Criticism**
1. **Do not judge until you have fully appreciated what the author tried to make you experience.** This is the parallel of understanding before judging.
2. **Grant the author their world.** Do not question the world, subject, or premise the author chose; judge only what they made of it.
3. **With fiction, the reader likes or dislikes; they do not agree or disagree.** Judgment of exposition concerns truth; judgment of imaginative work concerns mainly beauty, which is tied to the pleasure a work gives once known well.
4. **Appreciate before stating likes and dislikes.** Appreciating means having the experience the author aimed at by working on the reader's feelings and imagination. It cannot be done passively, any more than understanding philosophy can; it takes all the acts of careful reading.
5. **Move from taste to judgment.** The first verdict is one of taste: I like it or not, and why. Those first reasons are usually about the reader. To complete the criticism, point to what in the work caused the reaction, moving from what I like and why to what is good or bad in the work and why. The better a reader can name the causes of their pleasure, the nearer they come to seeing the work's artistic virtues and forming a standard, often shared with others of similar taste. Good taste can be learned by anyone who learns to read.

**Finding the author's problem** applies with care: saying what the author was trying to do often helps, but do not claim to read the author's mind from the work. The theoretical and practical question lists in [../analytical-reading/1-structure.md](../analytical-reading/1-structure.md) remain useful when adapted.

## The four questions, adapted
1. **What is it about as a whole?** Answered when you can describe the unity of the plot, including the action or movement of a lyric.
2. **What does it say in detail, and how?** Answered when you can say what roles the characters play and recount the key incidents and events in your own words.
3. **Is it true?** Answered with a reasoned judgment of its poetic truth: is it a likely story, does it satisfy heart and mind, is it beautiful, and why in each case.
4. **Why does it matter?** Strictly, this does not apply. With exposition, answering it means acting on an accepted practical text or adjusting one's views after a theoretical one. After reading a story, play, or poem well, no action is required; the reader's responsibilities end with the first three questions. Stories can carry political, economic, or moral points more powerfully than exposition, and have been censored for it, but practical effect is not their essence: imaginative writing is fine art, an end in itself. If a work moves the reader to act, ask whether it contains an implied statement that produced the feeling. It is right to notice and respond to such statements, but then the reader is responding to something other than the work, which exists in its own right and asks only to be experienced.

## Stories, novels, and epics
- **Read a story quickly, ideally at one sitting.** For a long novel, compress the reading into as short a time as possible. Otherwise the reader forgets what happened and loses the plot's unity. Readers who stretch out a novel they love are often indulging their feelings about it more than reading it.
- **Read with full immersion.** Let the characters into your mind and heart and suspend disbelief. Do not condemn what a character does before understanding why (and perhaps not even then). Live in the characters' world, where their actions may make sense, not in your own. Do not judge the world as a whole until you have lived in it as fully as you can.
- **Reading fast shows the whole; reading closely shows the details.**
- **Sort out characters and incidents gradually.** Do not give up on a long novel because of its many characters; as when joining a new class, individuals emerge from the crowd. Many are background figures who set off the main ones. By the end the reader will know who matters.
- **Do not expect clarity from the start.** The author flags what matters to the plot, but, as in life, events are understood looking back. After finishing, the relations among events become clear.
- **A story must be finished to be read well.** It ends on its last page; its characters have no life outside it, and guessing what happened before or after is beside the point. That limit is part of its satisfaction.
- **Stories meet unconscious needs.** Readers like or dislike certain kinds of people without knowing why, so a story that rewards or punishes them can stir feelings stronger than its merit warrants. Readers enjoy a sympathetic character's good fortune, identify with characters who love or who conquer or suffer, and find relief in the justice stories supply that life often seems to lack. In tragedy, the hero comes to understand their fate, and the reader shares that understanding without the suffering.
- **In judging, separate private needs from common ones.** Distinguish a story that satisfies the reader's own particular needs ("I like it, but I don't know why") from one that satisfies needs almost everyone shares. The latter are the stories that last, giving a sense of justice and understanding; their world is somehow good.
- **A satisfying story deals out fitting justice.** People get what their true merit deserves, and the storyteller convinces the reader of it. In a bad story, rewards and punishments seem random.
- **Epics make the greatest demands and give the greatest rewards.** Their difficulty lies less in their verse (prose translations exist for most) than in their elevation; they demand great attention, involvement, and imagination, and they reward only readers who do a thorough job. The major epics are foundational reading that later writers have almost all read.
- **Read a story or play through before studying it.** Read without stopping for glossaries or notes, and talk about the first reading before close study ([../inspectional-reading.md](../inspectional-reading.md)).

## Plays
- **Read a play as a story, but build the world yourself.** Plays lack the description novels provide, so the reader must be more active in creating the setting.
- **A play is incomplete on the page.** The whole work is realised only in performance, as music is in being heard; the reader must supply the missing physical dimension.
- **Stage it in your imagination.** Once you know what the play is about as a whole and in detail, and have asked the questions for any story, imagine it acted: direct the actors, decide how lines are spoken and scenes played, which words matter and which action is the climax. Where the playwright gives no direction (for example, whether a character is lying), you must decide, and the decision will shape your understanding. Do this even when directions are explicit: what is heard is fixed by the text, but what is seen is not. A play not staged in the mind has been read only in part.
- **The playwright rarely speaks directly.** A play tells its story through action and speech. Exceptions: in some comedy a character steps out to address the audience with the author's views, and some playwrights publish prefaces and detailed directions. When a playwright provides a preface, read it.
- **Read a play at one sitting,** as nearly as possible, to feel the whole.
- **Read puzzling verse aloud before turning to the notes.** Where verse drama is obscure, often because the language has changed, read the passage aloud, slowly, as if to an audience, with expression, trying to make the words mean something. Many difficulties clear. Go to the glossary or notes only if that fails.
- **Few plays are worth reading on the page, but those few are very great.** Most plays were written to be acted and are incomplete when read. The few great ones hold some of the deepest insights in any writing.
- **Ancient drama asks more of beginners.** Often only single plays of a set survive, little is known of how they were staged, and they assume stories their first audience knew. They still repay the effort and underlie much later drama. Where a chorus speaks, imagine it at ordinary human scale and the protagonists as larger than life.
- **In tragedy, the essence is lack of time.** Most problems in a classical tragedy could have been solved with enough time, but there never is enough: decisions must be made at once, without weighing consequences, and fallible heroes choose wrongly. It is easy to see what should have been done; ask whether you would have seen it in time.

## Lyric poetry
- **What counts as poetry.** For reading purposes, poetry is words arranged in a more or less orderly and disciplined way. Definitions that make it any overflow of feeling are too broad; definitions that require rhyme, a call to action, praise, or a special poetic diction are too narrow. Between them is a core most people recognise.
- **Poetry is less forbidding than people fear.** Read the right way, it takes less work to approach than expected, and it is often worth whatever work it takes. A good poem can be reread and thought about for a lifetime.
- **First, read the poem through without stopping,** whether or not you think you understand it. This matters more for poems than for any other kind of text. Most trouble comes from stopping at the first line or stanza, puzzling over words and syntax, and giving up because one expects to understand at once. A poem's unity, and the feeling or experience beneath it, is in the whole, almost never in the first line or stanza; many poems that look simple are complex underneath.
- **Second, read it through again, aloud.** Speaking forces understanding: you cannot slide over a phrase you do not understand, the ear catches wrong emphasis the eye misses, and rhythm and rhyme put the stress where it belongs. Reading aloud also opens the reader to the poem.
- **These two rules come before everything else.** Once the reader has even a vague sense of the unity, they can start asking questions.
- **Ask rhetorical questions, not logical ones.** Questions of exposition are grammatical and logical; questions of a lyric are mostly rhetorical, sometimes syntactical. The reader does not come to terms with a poem but must find its key words by ear: which words stand out, and why? Rhythm, rhyme, repetition? Do several stanzas treat the same ideas, and do those ideas form a sequence?
- **Look for the conflict.** Most good lyrics turn on a conflict. Sometimes two opposing people, images, or ideas are named and their conflict described; often it is only implied, such as memory against forgetting, home against exile, or youth against age, even when those words never appear. A title may invoke the conflict by alluding to another poem.
- **The parallel rules apply loosely.** They fit a lyric to the extent it has a narrative line, and less closely otherwise; but every lyric presents a concrete experience with a beginning, middle, and end, and has at least one character, the speaker.
- **Expect rich ambiguity.** The best poetry may be the most ambiguous, and some of it deliberately so.
- **Do not overrate background knowledge.** Readers often think they must know more about the poet and the times than they do, and lean on commentary and biography out of self-doubt. Knowing the context can help, but a mass of it does not guarantee understanding. Almost anyone can read almost any poem by working on it, reading it over and over.
- **Return to great lyrics.** They repay many visits, and between visits the reader may learn more about them than they realise.
- **To begin reading lyric poetry,** start with a good anthology (older ones supplemented by recent ones) rather than one poet's collected works, and a guide that discusses how to read well-known poems.

**Buddy as reader.** When discussing a story, play, or poem, state the plot's unity as a short narration, place a passage by where it falls in the plot, and give interpretations as readings the text supports, with the strongest alternatives, rather than as claims the author makes (adaptation, combining these rules with the grounding rules). Keep judgments of taste separate from judgments about the work, and name what in the work supports each.

## Questions to put to the learner
- What kind of work is this, and what does it ask of you as a reader?
- Are you letting it work on you, or resisting it?
- Tell the plot in two sentences. Where does it really begin? What are the crises, the climax, and what comes after?
- Who are the main characters, and which incidents matter to the plot?
- What is this world like? Could this have happened in it?
- For a play: how would you stage this scene? How should this line be spoken? What do you decide where the text is silent?
- For a poem: have you read it through, then aloud? Which words stand out, and why? What conflict does it turn on? Who is speaking?
- Have you appreciated what the author was trying to make you feel before judging it?
- What do you like or dislike, and why? What in the work causes that? Is your liking private, or would most readers share it?
