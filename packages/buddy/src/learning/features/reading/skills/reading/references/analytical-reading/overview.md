# Analytical Reading: Overview

Analytical reading is thorough reading of one text for understanding: the best reading possible when time is not the limit. The reader asks many ordered questions and works at the text until it is their own. It is always intensely active, and more or less demanding depending on the text. It is rarely needed when the goal is information or entertainment, and moving from understanding less to understanding more with the help of a text is almost impossible without it.

The rules are stated for whole books because a long, difficult book poses the hardest problems. They apply to articles, papers, chapters, and reports in the same field, adapted where they do not fit in the same way. As stated here they are for exposition (texts that convey knowledge); imaginative literature has parallel rules in [../kinds/imaginative-literature.md](../kinds/imaginative-literature.md), and each kind of exposition shifts them (see [../kinds/](../kinds/)).

## The fifteen rules
**Stage 1. Structure: finding what the text is about.** See [1-structure.md](1-structure.md).
1. Classify the text by its kind and its subject matter.
2. State what the whole text is about as briefly as you can.
3. List its major parts in order, show how they relate to each other and to the whole, and outline each part the same way.
4. State the question or questions the author set out to answer.

**Stage 2. Interpretation: finding what the text says.** See [2-interpretation.md](2-interpretation.md).
5. Find the author's key words and settle what the author means by each.
6. Find the most important sentences and draw out the claims they make.
7. Find the author's arguments in, or assemble them from, sequences of sentences.
8. Decide which of the author's questions were answered and which were not, and which failures the author recognised.

**Stage 3. Criticism: judging the text as a claim to knowledge.** See [3-criticism.md](3-criticism.md).
General maxims for fair criticism:
9. Finish the outline and the interpretation before criticising; be able to say "I understand" before agreeing, disagreeing, or suspending judgment.
10. Disagree reasonably, not to win or to quarrel.
11. Give reasons for every critical judgment, to show you know the difference between knowledge and personal opinion.

The specific grounds for adverse criticism:
12. Show where the author lacks relevant knowledge (uninformed).
13. Show where the author asserts what is not so (misinformed).
14. Show where the author's reasoning fails (illogical).
15. Show where the author's analysis or account is incomplete.

## How the stages relate
- **The stages answer the four questions.** Stage 1 answers most of "What is it about as a whole?"; stage 2 answers "What does it say in detail, and how?"; stage 3 answers "Is it true?" and "Why does it matter?". The answer to the first question improves as the later rules are applied.
- **Structure and interpretation meet.** Outlining works down from the whole to its parts to the points made in each part; interpretation works up from words to claims to arguments. The points at the bottom of the outline are the claims and arguments at the top of the interpretation. When both are done, you know what the text contains.
- **The stages end in a chain.** Stage 1 ends with the author's questions (Rule 4); stage 2 ends with which of them were answered (Rule 8); stage 3 ends with how complete the account is (Rule 15), which judges both how well the questions were posed and how well they were answered.
- **Each stage has its art.** Grammar and logic govern the first two stages: dealing with the words, and reaching the thought through them. Rhetoric governs the third: knowing how to respond to someone trying to convince or persuade you.
- **The stages are not separate passes for a practised reader.** Only a beginner needs to read once for structure and again for interpretation; a practised reader does both at once, and a good inspection does some of the work in advance. Criticism is the exception: it always comes last, for experts and beginners alike.

## How closely to apply them
The rules describe an ideal to approximate. Even the best readers apply them closely to only a few texts and roughly to most; the closeness depends on the text and the reader's purpose. The rules stay the same either way, and a good reader could apply them closely if asked. A reader is good to the degree they approach the ideal, and the ideal is approached by reading single texts well, not by skimming many.

**Buddy as reader.** Run the stages the request needs, silently, before answering. A request to explain a passage still needs enough structure to know what the passage is doing in the whole, and a request to judge a text needs stages 1 and 2 done first. Show the learner the results with locations, not the procedure (adaptation).

**Buddy as coach.** Take the learner through the stages in order, one move at a time, and let them do each move before you check it against the text. The rules are many because the one skill is complex; the learner learns the acts separately until they merge into one habit (see [../study.md](../study.md)).
