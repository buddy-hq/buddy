# Science And History Domain Placement

## What This Reference Solves

Teachers and designers often need to place science, history, and similar non-math, non-literacy material at an appropriate **depth** for a learner. The local corpus does **not** provide a reliable K–12 topic calendar (“teach the American Revolution in grade 5”). It **does** support placement by **prior knowledge**, **disciplinary prerequisites**, **misconception patterns**, **evidence and representation demands**, and **grade-band cognitive and language constraints**—then choosing how far to take a topic on a depth ladder.

This handbook is about **what material at what depth**, not which teaching method, activity format, or resource type to use.

## Domains And Bands Covered

| Domain family | Examples | Grade-band use |
|---------------|----------|----------------|
| History and social studies | Evidence, change, causation, accounts vs. past, significance | K–12; depth tied to **disciplinary idea progressions**, not topic lists |
| Science | Inquiry, models, conceptual change topics (e.g., force, light, ecosystems) | K–12; **topic choice is weakly tied to grade** in the corpus |
| Other weak-map domains | Geography, civics, interdisciplinary humanities | Same placement logic as history/science unless a stronger reference applies |

**School bands (K–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–12)** matter mainly as **constraints** on language, abstraction, working memory, and task complexity—not as automatic topic assignments.

## What The Corpus Can And Cannot Say

### Can say (medium–high confidence for placement logic)

- Students arrive with **everyday ideas** that help or hinder discipline-specific learning; instruction should start from those ideas, not assume empty minds.
- **History** has no agreed K–12 topic sequence in the sources; placement should follow **progressions of disciplinary understanding** (e.g., how students think about evidence, accounts, change, significance)—not a national scope chart.
- **Science** topics in exemplar curricula are **not** fixed by grade; the same topic (e.g., light) could fit multiple levels; topics were chosen to illustrate learning principles, not to define when to teach them.
- **Mathematics** in the same source volume *does* follow a more predictable grade-linked conceptual sequence (whole number → rational number → functions); science/history lack that parity—see confidence section below.
- **Misconceptions** are topic-specific; teachers need **diagnostics for the topic at hand**, not age alone.
- **Depth** should match whether the learner can meet **evidence**, **explanation**, and **representation** demands at that level—not whether a textbook lists the topic for that grade.
- **Grade-band developmental trends** (language, memory, abstraction) cap how far language and task complexity can go, with **large within-grade variance**; age labels are not readiness proof.

### Cannot say (low confidence—do not invent)

- A unified **science or history scope-and-sequence** by age or grade.
- That a topic “belongs” in a grade because it appears in a common textbook or standards list, without checking **learner ideas and prerequisites** for that topic.
- That **seventh graders automatically** think differently than **fourth graders** about history; idea change requires **instruction and problems that expose limits of everyday thinking**, not birthday alone.
- Exact **age-to-topic maps** for science units (e.g., “genetics only in high school”) from this corpus alone.

When the question is “what topic at what age for the whole subject?”, route to **`scope-sequence-limits.md`** and narrow the question by domain, diagnostic, or external curriculum/standards.

## Placement Logic: Prior Knowledge, Misconceptions, Depth

### 1. Start from learner ideas, not grade labels

Placement begins with what the learner **already treats as true** about the domain:

- **Substantive ideas** (e.g., towns “grow” like trees; heavy objects fall faster; past events are directly “seen” through sources).
- **Disciplinary ideas** (e.g., history as memorized facts vs. evidence-based accounts; science as true/false statements vs. models tested against observation).

Everyday ideas that work in daily life often **fail in the discipline**; they are not always visible without tasks that surface them. Grade may suggest **common** ideas in a population, but **individual readiness varies widely** (including cases where younger students outperform older peers on disciplinary reasoning).

### 2. Identify prerequisites for the *task*, not the whole unit

Separate:

- **Ideas required for this lesson’s goal** (e.g., distinguishing account from past event).
- **Ideas that are wrong but irrelevant today** (e.g., minor geographic errors while working on evidence use).

Do not block placement on fixing every misconception; **match depth to the disciplinary goal of the material**.

### 3. Choose depth on a five-level ladder

Use the **deepest level the learner can sustain** given prerequisites, misconceptions, and band constraints—not the grade where the topic often appears.

| Level | Material focus | Typical learner state | History example (generic) | Science example (generic) |
|-------|----------------|----------------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **1. Concrete phenomenon / source observation** | Observable features, simple sources, limited interpretation | Everyday concepts dominate; little disciplinary vocabulary | Describe what a photograph or artifact shows; notice it was made later than the event | Observe shadows, water taste, or motion; describe without full theory |
| **2. Guided concept building** | Connect observations to emerging disciplinary terms; structured questions | Some relevant vocabulary; misconceptions still active but addressable | Compare two short accounts of the “same” event; ask what differs and why that might be | Compare predictions before/after a demo; revise ideas with feedback |
| **3. Explanation from evidence** | Warranted claims using discipline-appropriate evidence rules | Can use evidence (not only testimony or single authority) with support | Explain a claim about a past change using named sources and limits of each source | Explain an outcome using a model or principle, not only “because it happened” |
| **4. Comparison of accounts / models** | Multiple explanations, criteria for judging among them | Recognizes accounts/models as constructed; can compare using explicit criteria | Compare two historians’ arguments; discuss significance, bias, and question-fit—not “which is lying” | Compare two models’ predictions; discuss which fits data and what would falsify |
| **5. Transfer to a new case** | Apply disciplinary structure to unfamiliar topic or data | Stable disciplinary ideas across topics; metacognitive monitoring of own reasoning | Apply evidence/significance rules to a new time period or controversy | Apply same principle (e.g., force pairs, variable control) in a novel context |

**Move down the ladder** when diagnostics show missing prerequisites, persistent misconceptions that block the goal, or language/representation overload.

**Move up** when the learner can explain and justify at the current level on a **new** prompt without heavy scaffolding.

### 4. Layer grade-band constraints (Ormrod-style)

After depth is chosen, check **band limits**—these rarely pick the topic, but they cap **how** material is expressed:

| Band | Placement-relevant constraints (paraphrased trends) |
|------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| **K–2** | Limited vocabulary; literal language; short complex sentences; concrete referents; partial word knowledge |
| **3–5** | Growing narrative and cause language; still uneven abstract vocabulary; increasing but incomplete perspective-taking in explanations |
| **6–8** | More sustained argument and text; abstract ideas need concrete anchors; wide spread in formal reasoning |
| **9–12** | Greater capacity for hypothetical and abstract disciplinary talk; still requires explicit scaffolding for expert-like evidence and model use |

**Rules of thumb:** tie abstract disciplinary concepts to concrete cases; expect **large within-class spread**; never infer readiness from age or grade alone.

### 5. History-specific placement anchors

History placement is driven by **second-order (disciplinary) progressions** more than by which century is “due”:

- **Evidence:** from “past as given” → testimony/truthfulness → evidence that answers questions no witness intended.
- **Accounts:** from “stories about the same thing told differently” → author purpose, question-fit, and legitimate differing conclusions.
- **Change:** from automatic “progress” → direction, pace, and tradeoffs across themes.
- **Substantive concepts** (state, trade, rights): numerous; diagnose per topic.

**Topic sequence is weak in the corpus**; **idea sequence is strong**. Plan material so core disciplinary ideas develop across units even when factual settings change.

### 6. Science-specific placement anchors

Science placement is driven by **conceptual structure and misconception research for the topic**:

- Learners build intuitive physics/biology from experience; **misconceptions often persist** after correct test answers unless probed later.
- New concepts often require **conceptual change**, not only adding facts—placement may need **bridging** from partially correct intuitions, not jumping to formal laws.
- **Inquiry depth** (hypothesis, designed test, evidence vs. opinion) is a separate axis from vocabulary difficulty—a younger learner may investigate concretely while an older learner still treats evidence as “what I already know.”

**Topic–grade alignment in U.S. curricula is explicitly unreliable in the corpus**; use **phenomenon complexity**, **model abstraction**, and **typical misconception profiles** for the topic.

## Readiness Checks

Run **short, topic-specific diagnostics** before locking depth. Grade band informs question wording, not the construct being tested.

### Prior knowledge

- Ask for an **untaught explanation** of the phenomenon or question (oral or written).
- Map responses to: everyday only, mixed everyday/discipline, partial disciplinary, disciplinary.

**Pass for level 3+:** uses relevant disciplinary distinctions (e.g., source vs. account; variable vs. story) even if imperfect.

### Vocabulary and language

- Can the learner understand **critical terms** in context (not glossary-only)?
- For text-heavy material, cross-check **`grade-band-language-and-text-complexity.md`** if sentence length or academic vocabulary may block access.

**Misplacement signal:** correct oral reasoning but failure when reading dense source text → lower text demand or teach terms, not necessarily lower conceptual depth.

### Evidence use

| Domain | Quick check |
|--------|-------------|
| History | “How could we know this if no one was there?” / “What does this source show about the event vs. about when it was made?” |
| Science | “What would you observe to test that?” / “What would count against your idea?” |

**Misplacement signal:** treats sources as complete truth or rejects history/science as “just guessing” → stay at levels 1–2 until evidence concept develops.

### Misconception probe

- Use **one known problematic idea** for the topic (from research summaries or prior class work).
- Prefer tasks that **conflict** with the misconception, not only ask “what do you believe?”

**Misplacement signal:** misconception drives the whole explanation → level 2 with targeted bridging, or level 1 if affective/identity blocks engagement.

### Representation

- Can the learner work in the **representations required** (maps, graphs, models, primary excerpts, multi-step written argument)?

**Misplacement signal:** failure only on formal representation while oral reasoning is sound → adjust representation, not necessarily conceptual level.

## Generic Placement Examples (Copyright-Safe)

These illustrate **logic only**; they are not lesson plans.

1. **History, upper elementary:** Goal is “why two textbooks disagree on when an empire ended.” Diagnostic shows students think one book must be wrong. **Place at level 2–3:** compare what each account emphasizes (different questions), not full historiography debate. **Do not** require level 4 until evidence vs. testimony is stable.

2. **History, secondary:** Same disciplinary goal as above, but learners already distinguish account from past and use evidence language. **Place at level 4:** compare criteria historians use (periodization, significance). **Grade label** secondary is insufficient if diagnostics show level-2 thinking.

3. **Science, middle years:** Unit on forces. Diagnostic shows “only active agents push.” **Place at level 2** with phenomena that challenge passive-force ideas before formal symbolic level 3. **Do not** assign level 5 transfer until level 3 explanations are stable on novel scenarios.

4. **Science, secondary:** Genetics topic listed in syllabus, but learners lack variable control and model language from prior inquiry. **Lower depth** to guided investigation and evidence rules even if grade band is high.

5. **Cross-domain:** Learner reads at grade but cannot sustain written argument. **Keep conceptual depth** if oral diagnostic passes; **reduce text load** and cross-reference language reference—not automatic topic delay.

|------|------------|-------|

## Common Misplacement

| Error | Why it fails | Correction |
|-------|--------------|------------|
| Choosing topic by grade-level pacing chart only | Isolated objectives miss conceptual networks; history has no shared sequence in sources | Diagnose ideas; use depth ladder |
| Assuming grade = disciplinary level | Age does not automatically shift evidence/model thinking | Use readiness checks |
| Teaching facts before disciplinary access | Students memorize without knowing how claims are warranted | Place level 1–2 work on sources/models before coverage breadth |
| Max depth because “honors” or older students | Overloads representation or evidence demands | Highest sustainable level on diagnostic |
| Delaying topic because of unrelated misconception | Blocks progress on relevant goals | Fix or park irrelevant ideas; teach to task |
| Treating science topic order as fixed | Corpus treats topics as illustrative, not prescriptive | Place by phenomenon/model/misconception profile |

## Handling Uneven Readiness

- **Ahead on disciplinary ideas, behind on language:** Keep depth; simplify text and vocabulary supports.
- **Ahead on facts, weak on evidence/models:** Do not advance depth; add level 2–3 disciplinary tasks.
- **Wide spread in one class:** Tier **depth of product** (e.g., compare two accounts with more or less scaffolding), not always different topics.
- **Out-of-school knowledge:** Treat as asset for level 1–2 phenomena; still check disciplinary misconceptions.

## Next Material

After diagnostics show the learner can sustain the **current** depth on a **new** prompt without heavy scaffolding, advance **one level** on the depth ladder—not to the next grade topic.

| From level | Typical next material (same topic or parallel phenomenon) | Gate before advancing |
|------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **1 → 2** | Structured comparison or guided questions with emerging disciplinary terms; a second source or contrasting observation | Accurate description of sources/phenomena; willingness to revise after a conflicting task |
| **2 → 3** | Warranted explanation: claims tied to named evidence, source limits, or model-based prediction | Uses relevant disciplinary distinctions (account vs. past; variable vs. story) even if imperfect; blocking misconception no longer drives the whole explanation |
| **3 → 4** | Compare two accounts or models with explicit criteria (question-fit, significance, falsification, bias) | Stable level-3 explanation on at least one **novel** prompt; representation load manageable |
| **4 → 5** | Apply the same disciplinary structure to an unfamiliar case or controversy with reduced scaffolding | Compares using criteria without defaulting to single-authority truth or “which source is lying” |

If diagnostics **fail** at the target level, **next material** is usually **more work at the same or lower level** (narrower phenomenon, shorter texts, one targeted bridging task for the blocking misconception)—not a jump to the next textbook unit.

When the question is **which topic comes next in the year**, this handbook does not answer that; use external curriculum/standards and **`scope-sequence-limits.md`**. Here, “next” means **next depth step within a chosen topic**.

## Misuse And Overclaim Warnings

- Do not present **grade bands as topic assignments** for science or history.
- Do not claim **“students this age cannot”** without a topic-specific diagnostic; cite band limits as **tendencies**, not bans.
- Do not copy **case study topics, lesson structures, or distinctive classroom narratives** from source volumes; use the placement logic only.
- Do not substitute this file for **standards documents or district scope charts** when legal or policy alignment is required—use those externally and apply this handbook for depth and readiness.
- Do not fold **teaching method** (debate, inquiry lab, project) into placement decisions; method follows depth and goal, not the reverse.

## Completion Check

For a proposed science/history/domain topic, you should be able to answer:

1. **What depth level (1–5)** fits the learner’s current disciplinary thinking on a short diagnostic?
2. **Which prerequisites and misconceptions** matter for *this* goal—not the whole subject?
3. **Which band constraints** cap language, abstraction, and representation?
4. **How confident** can you be without a formal curriculum map—and when must you defer to **`scope-sequence-limits.md`** or external standards?
