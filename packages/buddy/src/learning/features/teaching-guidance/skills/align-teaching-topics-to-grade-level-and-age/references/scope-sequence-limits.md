# Scope, Sequence, And Confidence Limits

## What This Reference Solves

Teachers and instructional designers often ask for a single map of **what to teach at every age or grade** across subjects: a K–12 scope-and-sequence, a pacing guide, or an age-to-topic chart. The local research corpus does **not** contain one authoritative, all-subject chart. It **does** contain strong guidance on **how to place material without fake precision**: use grade-band learner profiles, domain-specific diagnostic progressions, coherent knowledge sequences (especially literacy), and readiness checks—while treating isolated procedural scope charts as a design risk, not a gold standard.

Use this handbook when:

- the question is broad (“what should a 7-year-old learn?” “give me the K–12 sequence for science”);
- the domain is weakly mapped in local sources (most science/history topic timing);
- a proposed placement sounds like an exact age claim without domain evidence;
- you need to decide **which specialized reference** can answer confidently, or **what to do instead** when none can.

This file is a **guardrail and router**, not a substitute curriculum.

## What Ages, Grades, And Domains It Covers

- **Grades and bands:** preschool through grade 12, commonly framed as K–2, 3–5, 6–8, and 9–12 when sources use school bands rather than single ages.
- **Domains:** all subjects at the level of **meta-placement**; substantive topic placement is delegated to domain references listed below.
- **Not covered here:** lesson design, teaching models, worksheet authoring, or full curriculum alignment systems—those belong in other skill families (see [Boundaries](#boundaries-material-placement-vs-other-work)).

## Source Tradition

Three ideas recur across the strongest local sources:

1. **Networks, not rutted paths** (*How People Learn*, design of learning environments): curricula organized as long lists of procedural objectives by grade often fail to help learners see how ideas connect. What matters is the **conceptual network**—where the learner is in the landscape and how pieces relate—not mastery of isolated objectives on a chart.
2. **Domain-specific progressions and diagnostics** (*How Students Learn*): mathematics offers relatively stable K–12 conceptual shifts (whole number → rational number → functions) and, for early whole number, a **diagnostic developmental pathway** (Number Knowledge levels tied to roughly ages 4, 6, and 8). History has **no agreed K–12 topic sequence** in the corpus; science topic order is **less predictable than mathematics**, with topics often chosen to illustrate learning principles rather than to mirror a standard spine.
3. **Coherent content sequences vs. skills-only literacy** (*The Knowledge Gap*): literacy and comprehension depend on **background knowledge and vocabulary built over time**, which argues for **content-focused, grade-linked curriculum**—not a universal age chart invented from general development alone. The corpus supports the **logic** of sequenced knowledge; it does **not** supply a complete, authoritative grade-by-grade topic list for all subjects.

## Supported Answer Types

Answer confidently (with the right reference) when the user needs:

- **Grade-band traits** that change material choice: language load, memory demand, metacognitive expectations, social/moral context (K–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–12).
- **Early number diagnostic placement**: where a child sits on a whole-number developmental pathway and what number material is sensible **next**, including typical misunderstandings for that level.
- **Domain math progressions**: whether an idea is introductory (surface), consolidating (deep), or transfer-level for a band; what earlier grade-level foundations matter (e.g., solid whole-number division understanding before treating fraction division as routine procedure).
- **Literacy and knowledge sequencing logic**: prioritize building vocabulary and background knowledge in a **coherent sequence**; judge text/task demand against what the learner has already studied—not against a generic “reading level” alone.
- **Readiness and prerequisites**: representational capacity, executive demand, language, play/concrete needs, zone of proximal development—before committing to a topic.
- **Scaling an already-chosen topic** for a band: abstraction, independence, example density, task complexity—without inventing a full subject scope.

For each supported case, answer in this shape:

1. **Placement:** what material or depth fits the requested age, grade, or band.
2. **Why:** progression logic, band traits, or diagnostic result—not age alone.
3. **Prerequisite check:** one small probe to verify readiness.
4. **Confidence:** high, medium, or low, with limits stated.
5. **Next material:** the likely next step if the learner succeeds.

## Unsupported Answer Types

Do **not** present the following as corpus-grounded unless tied to an external standards document or curriculum the user supplies:

| Unsupported claim | Why it fails | What to say instead |
|-------------------|--------------|---------------------|
| **Unified all-subject K–12 scope-and-sequence** | No grep-rich master chart; HPL warns against isolated procedural scope charts divorced from conceptual networks | Offer domain routing + diagnostics; cite need for adopted standards or curriculum |
| **Exact age → topic** (“all 9-year-olds should learn X”) | Wide within-grade variation; age bands are planning tools, not ceilings | Use band + diagnostic; cite Number Knowledge only for early whole number |
| **Science / history age maps** | HSL: science sequence “far less predictable”; history has “no agreed-upon sequence” for K–12 topics | Use conceptual progression on a **chosen** topic; see `science-history-domain-placement.md` |
| **Complete literacy/history/social studies topic calendar** | Knowledge Gap argues for coherent content curriculum but corpus does not reproduce a full sequence | Principles + readiness; point to school’s Core Knowledge / state framework / EngageNY-style materials if user needs lists |
| **Pedagogy-only “what topic when”** | Visible Learning math emphasizes surface/deep/transfer and progression **mechanism**, not a topic spine | Route to `mathematics-grade-level-progressions.md` or teaching-models for **how** to teach |
| **Rigid developmental ceilings** | HPL: young learners can show sophisticated thinking **when they have the knowledge** to support it | Check prerequisites and knowledge, not age alone |

## Confidence Labels

Use explicit confidence on every placement answer:

- **High:** source gives grade-band expectations, a named diagnostic level, or a well-documented domain progression for the specific skill (e.g., early whole number; Ormrod band traits for format/load).
- **Medium:** sources give band traits or progression logic but not exact topic timing (most secondary math depth; literacy stretch; adolescent argument demand).
- **Low:** only general principles or discipline warnings; **do not** invent a sequence—run a diagnostic or require an external scope.

## When Confidence Is Low: Response Protocol

Work through these steps in order; stop when placement is actionable.

1. **Narrow the domain.** Replace “what should a 10-year-old learn?” with “what number / literacy / history / science material for this goal?”
2. **Open the matching reference** from the table above (usually one file, at most two).
3. **Run a short diagnostic** instead of guessing from age:
   - **Early number:** oral Number Knowledge–style items (counting, more/less, simple stories, how they know).
   - **Math beyond early number:** a few problems that reveal whether the issue is surface skill, missing concept, or transfer (similar vs. different problem types).
   - **Literacy:** vocabulary and background on the **topic** of the text, not just word-count or “grade level”; can they explain key ideas after a supported read?
   - **History/science:** probe prior conceptions and whether they can explain a phenomenon or source—not calendar age.
4. **Separate readiness from topic choice.** If development/readiness is the bottleneck, use `developmental-readiness-prerequisites.md` before selecting content.
5. **Name the limit and the external source needed** when the user wants a full scope: state standards, district framework, adopted curriculum (e.g., content-rich literacy materials), or international progression documents—not this corpus alone.
6. **Avoid fake precision.** Prefer “typical for this band, verify with …” over definitive age-to-topic lists.

## Warnings Against Common Misuse

### Isolated scope-and-sequence charts

Traditional scope-and-sequence charts list procedural objectives by grade. An individual objective may be fine, but treating the chart as the **whole curriculum** trains routines without integrated understanding. Prefer:

- knowing **where the learner is** in a conceptual network;
- introducing ideas when learners see **why** they need them;
- balancing understanding with **automaticity** where needed (e.g., literacy and calculation fluency).

Do not reject all sequencing—**coherent domain and knowledge sequences** matter—but reject **disconnected** procedural lists presented as complete answers to “what to teach when.”

### Rigid age ceilings and floors

Grade and age bands help plan for **typical** classrooms; they do not forbid advanced material when prerequisites and knowledge are in place, and they do not guarantee readiness because a child “is in fourth grade.” *How Students Learn* emphasizes wide variation in preschool and primary grades while teachers still set **grade-level objectives**—use norms for planning, diagnostics for individuals.

### Confusing “level” with “topic”

Visible Learning for Mathematics stresses **surface, deep, and transfer** phases within topics and **progression across grades**—not a substitute for a user’s district topic list. A fifth grader might be in surface learning on one topic and transfer on another in the same year.

### Literacy as skills-only

Skills-focused elementary literacy (comprehension strategies without coherent content) is criticized in *The Knowledge Gap* as leaving teachers to “teach nothing in particular.” Placement for reading should include **what knowledge the text assumes**, not only whether the child can “find the main idea.”

## Learners Ahead, Behind, Or Uneven

- **Ahead on a topic:** do not skip the conceptual network; add depth, transfer, or connection—not only the next grade’s topic list. Check whether “fast” performance is procedural without understanding (HPL assessment caution).
- **Behind:** diagnose **which prerequisite** in the domain pathway is missing; avoid repeating an entire lower grade’s scope chart.
- **Uneven across subjects:** normal; route **per domain** rather than a single age label.
- **Uneven within a subject:** common in early number; use diagnostic levels, not birth date alone.

## Misplacement Patterns To Flag

| Pattern | Risk | Correction |
|---------|------|------------|
| Quoting a scope chart without conceptual links | Rote coverage | Teach within a network; use formative probes |
| Age = grade = topic | Fake precision | Diagnostic + domain ref |
| “Reading level” without content knowledge | Incomprehensible stretch text | Knowledge sequencing + vocabulary check |
| Skipping early number levels | Fragile algorithms | `early-number-knowledge.md` |
| Encyclopedic breadth in upper grades | Superficial coverage (Knowledge Gap critique of overstuffed texts) | Fewer topics, deeper treatment |
| Denying useful domain answers because no universal map exists | Over-blocking | Use domain refs with stated limits |

## Completion Check For Teachers

Before answering “can this age/level placement question be answered from the corpus?”:

1. Is it **band/profile**, **domain progression**, **literacy knowledge**, **diagnostic level**, or **readiness**? → Route; answer with stated confidence.
2. Is it a **full multi-subject scope** or **exact age-to-topic** without domain evidence? → **No**; state limit; name external curriculum/standards; offer diagnostic.
3. Is it **science/history topic calendar**? → **Low confidence**; use domain placement ref for principles on a chosen topic only.
4. Did you separate **what material** from **how to teach** and from **curriculum policy**?
