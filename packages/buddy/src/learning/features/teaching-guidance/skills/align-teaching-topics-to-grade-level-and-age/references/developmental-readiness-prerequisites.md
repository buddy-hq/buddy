# Developmental Readiness Prerequisites

## What This Reference Solves

Teachers and instructional designers need to decide whether a learner is ready for a **kind** of material—not only a topic label. This handbook supports placement for:

- **Representation:** physical exploration, iconic/visual models, symbols, purely verbal or abstract formulations
- **Task demand:** short vs extended work, guided vs independent production, play- or experience-based vs disembedded verbal reasoning
- **Cognitive load:** how much must be held in mind, generalized from examples, or reasoned about hypothetically
- **Support level:** what evidence shows the learner is in reach with scaffolding vs needs prerequisite material first

**Core question:** Is this learner ready for this type of material, what prerequisite evidence should be checked, and what support would move the material into reach?

**Confidence:** Medium overall. Local sources give strong principles and illustrative age-linked patterns, but they do not support precise age-to-topic prescriptions. Use diagnostics over calendar age.

## Coverage

- **Ages and bands:** infancy through adolescence and early adulthood, with emphasis on when representational and abstraction demands typically shift
- **Domains:** domain-neutral readiness logic; pair with subject references (math, literacy, science/history) for topic-specific progressions
- **Not covered here:** grade-band wording, formatting, or task-length defaults → `grade-band-material-leveling.md` and `grade-band-language-and-text-complexity.md`; full adolescent disciplinary depth → `adolescent-secondary-material-depth.md`; scope-chart skepticism → `scope-sequence-limits.md`

## Source Tradition

Guidance synthesizes constructivist and sociocultural developmental theory as applied to teaching: Piagetian stage insights (used cautiously), Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development (ZPD), Bruner’s spiral curriculum and scaffolding, Donaldson’s embedded vs disembedded thinking, Erikson’s psychosocial tasks where they change what material feels safe or motivating, and information-processing views of representation, memory, executive function, and individual differences (Bjorklund; Feldman; Rathus-style adolescent summaries).

## Readiness Principles For Material Placement

### 1. Readiness Is About Support And Representation, Not Permission

A learner who is not yet independent on a demand is often **near readiness** in the ZPD: able to participate with help that extends what they already grasp. Placement should ask “what representation and support make this reachable?”—not “is this age allowed?”

Vygotsky’s ZPD is the gap between **actual development** (what the learner manages alone now) and **potential development** (what they can do with appropriate help now). Effective help is a **supported stretch** built on current competence—not a leap to an adult-chosen goal that shares no reference point with the learner’s experience.

### 2. Start From Competence, Not Deficit Labels

Stage language is useful for anticipating common bottlenecks, but classic deficit framing (“cannot yet do X at age Y”) has been challenged by observational and methodological research. Young children often show sophisticated reasoning in meaningful contexts (play, familiar stories, hands-on tasks) that disappears in decontextualized tests. Material should be placed to **surface competence** first, then extend.

### 3. Embedded Before Disembedded

**Embedded thinking** ties ideas to familiar actions, objects, stories, or images the learner can manipulate or visualize. **Disembedded thinking** handles concepts without those anchors—typical of much school discourse.

Learners increasingly manage disembedded material when underlying concepts are already solid in embedded form. Pushing abstract verbal formulations before that grounding tends to produce **compliant guessing** (learners infer what the adult wants) rather than understanding.

### 4. Specific-To-General Reasoning Is Common In Childhood

Many school tasks assume general rules applied to cases. Young learners often reason from **specific, familiar examples** toward generalizations (e.g., safety rules make sense after kerbside practice with meaning, not before). Material that jumps to general principles without linked cases is often misplaced even when vocabulary sounds simple.

### 5. Spiral, Don’t One-Shot

The **spiral curriculum** expects the same ideas and resource types to return across years with **qualitatively different use** as cognition matures (e.g., blocks for sensory exploration → construction → measurement). “Already covered” at a shallow pass does not mean ready for a deeper representational demand; it may mean ready for the **next pass** with less scaffolding.

### 6. Individual And Within-Learner Variability Are Diagnostic Data

Between-learner differences and **same learner, different task** differences are normal. Uneven profiles (strong representation in one domain, weak language for that domain) are placement signals, not anomalies to average away.

### 7. Psychosocial Readiness Affects Engagement, Not Cognitive Possibility

Erikson’s dilemmas (trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity) describe recurring tensions that shape willingness to risk, persist, or accept correction. They do not set cognitive ceilings, but they matter for whether material feels threatening, shame-inducing, or worth effort—especially for autonomy-heavy tasks (2–3), initiative/play-rich tasks (4–5), competence-visible tasks (middle childhood), and identity-linked tasks (adolescence).

## Age-Linked Patterns (Tendencies, Not Ceilings)

Use these as **hypotheses to test**, not cutoffs. Wide variation is documented (e.g., play–language links, naming explosion, formal operations).

| Band (approx.) | Representational tendency | Typical material fit | Common misplacement |
|----------------|---------------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Infancy (~0–18 mo) | Sensorimotor schemas; object exploration through action and senses | Hands-on, immediate feedback, caregiver-mediated routines | Symbol-heavy explanations; multi-step verbal instructions |
| Late infancy–toddler (~18–36 mo) | Emerging symbols; words tied to familiar categories; pretend play grows with language | Named objects, simple pretend, one-step requests, parallel adult narration | Abstract categories without perceptual anchors; long verbal chains |
| Preschool (~2–6) | Symbolic function expands; egocentric *cognitive* perspective (difficulty taking another’s view); strong embedded reasoning | Stories, play-based models, manipulatives, familiar contexts, short cycles | Disembedded logic puzzles; “trick” questions; assessment that rewards guessing |
| Early school (~5–8) | Shift toward operations on concrete quantities and categories; growing but uneven disembedding | Manipulatives → pictures → words; explicit bridges from experience to labels | Skipping manipulative/familiar image phase; purely verbal “word problems” without sense-making context |
| Middle childhood (~8–12) | Concrete operational strengths: conservation-like reasoning, classification, reversible thought on tangible content | Organized tasks on real objects/events; explicit variable control in hands-on science | Formal hypothetical proofs; identity-heavy social dilemmas without support |
| Adolescence (~11+) | Emerging formal operations in familiar domains: hypothetical-deductive reasoning, propositional logic, abstract symbols | Hypothesis testing, algebra-like symbols, argument from premises, perspective-rich scenarios | Assuming formal thought in *all* domains; ignoring egocentric social perspective biases; one-shot abstraction without domain experience |

**Adolescent nuance:** Formal operational capacities often emerge **gradually**, may be **domain-specific** (strong in practiced areas, weak in novel ones), and are **culturally and experientially conditioned**. Many adults use formal reasoning inconsistently. Adolescent **egocentrism** (imaginary audience, personal fable) affects how feedback and visibility land—not whether abstract material can be introduced with structure.

**Infancy and toddler placement:** Rows for ~0–36 months summarize representational tendencies only. For dimension-level diagnostics in play, routines, and caregiver-mediated tasks, use `early-childhood-foundations.md` before relying on the school-age checks below at full granularity.

## Prerequisite Dimensions And Diagnostic Checks

For each placement decision, scan the five dimensions below. A weakness in any one may block independent work but still allow **supported** placement in the ZPD.

### A. Representation

**What to verify:** Can the learner hold the idea in the proposed format—action, image, object, symbol, or pure language?

| Readiness signal | Quick diagnostic |
|------------------|------------------|
| Can act out or manipulate the situation | Give a physical or drawable model; ask them to show, not tell |
| Can use a stand-in (toy, picture, gesture) for the real thing | Ask them to explain using the model; note scale/confusion errors |
| Can use words/symbols for ideas they have experienced | Ask for a verbal summary of a **familiar** event before introducing new notation |
| Can reason with symbols detached from experience | Present a minimal symbolic task in a **practiced** domain; if failure, retreat one representation level |

**Misplacement:** Jumping to notation or definition before the learner can re-enact or picture the situation (common in early math language, science vocabulary, and history timelines without narrative anchor).

**If near readiness:** Keep the same concept; add manipulative, story, or worked example; require demonstration before verbal rule.

### B. Language And Communication

**What to verify:** Vocabulary, syntax, and discourse demands match what the learner can produce and parse in **this** topic—not only general oral fluency.

| Readiness signal | Quick diagnostic |
|------------------|------------------|
| Names familiar categories; asks “what’s that?” | Point labeling in context |
| Links play patterns to phrases (pretend + short utterances) | Observe play narration; note gap between play complexity and speech |
| Uses words for categories with over/under-extension | Correct gently; check if errors are logical extensions |
| Handles academic terms for taught concepts | Explain to a peer or younger child using taught terms only |

**Misplacement:** Long abstract definitions; questions with no shared referent; “test” questions where the adult already knows the answer (often produces plausible but empty answers in young children).

**If near readiness:** Comment on what they are doing; reduce questioning; build vocabulary from shared activity before reading-heavy exposition.

### C. Memory And Cognitive Load

**What to verify:** Can the learner hold the task elements in mind, retrieve relevant prior knowledge, and optionally monitor their own memory?

| Readiness signal | Quick diagnostic |
|------------------|------------------|
| Remembers routines and scripts for familiar events | Retell a familiar sequence; note missing middle steps |
| Recalls studied material with cues | Short delayed recall with one cue (not open-ended “everything”) |
| Uses simple strategies when prompted (rehearsal, grouping) | Think-aloud during a 3–5 item memory task |
| Shows metamemory (“I need to study X”) | Ask what part will be hardest and what they will do |

**Misplacement:** Multi-step instructions without external memory aids; large unstructured lists; assuming verbal recall of experiences encoded before the learner had words for them.

**If near readiness:** Externalize (lists, diagrams, numbered steps); shrink chunk size; teach one strategy explicitly; spiral back after sleep/time gap.

### D. Self-Regulation And Executive Function

**What to verify:** Impulse control, persistence, attention, and emotional tolerance match task length and independence level.

| Readiness signal | Quick diagnostic |
|------------------|------------------|
| Brief delay of gratification with support | Short waiting game or turn-taking with clear rules |
| Returns to task after distraction (with cue) | Interrupted activity; note need for re-orienting |
| Inhibits impulsive answer when asked to wait | “Think time” before sharing; compare first vs revised answer |
| Manages frustration without shutting down | Challenging puzzle just above comfort; observe recovery |

**Misplacement:** Long independent projects when the learner cannot yet inhibit immediate response or recover from error; high-stakes public correction during identity-sensitive periods.

**If near readiness:** Shorter segments; clearer stop rules; private feedback; predictable routines; “hot” vs “cool” task framing (emotionally loaded vs neutral practice).

### E. Abstraction And Logical Form

**What to verify:** Whether the task requires embedded reasoning, hypothetical reasoning, or control of variables.

| Readiness signal | Quick diagnostic |
|------------------|------------------|
| Reasons about familiar “what if” in story/play | Counterfactual in a known narrative (“What if the bus driver…?”) |
| Separates variables in concrete settings | Change one feature at a time in a hands-on comparison |
| Forms and tests a hypothesis | Predict → try → revise in a domain with some prior experience |
| Handles logic without concrete referents | Simple premise–conclusion in unfamiliar content (often late; domain-specific) |

**Misplacement:** Hypothetical-deductive or purely propositional tasks before concrete control-of-variables practice; causal questions about phenomena outside the learner’s knowledge base.

**If near readiness:** One variable at a time; structured experiments; explicit “start from what you know” prompts; accept concrete approaches before demanding formal proof language.

## ZPD And Scaffolding: Placement Rules

Use this sequence when material feels “too hard” or “too easy”:

1. **Establish actual level:** What can the learner do or explain **without** help on a close cousin of the target task?
2. **Name the stretch:** What single step beyond that is they keen but uncertain about?
3. **Choose scaffold type:** verbal hint, demonstration, simplified case, joint problem-solving, peer slightly ahead, environmental rearrangement, or real-world visit/experience
4. **Fade:** Remove the scaffold when performance stabilizes; keep spiral returns for transfer

**Placement implications:**

| Observation | Adjust material |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Success only with heavy hand-holding on every step | Material is above independent level; keep goal but shrink steps or enrich representation |
| Success alone but no transfer | May be rote; add novel surface, require explanation, or spiral prerequisite |
| Boredom, errors careless | May be below productive level; increase depth, hypothesis, or choice—not only speed |
| Distress or random guessing | Retreat to simpler version of **same** idea; do not push adult goal language |

**Scaffolding is not directing:** Observe first; extend the child’s line of inquiry; avoid over-questioning that blocks thinking. Apprenticeship-style participation (guided real tasks) can outperform decontextualized instruction when culture and safety allow.

**Peer and joint work:** Partners slightly ahead can support ZPD; sometimes pairs progress more when an adult is **not** immediately intervening—still design for safety and accountability.

## Adolescent-Specific Placement Notes

When sources support distinct adolescent patterns, check these in addition to the five dimensions:

- **Hypothetical thinking:** Can explore possibilities, ideals, and “what might be”—useful for planning, ethics, and science—but may run ahead of practical constraints or social nuance
- **Symbolic systems:** Algebra-like variables, formal argument, disciplinary notation—often learnable when tied to domains the learner has practiced
- **Egocentrism:** Imaginary audience and personal fable can inflate stakes of visibility and uniqueness; material with high public comparison or “you should already know this” framing may misfire even when cognition suffices
- **Identity and Erikson:** Exploration of roles and values can motivate disciplinary depth or derail it if material feels identity-threatening
- **Inconsistency:** Formal reasoning may appear in one class and vanish in another—diagnose per domain, not per grade label

Do **not** infer that adolescents uniformly think formally, or that lack of formal performance means inability ever—check experience, culture, motivation, and task familiarity.

## Handling Uneven, Advanced, Or Prerequisite-Gap Learners

| Profile | Placement move |
|---------|----------------|
| Advanced in one domain only | Match representation in **that** domain; do not assume transfer to unrelated symbolic demands |
| Strong orally, weak in symbols | Delay notation; strengthen embodied/image models |
| Strong symbols, weak concepts | Require explanation and novel application; restrict drill that masks gaps |
| Behind on prerequisites | Teach prerequisite in embedded form; avoid relabeling the same gap with harder vocabulary |
| Variable day-to-day | Stabilize load (sleep, stress, emotional safety); use shortest reliable diagnostic, not one snapshot |
| Older learner with gap | Same logic as younger gap: representation and support, not shame framing; spiral from secure base |

**Never conclude** “cannot learn this because of age/stage.” Conclude “not yet independent in this form; needs prerequisite X or scaffold Y.”

## Common Misplacement Patterns

- Treating stage labels as **forbidden content** lists
- Confusing **cognitive egocentrism** with moral selfishness
- Skipping **concrete/experiential** passes because the curriculum map says a grade owns the topic
- Using **verbal fluency** as proof of conceptual readiness
- Assigning **long independent** work when executive or emotional prerequisites are immature
- **One-shot** exposure without spiral return at higher representational demand
- **Abstract assessment** of ideas never encountered in embedded form
- Assuming **adolescents** reason formally in every subject because they do in one

## Confidence And Limits

| Claim type | Confidence |
|------------|------------|
| ZPD, scaffolding, spiral, embedded vs disembedded | **High** (multiple consistent sources) |
| Play–language linkage patterns with wide age ranges | **Medium** (strong observational tradition; high individual variation) |
| Piagetian age bands as typical trends | **Medium** (useful heuristics; contested as rigid stages) |
| Formal operational onset and universality | **Low–medium** for exact ages; **high** that domain-specificity and inconsistency are real |
| Exact age for any specific topic | **Low** — use diagnostics and domain references |

The local corpus does **not** provide a complete prerequisite graph for all subjects. When topic-specific readiness is unclear, run the smallest diagnostic above and consult the matching domain reference.

## Quick Placement Workflow

1. Name the **material type** (representation + task + independence + duration).
2. Score the five prerequisite dimensions with a **short diagnostic** each.
3. If all strong → place at target depth with fading support planned.
4. If weak on representation/language → step down one representational form; keep learning goal.
5. If weak on memory/executive → shorten, externalize, structure; do not only “explain louder.”
6. If weak on abstraction → embed in familiar context; spiral back later.
7. State **confidence** and what was **not** tested; if topic-specific readiness is still unclear, consult the matching domain reference (see Relationships).
