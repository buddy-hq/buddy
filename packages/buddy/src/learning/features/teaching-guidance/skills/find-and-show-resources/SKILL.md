# Find and Show Resources

A good teacher doesn't redraw what already exists. When the real object, the original source, or a well-built simulation is the thing the learner should see, find it and show it, then keep teaching around it. This skill covers where to look, how to judge what you find, and how to present it so the learner does something with it.

## Look in the learner's own material first

If the learner has already brought the source (a book, paper, notes, or dataset in their library or workspace), use it. Open it in the reader on the Bench and point to the exact page, passage, or figure. Their own material is the most relevant and needs no vetting for fit.

## Where to look on the web

Search for the specific thing ("PhET projectile motion", "Wikimedia Commons mitochondrion electron micrograph") rather than a broad topic. Prefer the established places below, which are stable and reliable:

| You need | Look first at |
| --- | --- |
| A simulation or manipulable model | PhET (phet.colorado.edu) for physics, chemistry, math, and biology; Desmos (desmos.com) for graphs; GeoGebra (geogebra.org) for geometry; Falstad (falstad.com/circuit) for circuits |
| An interactive explainer | Seeing Theory (seeing-theory.brown.edu) for probability; Explained Visually (setosa.io/ev); Nicky Case's explorables (ncase.me); Distill (distill.pub) for machine learning; VisuAlgo (visualgo.net) for algorithms; CNN Explainer and TensorFlow Playground for neural networks |
| A real image or figure | Wikimedia Commons (commons.wikimedia.org), which is openly licensed; NASA Image Library (images.nasa.gov); museum open-access collections such as Smithsonian Open Access and The Met; textbook figures on OpenStax (openstax.org) |
| A primary source or document | Library of Congress (loc.gov), U.S. National Archives (archives.gov), the Avalon Project (avalon.law.yale.edu), Project Gutenberg (gutenberg.org), Internet Archive (archive.org) |
| Real data, often already charted | Our World in Data (ourworldindata.org), Gapminder (gapminder.org/tools), FRED (fred.stlouisfed.org) |
| A video | Established educational channels and university lecture series. Prefer a video whose title, description, or chapters let you confirm what it shows. |

Open only addresses you have reason to believe exist, such as ones from search results, the user, or the sites above. Don't guess deep links.

## Judge what you found

Before showing a resource, check that it:

- **Shows the thing:** it matches the exact idea and level, not just the topic.
- **Is accurate and authoritative:** it comes from a recognizable institution or author and agrees with what you know. Where you can, fetch the page to confirm it says what you expect.
- **Fits the learner:** the reading level, depth, and content suit their age and goal. Flag anything graphic or sensitive in one line.
- **Is usable:** it isn't paywalled, broken, or buried in ads. For images, prefer openly licensed ones and credit the source.
- **Can be described honestly:** you can't watch a video or operate a page. Describe only what you've confirmed from its title, description, transcript, or page text, and don't invent timestamps.

## Present it so the learner does something

- **Frame first.** Open the resource once the learner knows what to look for, usually after a short explanation or picture, not as the first move on a new idea. Opened too early, a resource replaces your teaching instead of supporting it.
- **Announce it in one clause** and give one specific task:
  - "I've opened the PhET projectile sim. Set the angle to 45° and then 60° and compare the range."
  - "In the photo, find the inner folds; those are where the energy work happens."
  - "Read the second paragraph of the letter and notice who it's addressed to."
- **Show one resource at a time.** A list of links is further reading, not teaching. Give one only when the user asks for more to explore.
- **Bring it back.** Ask what they noticed and build on it. Buddy doesn't see what happens in the Browser.
- **Remember that Browser tabs stay the user's.** Buddy can't refocus them later, so point back to a tab that is already open instead of reopening the page.
- **Keep an image inline when that's the point.** When a single openly licensed image should appear in the chat, and you can save files, you may save it to the workspace and show it inline with its credit. Otherwise open its page in the Browser.

## Common failures

- **Recreating what exists:** drawing a mitochondrion when a real micrograph is one search away, or building a projectile widget when PhET has one.
- **Handing off the teaching:** opening a long video or a complex simulation with no frame and no task.
- **Link dumps:** five resources when one well-chosen one teaches.
- **Unverified claims:** promising a video "shows X at 3:40" without confirming it.
- **Generated images passed off as real.** When appearance or evidence is the point, show the real thing; label any illustration as an illustration.
