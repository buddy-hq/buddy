# Project And Performance Materials

A handbook for teachers and instructional designers who create, review, and improve **project and performance materials**: the concrete documents that structure extended student work toward authentic outcomes and visible evidence of learning.

---

## What This Resource Family Is

**Project and performance materials** are learner- and teacher-facing artifacts that organize work over time toward a substantive product, presentation, or performance. They make the task, timeline, standards, collaboration, inquiry, audience, and quality expectations explicit enough that students can act without guessing what “the project” means.

Typical artifacts include:

- **Project briefs** and charters (purpose, constraints, outcomes, timeline)
- **Driving-question** or problem-framing sheets
- **Performance-task** packets (scenario, role, deliverables, constraints)
- **Milestone** and checkpoint planners (deadlines, intermediate products, review points)
- **Entry-event** or launch materials (hook, initial questions, need-to-know starters)
- **Inquiry supports** (resource guides, interview protocols, fieldwork permissions, research logs)
- **Collaboration materials** (team contracts, role cards, meeting agendas, contribution trackers)
- **Product specifications** (format, length, technical requirements, audience, citation expectations)
- **Critique and revision** protocols tied to the project
- **Reflection** prompts and learning logs
- **Exhibition, presentation, or portfolio** guides (rehearsal checklists, audience Q&A, display standards)
- **Public-product** distribution plans (where work is shared and who sees it)

The **pedagogical job** is not to “assign a fun activity at the end of a unit.” It is to use extended, meaningful work as the **primary vehicle** for students to build disciplinary knowledge, apply it in context, practice success skills (collaboration, communication, self-management, problem solving), and demonstrate learning to a real or realistic audience.

In high-quality project-based learning, the project is the **main course**, not a dessert served after conventional coverage. Materials should reflect that: the brief frames inquiry and products that carry standards-aligned learning, not decoration on top of a finished unit.

## Artifacts In This Family Vs Nearby Families

| Belongs here | Keep separate (nearby family) |
|---|---|
| Project brief with driving question, timeline, products, audience | End-of-unit **worksheet** or drill pack (`practice-materials-worksheets`) |
| Performance-task scenario and deliverable spec | **Quiz or test** with fixed items (`assessment-instruments`, `question-item-sets`) |
| Milestone tracker and checkpoint rubrics embedded in the project | Standalone **rubric** or marking guide used across tasks (`criteria-scoring-materials`) |
| Critique protocol written for this project’s products | Generic **feedback form** or revision tracker (`feedback-revision-materials`) |
| Annotated **exemplar** of a strong public product | Success criteria and model work packs (`success-exemplar-materials`) |
| Science **investigation** or lab sequence as the core task | Investigation/lab handouts when inquiry is the dominant structure (`investigation-lab-materials`) |
| Seminar or discussion protocol only | Discussion/collaboration sheets without a culminating product (`discussion-collaboration-materials`) |
| Essay or report prompt without extended inquiry | Writing-task materials (`writing-task-materials`) |

**Performance materials** overlap projects when the culminating evidence is a live or recorded performance (presentation, debate, demonstration, portfolio defense). Author them here when the **extended arc, inquiry, and public audience** are central; use writing-task or criteria-scoring families when a single written performance dominates and the arc is short.

**Arts, music, and live performance (boundary).** In PBL traditions, “performance” usually means **presenting or defending learning** to an audience—not conservatory-style technique assessment. Formal programmes such as the IB explicitly include **artistic performances** alongside investigations, fieldwork, and oral work as summative evidence types. When the culminating work is **theatre, music, or dance** judged on repertoire, technique, or adjudication standards, this handbook still covers **briefs, rehearsal milestones, audience criteria, and reflection**—but authors should pull **discipline- and qualification-specific** rubrics and syllabi from official arts programmes or professional standards; neither authority map nor PBLWorks supplies a full conservatory performance manual.

## When This Family Fits

Use project and performance materials when:

- Learning goals require **application**, synthesis, or transfer—not only recall—and students need time to investigate, draft, and revise.
- An **authentic or realistic context** (community issue, client, publication, exhibition, competition, workplace scenario) strengthens motivation and rigor.
- You want evidence of **process and product** (inquiry moves, collaboration, drafts, final work).
- Standards or competencies are best shown through **integrated work** across reading, writing, research, design, or presentation.
- Students benefit from **voice and choice** within clear constraints (topic angle, product format, team roles).
- A **public or peer audience** beyond the teacher will view or use the work.

Materials should answer, in writing: *What are we trying to find out or solve? What will we make or do? For whom? By when? How will we know it is good enough?*

### World-language performance programmes

For modern language courses, extend the brief with **mode-based performance targets** aligned to Can-Do traditions: **interpretive** (understanding authentic texts or media), **interpersonal** (negotiating meaning in live exchange), and **presentational** (explaining or persuading for an audience), plus **intercultural** competence when culture is part of the driving question. Materials should name which modes each milestone and final product will demonstrate, how learners will self-assess against Can-Do indicators, and how team vs individual evidence is credited. The project arc elsewhere in this handbook still applies; mode descriptors replace vague “communication skills” where precision matters.

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit

Avoid defaulting to a project brief when:

- The goal is **fluency, vocabulary, or procedure practice** better served by short, spaced practice sheets.
- Time is too limited for **sustained inquiry**; a one-off “poster project” will likely be thin.
- There is **no real question or problem**—only a topical craft or slideshow after lectures.
- **Criteria and scaffolds are missing**; students will produce uneven work and equate “project” with decoration.
- Assessment must be **highly standardized and quick** for large cohorts without moderation capacity.
- Collaboration is **unstructured group work** with no roles, checkpoints, or individual accountability.
- Access barriers (materials, technology, home support, language) are unaddressed and the brief assumes resources not all learners have.

A performance task can be appropriate in shorter windows if it still includes clear evidence targets, constraints, and quality descriptors—but it is not a substitute for a full project arc when the learning goals demand extended investigation and revision.

### Choosing project materials vs a performance-task instrument vs a short writing task

| If the primary job is… | Author here | Use a neighbor instead |
|---|---|---|
| Extended inquiry, milestones, public product, team collaboration over weeks | **Project and performance materials** | — |
| A bounded scenario with deliverables and criteria, often summative, without a full PBL arc | **Performance-task packet here**; if the packet *is* the scored exam, also align with `assessment-instruments` | Item banks and fixed MCQ exams → `assessment-instruments` |
| One dominant written product with a shorter arc | — | `writing-task-materials` |
| Rubric or mark scheme reused across tasks | Point to criteria in the brief | `criteria-scoring-materials` |

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

### PBLWorks Gold Standard PBL (core)

PBLWorks defines project-based learning as learning through **real-world and personally meaningful projects** over an extended period, culminating in work shared with a **real audience**. Their Gold Standard model is research-informed and splits into:

1. **Essential project design elements**—what strong project *materials* must embody in the brief, milestones, and product specs.
2. **Project-based teaching practices**—what teacher-facing planning and student-facing scaffolds must support from launch through culmination.

This handbook treats those elements and practices as the **primary design vocabulary** for authoring and reviewing project/performance artifacts. Do not reproduce PBLWorks rubrics, posters, or branded diagrams; paraphrase and apply the ideas in original materials.

### University teaching centers (supporting)

Open guides such as Carnegie Mellon’s rubric resource clarify how to express **performance expectations** for projects, presentations, and team products: component parts, levels of mastery, formative vs summative use, and communicating standards before work is due. Use this tradition when project materials need aligned **criteria sheets** or when the brief should point to how work will be judged—without duplicating full rubric tables from copyrighted sources.

### Exam and programme assessment documentation (supporting, context-specific)

Formal programmes emphasize **internal assessment**, performance tasks, investigations, fieldwork, oral interviews, and **artistic performances** judged against **set standards** rather than rank ordering alone. That tradition helps when materials must mirror **moderated performance assessment**: clear task wording, evidence requirements, criterion- or markband-based judging, and fair application of criteria across schools.

**International Baccalaureate.** Use these **open IBO publications** and summary pages when authoring moderated performance or internal-assessment packets:

| Document | Use when authoring |
|---|---|
| *Assessment principles and practices—Quality assessments in a digital age* (2018 PDF) | Validity chain, marks vs grades, assessment cycle, moderation concepts, varied task types |
| *Assessment principles and practices—A guide to assessment for teachers and coordinators* (PDF) | Internal-assessment moderation, sample selection, enquiry upon results at qualification boundary |
| *About assessment* (open web) | Task variety (including artistic performance); links to examiner/teacher/student assessment guides |
| Subject-specific teacher support / IA criteria (via official IB subject guides) | Task wording, evidence requirements, portfolio rules—**always** verify the current guide for your subject |

Authoring implications for **IA-style** or moderated performance packets:

- State **what evidence** is required (process logs, drafts, final product, oral, live performance) and **who marks** against which criteria or markbands.
- Build **individual accountability** inside team products when collaboration is allowed.
- Plan for **external moderation** logic: teacher marks are reviewed for consistent standard application; materials should make criteria observable enough that another teacher could judge from the packet.
- Do not copy proprietary IA forms, mark schemes, or task-specific criteria from IB subject guides.

This handbook stays **programme-neutral** for general classroom PBL; pull programme rules only when the brief must mirror a qualification.

### Cambridge and OCR qualification performance (pointers)

**Cambridge Assessment Network** supports **design quality** for qualification-style performance and project assessment—validity, reliability, fairness, standards, comparability, practicality—not day-to-day PBL brief templates.

**Cambridge International** and **OCR** operate **subject qualifications** where extended work often includes coursework, practical endorsements, presentations, or performance components. Neither board publishes a single “project brief” manual for all subjects; instead, each specification defines tasks, evidence, and marking.

| When authoring for… | Practical pointer |
|---|---|
| Cambridge IGCSE / A Level coursework or practical | Use the **current syllabus** and published **support materials** on Cambridge International subject pages; align milestone and product specs to command words and assessment objectives in your own words; do not reproduce mark schemes or candidate work. |
| OCR **Extended Project** (EPQ) | Project may be report, **performance/event**, artefact, or design; learners are judged on **planning, research, development, and evaluation**—author process milestones and criteria accordingly (see OCR EPQ qualification pages). |
| OCR GCSE / A Level / vocational practicals | Use qualification pages, **Teach Cambridge**, **ExamBuilder**, or past-paper materials for **format and evidence** realism; do not reproduce mark schemes or candidate work. |

Keep **Gold Standard PBL** as the default lens for extended **classroom** projects without external moderation. Use qualification pointers when materials must **mirror** moderated performance assessment or a named board task type.

### Collaborative-learning evidence (supporting lens)

The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit defines collaborative learning as pupils working together in groups **small enough that everyone participates**—**distinct from unstructured group work**. Approaches may use separate subtasks toward a common outcome or shared work on one task.

| Theme | Practical authoring takeaway for project materials |
|---|---|
| Structure | Tasks must be **designed** for interdependence; seating pupils together is insufficient |
| Group size | **Three to five** learners per group is most promising in toolkit summaries |
| Joint outcomes | Shared group goals tend to show **stronger impact** than parallel individual subtasks only |
| Talk | Ensure **all learners articulate thinking**; silent passengers undermine benefits |
| Support | Pupils and teachers need **practice** in collaboration—it is not automatic |
| Equity | Lower-attaining pupils need **designed inclusion** or gaps widen |
| Competition | Between-group competition can help but may shift focus away from learning quality |

**Authoring implication:** Every collaboration pack in a project should specify **participation**, **shared outcome**, **individual accountability**, and **what each milestone produces**—not only “work in a group.”

### Accessibility frameworks (cross-cutting lens)

Universal design for learning applies to projects as much as to worksheets: multiple ways to **engage**, represent information, and **express** learning (oral, written, visual, recorded, assistive technology). Project briefs should not assume a single product format or presentation mode when alternatives can show the same learning.

## Design Elements Every Strong Project Brief Should Embody

The following seven features come from PBLWorks’ Gold Standard **project design** tradition. Authoring materials should make each one visible to teachers and, where appropriate, to students—not as jargon labels, but as concrete sections, prompts, or checklists.

### 1. Challenging problem or question

Frame the work with a **meaningful problem to solve or question to answer** at the right level of challenge—often called a **driving question**. It should be understandable to students, open enough for inquiry, and anchored in standards.

**In materials:** state the driving question prominently; note what makes it worth answering; list sub-questions students may pursue; distinguish it from a mere topic title (“Civil War”) or a recipe (“make a diorama”).

### 2. Sustained inquiry

Students need a **rigorous, extended process** of asking questions, finding resources, and applying information—not a single afternoon of search-and-paste.

**In materials:** inquiry calendars; “need to know” lists; source-quality guidance; checkpoints for research notes, interviews, or data collection; expectations for how findings feed the product.

### 3. Authenticity

Authenticity means **real-world context, tasks, tools, quality standards, or impact**, and/or connection to **students’ lives and concerns**.

**In materials:** name the audience or client; describe real constraints (budget, genre, safety, ethics); link to community or disciplinary norms; avoid fake authenticity (“pretend you are a king”) when a genuine connection is possible.

### 4. Student voice and choice

Students should make **some decisions** about how they work and what they create, and express **their ideas in their own voice** within non-negotiables you set for rigor and equity.

**In materials:** choice menus (product format, subtopic, team structure); boundaries (“must include primary sources”; “must address the driving question”); equitable options so choice does not become tracking by resource.

### 5. Reflection

Students and teachers reflect on **what was learned**, how inquiry and collaboration worked, **quality of work**, and **obstacles and strategies**.

**In materials:** reflection prompts at milestones and culmination; teacher planning notes for debrief; prompts that target process and content, not only “what I liked.”

### 6. Critique and revision

Students **give, receive, and use feedback** to improve process and products before the final audience sees work.

**In materials:** draft deadlines; peer/teacher/expert critique protocols; revision plans; criteria linked to feedback (what to improve and how).

### 7. Public product

Students make work **public**—shared with and explained to people **beyond the classroom**.

**In materials:** audience description; presentation or publication format; accessibility and consent (especially for student images, interviews, or online posting); success indicators for the public moment.

**Learning goals** stated in the brief should include **standards-based content** and **success skills** such as critical thinking, problem solving, communication, self-management, project management, and collaboration—aligned to what the milestone and product evidence will show.

## Teaching Practices Your Materials Must Support

Gold Standard **project-based teaching** describes what must happen while students use your materials. Author checklists and teacher-facing notes so artifacts support these seven practices—not only student handouts.

| Practice | What it means for your materials |
|---|---|
| **Design and plan** | Teacher planner: launch, milestones, resources, differentiation, culminating event; room for student voice within the plan. |
| **Align to standards** | Explicit standards/competencies map; each major product tied to evidence you will look for. |
| **Build culture** | Norms for independence, inquiry, teamwork, and quality; safety and belonging for risk-taking and critique. |
| **Manage activities** | Calendars, checkpoints, deadlines, resource lists, roles, and “making work public” steps. |
| **Scaffold student learning** | Mini-lessons, templates, exemplars, language supports, and tools so all students can reach goals—not only self-starters. |
| **Assess student learning** | Formative checks, rubrics or criteria references, self- and peer-assessment on team and individual contributions. |
| **Engage and coach** | Conference prompts, observation notes, criteria for when to teach a skill vs when students struggle productively. |

Materials fail when they are only a **student packet** with no teacher planning layer, or only a **teacher guide** with no student-readable driving question and milestones.

## Key Authoring Decisions

Before drafting, decide:

1. **Main course or dessert?** Will the project carry the unit’s learning, or supplement prior instruction? Materials for a main-course project need fuller inquiry, scaffolding, and assessment design.

2. **Driving question quality.** Is it engaging, open, age-appropriate, and standards-aligned? Can students list what they need to find out?

3. **Products and evidence.** What artifacts prove learning (report, model, policy brief, performance, portfolio)? What **draft** evidence is required along the way?

4. **Audience and authenticity.** Who is the real or realistic audience? What makes the task matter beyond a grade?

5. **Timeline and cognitive load.** How many weeks? How many simultaneous products? Where are hard deadlines vs flexible work time?

6. **Individual vs team accountability.** What is shared? What is graded individually? How will free-riding be prevented without killing collaboration?

7. **Scaffolding level.** What do novices need day one vs what should they figure out by the final week? Plan **fade** of supports.

8. **Equity and access.** Materials, language, technology, transportation, home roles, and presentation anxiety—offer **equivalent pathways** where possible.

9. **Assessment use.** Formative checkpoints vs summative judgment; whether scores are standards-referenced; how peer and self-assessment feed revision.

10. **Critique culture.** Models of kind, specific feedback; time for revision; protection from public sharing of draft work that shames learners.

## Practical Authoring Workflow

### Phase 1 — Anchor outcomes

- List standards/competencies and **success skills** the project must develop.
- Draft a **driving question** and 3–5 possible **sub-questions**.
- Define **final public product(s)** and intermediate milestones.

### Phase 2 — Design the arc

- Map **week-by-week** (or phase) milestones: entry event, research, draft, critique, revision, rehearsal, public share.
- Design a strong **entry event / launch** (distinct from a topic lecture or unit recap):
  - Use an authentic **hook**—guest, scenario, artifact, field connection, or problem reveal—that makes the driving question feel worth pursuing.
  - Let the hook provoke **student-generated questions**; capture them as a living **need-to-know list**, not a teacher-prepared outline handed out on day one.
  - Include launch materials students can read or experience (brief scenario, video prompt, letter from a client, site visit guide) plus teacher timing and facilitation notes.
  - **Revisit and extend** the need-to-know list at checkpoints; shallow or missing student questions often signal weak authenticity or an unclear driving question.
  - **Launch for inquiry**, then scaffold—avoid front-loading answers the project is meant to teach.
- For each milestone, specify **deliverable**, **criteria**, and **who reviews it**.
- Plan **mini-lessons** and scaffolds at predictable struggle points.

### Phase 3 — Draft core artifacts

1. **One-page project overview** for students: question, why it matters, products, audience, timeline, norms.
2. **Detailed brief** for teachers: standards map, differentiation, materials, safety/ethics, assessment plan.
3. **Milestone sheet** students keep: dates, tasks, reflection boxes.
4. **Collaboration pack** (if teams): contract, roles, meeting norms, contribution log.
5. **Inquiry pack**: source guidelines, note-taking, citation, interview/fieldwork rules.
6. **Critique and revision** protocol aligned to criteria.
7. **Exhibition/presentation** guide: rehearsal, tech, Q&A, accessibility.

### Phase 4 — Align assessment materials

- Link to or embed **criteria** (see criteria-scoring family); avoid vague “creativity points.”
- Add **self- and peer-assessment** tied to revision, not only grades.
- Note **formative** uses (conference, draft feedback) vs **summative** judgment.

### Phase 5 — Pilot and revise

- Walk the timeline as a teacher: is any week impossible?
- Ask whether a stranger could see **what good work looks like** before day one.
- Check authenticity, voice/choice, and public product for your context—not for an idealized video.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

Use this review before publishing or adopting materials.

### Project design (student-facing)

- [ ] A **clear driving question** or problem—not only a topic.
- [ ] **Sustained inquiry** planned with checkpoints and resource guidance.
- [ ] **Authentic** context, tools, or audience—or explicit personal relevance.
- [ ] Meaningful **voice and choice** within clear non-negotiables.
- [ ] **Reflection** built into milestones and culmination.
- [ ] **Critique and revision** before final public share.
- [ ] **Public product** with named audience and sharing plan.

### Performance and evidence

- [ ] Deliverables match stated **learning goals** (content + skills).
- [ ] **Intermediate evidence** required (notes, drafts, data, rehearsals).
- [ ] **Criteria** describe observable quality; avoid vague praise categories.
- [ ] **Individual accountability** in team tasks is defined.

### Teaching and management (teacher-facing)

- [ ] **Standards alignment** documented.
- [ ] **Scaffolds** identified for language, executive function, and content gaps.
- [ ] **Formative assessment** points change instruction or support—not only grades.
- [ ] **Time and resources** are realistic for your setting.
- [ ] **Access and safety** (digital, physical, emotional) addressed.

### Distinction from weak “projects”

- [ ] Project is **main course** OR brief honestly scopes a shorter performance task with full criteria.
- [ ] Not a **dessert** recap disguised as PBL.
- [ ] Requires **thinking beyond recall** to create the final work.

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|---|---|---|
| Topic label instead of driving question | No inquiry arc | Write an arguable, investigable question |
| One-shot research day | No sustained inquiry | Multi-checkpoint research with quality criteria |
| Poster after lectures | Dessert project | Integrate learning through the project timeline |
| “Choose any format” with no criteria | Unequal rigor | Constrain formats or provide criteria per format |
| Public share without rehearsal | Anxiety, weak communication | Rehearsal guide, feedback, optional formats |
| Group grade only | Free-riding, unfairness | Individual logs, roles, peer + teacher input |
| Rubric only at the end | No revision culture | Draft criteria + critique before summative |
| Fake authenticity | Cynicism | Real audience, data, or community tie when possible |
| Product without standards map | Activity-rich, learning-poor | Tie each product to explicit outcomes |
| Ignoring access | Widens gaps | Multiple means of engagement and expression |
| Random “project idea” lists | No design elements or criteria | Start from driving question, standards map, and milestones |
| Marketplace crafts after coverage | Dessert activity, weak inquiry | Main-course project with public product and revision |
| AI prompt packs without design | Missing inquiry, audience, revision | Full brief with Gold Standard elements and criteria |
| Copied PBLWorks rubrics or posters | Copyright/trademark risk | Paraphrase design ideas in original materials |

## How Frameworks Influence These Materials (Without Becoming The Resource)

- **Gold Standard PBL** is the central **design tradition** for this family—not a handout to paste into class. Your job is to **instantiate** its elements in original briefs and milestones.
- **UDL** should shape **options** for representation, action, and expression in projects; it is not itself a project brief.
- **Standards frameworks** (state, national, discipline-specific) inform **alignment tables** inside teacher materials; they are not student-facing “framework posters.”
- **Collaborative-learning research** informs **structure** of team materials; it does not replace a project brief.
- **Formal assessment programmes** (IB internal assessment, Cambridge/OCR coursework) inform **moderation, evidence rules, and task-type realism** when you author for those contexts; classroom projects without external moderation still benefit from **standards-referenced** criteria.
- **Arts and live-performance disciplines** need **qualification- or conservatory-specific** technical criteria in addition to project briefs; this family supplies arc, rehearsal, and audience structure—not full adjudication manuals.

---

## Relationships To Nearby Resource Families

- **Investigation and lab materials:** Use when the dominant structure is **disciplinary investigation** (hypothesis, data, CER, engineering design) rather than a multidisciplinary project. Projects may *include* labs; lab handouts stay in the lab family when they are the spine of the work.
- **Writing-task materials:** Use for **writing-centric** assignments with a shorter arc; project materials may contain writing products as one of several deliverables.
- **Criteria-scoring materials:** Rubrics and marking guides often **accompany** projects but live as their own family when reused across tasks.
- **Feedback-revision materials:** Generic revision trackers and conference forms can support any project; project-specific **critique protocols** belong here when tied to one driving question and product set.
- **Success-exemplar materials:** Annotated models of strong products; project briefs should **point to** exemplars, not duplicate long annotated samples.
- **Discussion-collaboration materials:** Protocols for talk without a culminating project product; merge when discussion is a **milestone** toward the public product.
- **Assessment instruments:** Summative tests and fixed-item exams; performance tasks overlap when the **task sheet** is the instrument—still author performance evidence and criteria here, not item banks.

## Sample Material Outlines (Original Patterns)

These are **patterns**, not copies of any vendor template.

### Student project overview (1–2 pages)

- Driving question and “why we’re doing this”
- What you will create and who will see it
- Timeline with milestone dates
- Team info and norms (if applicable)
- How you will be graded (summary + where full criteria live)
- Where to find scaffolds (research guide, critique form)

### Teacher implementation guide

- Standards and success skills map
- Entry event description and materials
- Weekly plan with scaffolds and formative checks
- Differentiation and access notes
- Assessment plan (formative/summative, peer/self)
- Risk, safety, ethics, and community contact checklist

### Milestone tracker (student)

| Phase | Due | Deliverable | Reflection prompt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch | | Need-to-know list | What do we already think? |
| Research | | Annotated sources | What surprised us? |
| Draft | | Product draft | What feedback do we need? |
| Revision | | Revised product | What changed and why? |
| Public | | Presentation/exhibition | What did we learn? |

## Summary For Reviewers

Project and performance materials turn **extended, authentic work** into usable artifacts: questions, timelines, scaffolds, collaboration rules, critique, and public sharing. Author them using PBLWorks’ Gold Standard **design elements** and **teaching practices** as the core lens; pair with clear **criteria**, structured **collaboration**, and **access** choices. Keep rubrics, labs, short writing tasks, and generic feedback tools in their own families unless this project brief truly integrates them.

For a **specific IB subject internal assessment**, use that subject’s current guide and published teacher-support material on the IBO site; this handbook does not supply task-specific criteria wording.
