# Success And Exemplar Materials

Success and exemplar materials make **quality visible before and during production**. They answer a question learners cannot reliably answer from a grade alone: *What does strong work look like for this task, and how does my current work compare?*

This family is fragmented across assessment theory, university teaching guides, evidence on feedback, and extended-task design. There is no single “exemplar standards body.” Responsible authoring therefore **triangulates**: Sadler’s formative-assessment logic for why exemplars matter; Carnegie Mellon’s criteria-and-rubric practice for making expectations explicit; the Education Endowment Foundation’s feedback guidance for closing gaps; and PBLWorks’ project-design tradition when quality is tied to authentic products, critique, and revision.

---

## What This Family Is For

The pedagogical job is to **externalize a reference level of quality** so learners can:

1. **Hold a concept of the standard** being aimed for (goal, reference level, or success profile).
2. **Compare** current work to that standard with reasonable objectivity.
3. **Choose moves** that close the gap between actual and intended performance.

Royce Sadler argues that accurate teacher judgments alone often fail to produce improvement. Learners also need **evaluative expertise**: appreciation of quality, comparison skill, and a repertoire of revision tactics—not only comments received after submission. Success and exemplar materials are the concrete artifacts that build that expertise when they are used as part of production and revision, not only as post-hoc models.

These materials matter most where outcomes are **judged qualitatively with multiple criteria** (writing, design, inquiry products, performances, extended responses). They matter less where “correct/incorrect” is sufficient and the main learning loop is knowledge of results.

---

## Artifacts In This Family

| Artifact type | Typical form | Primary function |
|---|---|---|
| **Success criteria sheet** | Bulleted or short descriptors of what success requires | States the target before/during work; anchors feedback |
| **Quality indicator / “what good looks like” guide** | Checklist or short prose describing strong performance | Makes tacit teacher standards more public |
| **Model answer / model response** | Completed response to the same or parallel task | Shows an achievable end state; must be explained, not copied blindly |
| **Annotated exemplar** | Sample work with teacher/expert notes on *why* it succeeds (or partly succeeds) | Teaches criteria in context; links features to quality |
| **Anchor set / benchmark responses** | Two or more samples at different quality levels for the same task | Calibrates judgment; supports comparative discussion |
| **Non-exemplar / “what not to do” sample** | Weak or flawed response used carefully for diagnosis | Surfaces misconceptions and common errors without shame-by-name |
| **Comparison prompt sheet** | Questions or routines for comparing own work to models | Turns passive viewing into evaluative practice |
| **Anchor paper / anchor portfolio piece** | Discipline-trusted reference piece used across a unit or program | Stabilizes standards across teachers or cohorts |

### Variants Worth Naming

- **Process exemplars** show strong planning, inquiry logs, or drafts—not only final products.
- **Partial exemplars** show one criterion done well (e.g., strong evidence use in an otherwise unfinished essay).
- **Genre sets** include multiple strong examples with different surface features so learners see that quality can be expressed in more than one way.
- **Public-product exemplars** (common in project-based work) show how audience-ready outcomes meet authenticity and craft expectations.

### What Belongs In Nearby Families (Keep Separate)

| Nearby family | Contrast |
|---|---|
| **Criteria and scoring materials** | Rubrics, marking guides, and level descriptors **define** judgment dimensions and score bands. Success/exemplar materials **instantiate** those dimensions in real work. Author both together when possible; do not treat a rubric alone as “showing” quality. |
| **Feedback and revision materials** | Feedback forms and revision trackers **operate on** work in progress. Exemplars **establish the reference** feedback points toward. |
| **Worked-example materials** | Worked examples teach **procedure and solution paths** step by step. Exemplars teach **quality of a product or performance** against multiple criteria. |
| **Assessment instruments** | Tests and exams **measure** achievement. Exemplars **develop** evaluative capacity and self-monitoring during learning. |
| **Practice materials / worksheets** | Practice sheets emphasize **rehearsal and retrieval**. Exemplar sets emphasize **judgment, comparison, and revision toward a standard**. |

## When This Family Helps

Use success and exemplar materials when:

- The task has **multiple criteria** and fuzzy dimensions (coherence, reasoning quality, craft, persuasiveness).
- Learners are new to a **genre, format, or discipline convention** and cannot infer standards from the prompt alone.
- You want learners to **self-monitor during production**, not only react to grades afterward.
- **Peer review, critique, or revision cycles** need a shared picture of quality.
- Teachers need **calibration** across a team (common anchors reduce drift in what “good” means).
- Extended tasks include **authentic products** where “finished” is ambiguous without examples.

---

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit Or Commonly Misused

**Poor fit**

- Outcomes are binary (correct/incorrect) and the main need is practice or error correction, not quality exemplification.
- You have not yet clarified the **learning goal and evidence**—exemplars cannot substitute for task design.
- Summative ranking is the real purpose (sorting students against each other). Sadler stresses that norm-referenced comparison is a weak basis for formative improvement.

**Common misuses**

- **Model answer only, no criteria** — learners mimic surface features without understanding quality.
- **Single exemplar** — implies one “right” shape; weak for creative or multi-path tasks.
- **Unannotated perfect work** — intimidates novices and hides how quality was achieved.
- **Exemplars shown only after grading** — too late to support self-monitoring during production.
- **Named weak student work** — damages trust; use anonymized non-exemplars instead.
- **Decorative posters** — “excellent/good/fair” labels without observable descriptors or task linkage.
- **Exemplars divorced from revision** — viewing without comparison prompts, feedback, or a rewrite plan.

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

### Sadler — formative assessment and evaluative expertise

Sadler’s 1989 account is the deepest theoretical anchor for this family. Core ideas for authoring:

- **Feedback closes a gap** only when information about the gap between actual and reference performance is used to alter the gap—not when it is only recorded or reduced to a summary grade.
- **Three simultaneous conditions** for improvement: concept of the standard; comparison of current work to the standard; action that closes the gap. They are not a optional sequence.
- **Standards are often tacit** in expert teachers (“guild knowledge”). Exemplar materials help **externalize** standards so learners are not permanently dependent on teacher judgment.
- **Descriptive statements and exemplars combine**: verbal descriptors specify properties at a level of quality; exemplars make standards **concrete**. Neither alone is sufficient for complex performance.
- **Multiple exemplars per standard** teach that quality can be realized in different surface forms; learners must abstract what runs across cases.
- **Non-exemplars and contrast** support diagnosis; creativity is not opposed to disciplined quality when learners understand genre constraints.
- **Evaluative experience must be authentic** during production—peer comparison, guided critique, and self-assessment against models—not only teacher end comments.

Authoring implication: every exemplar pack should support **appreciation, comparison, and tactics**, not only display of a strong sample.

### Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center — explicit expectations via criteria

The university rubric guide treats rubrics as tools that **make performance expectations explicit** and support both scoring and formative feedback. For this family, the transferable points are:

- Divide work into **component parts** with describable differences across levels of mastery.
- Give criteria **with the assignment** so learners can monitor progress while working.
- Use returned work plus criteria so learners see **strengths, weaknesses, and where to direct effort**.

Success criteria sheets and annotated exemplars are how many K–12 and higher-ed teachers operationalize “explicit expectations” when a full rubric would be heavy for a short task.

### Education Endowment Foundation — feedback that moves learning forward

The EEF feedback guidance stresses **principles over delivery mode** (written vs verbal). For exemplar authoring, the relevant principles are:

1. **Lay foundations** with strong initial teaching and formative assessment—including **clarifying learning intentions and success criteria** before feedback can work well.
2. **Time feedback** to move learning forward, targeting real gaps (task, subject, self-regulation)—not personal traits.
3. **Plan how learners receive and use** feedback; monitor whether they act on it.

The report also describes classroom moves that exemplar materials support (without requiring any particular branded routine): discussing anonymized work to build criteria; studying strong models with attention to *why* they work; using **non-examples** carefully; and comparison activities (e.g., choosing the strongest attempt among several tries and defending the choice).

Exemplars should be designed as **input to a feedback loop**, not as static display.

### PBLWorks — quality in authentic products, critique, and revision

Gold Standard project design treats **critique and revision** and **public product** as essential elements. For extended performance:

- Quality is negotiated against **authentic standards** (real-world tools, audiences, or impact).
- Exemplars often need to show **process artifacts** (drafts, inquiry evidence) as well as final products.
- Critique protocols should connect to **revision plans**, not end at praise.

Use this tradition when success criteria attach to **projects, exhibitions, or multi-week products**—not only to short classroom tasks.

## Design Decisions Authors Must Make

### 1. What is the reference level?

Decide whether materials target:

- **Minimum acceptable** (threshold),
- **Proficient / on-standard** (typical expectation),
- **Exemplary** (stretch),
- or **multiple anchor points** along a continuum.

Mismatch between the exemplar’s level and the stated success criteria is a frequent source of learner confusion.

### 2. Descriptors, exemplars, or both?

Sadler’s combination is the default for complex work:

- **Descriptors** state properties (what must be present, accurate, or developed).
- **Exemplars** show how those properties appear in real responses.

For novices, lean slightly more concrete (exemplars + light annotation); for advanced learners, lean more on **criteria + comparative judgment** across several samples.

### 3. How many exemplars, and how diverse?

- Use **more than one** strong example when multiple valid solutions exist.
- Include **contrast** (strong vs weak, or two strengths with different tradeoffs) when calibration matters.
- For fuzzy criteria (e.g., “insightful analysis”), multiple surface-different strong samples help learners abstract the underlying quality.

### 4. Annotate for judgment, not admiration

Annotations should answer:

- Which **criterion** does this feature satisfy?
- **What would weaken** this if changed?
- What **move** could a writer/maker use to approach this quality?

Avoid vague praise (“great hook”) without tying to the success criteria.

### 5. Anonymization, consent, and dignity

- Use **permission-based or anonymized** student work when possible.
- Never display identifiable weak work as a cautionary tale.
- Non-exemplars should focus on **patterns of error**, not individuals.

### 6. Timing in the instructional sequence

| Timing | Typical use |
|---|---|
| **Before production** | Success criteria, genre exemplars, anchor discussion |
| **During production** | Checklists, partial models, comparison to draft criteria |
| **After draft, before final** | Annotated anchors, peer comparison sheets, revision targets |
| **After summative grading** | Limited value for formative goals; may support exam reflection |

### 7. Peer, self, and teacher roles

Design who compares work to the standard:

- **Teacher-led calibration** (norming on anchors),
- **Structured peer review** (criteria + exemplar side by side),
- **Self-assessment** (learners score own draft against success criteria, then revise).

Sadler’s peer-evaluation discussion applies: peers often judge others’ work more objectively than their own; design protocols that reduce defensiveness.

### 8. Accessibility and multiple representations (CAST UDL lens)

Quality can be shown through text, audio, video of performance, images of visual work, or multimodal portfolios. CAST Universal Design for Learning treats **representation**, **action/expression**, and **engagement** as design levers—not separate “access add-ons.” For exemplar packs:

**Multiple means to represent quality (Representation)**

- Offer the **same anchor** in more than one medium when the construct allows (e.g., captioned video of an oral presentation plus a transcript with criterion-linked notes).
- **Customize display** where possible: readable fonts, adjustable contrast, headings that map to criteria, glossaries for discipline terms used in annotations.
- **Illustrate through multiple media** (CAST Consideration 2.5): diagrams, timelines, or worked annotations—not only dense prose—when they clarify what “strong” looks like.
- **Highlight critical features** (Consideration 3.2): annotations should accentuate the evidence that justifies a level, not decorate the sample.

**Multiple means to use exemplars (Action & Expression)**

- Let learners **compare** via oral rehearsal, graphic organizers, or checklists—not only a written “compare to model” paragraph.
- Support **graduated practice** (Consideration 5.3): start with heavily annotated partial exemplars, fade to learner self-assessment against bare anchors.
- Do not require a **single demonstration format** for the comparison task unless that format is part of the construct (e.g., handwriting fluency).

**Engagement and dignity**

- Exemplars should not imply one cultural “ideal” performer; include **range** in strong samples when the task permits.
- Avoid exemplar sets that only reward one sensory channel (e.g., wall-sized text posters unreadable to some learners).

Plan **accessible formats** so exemplars do not become barriers: captions and transcripts for audio/video models, alt text for visual work, translated or simplified success criteria where needed, and plain-language criterion labels alongside assessor jargon.

### 9. Qualification-style anchor packs (IB, OCR, and similar)

Use this overlay when the task **mirrors high-stakes or programme-specific formats** (extended essays, structured questions, moderated coursework, board-style mark schemes)—not for every classroom checklist.

**What qualification traditions add**

Exam-board and programme operators treat exemplars as **operational anchors**: definitively marked responses that train scorers, monitor drift, and make markscheme language concrete. That logic parallels classroom anchor sets but often includes **script types** and **borderline rules** authors should know about:

| Tradition | Exemplar logic (paraphrased) | Classroom authoring use |
|---|---|---|
| **IB (standards-based programmes)** | In IBO’s open *Assessment principles and practices* (2018), principal examiners set a marking standard; training uses **practice**, **qualification**, and **seed** scripts so others learn, prove, and monitor alignment (*Standard setting—Preparing examiners for marking*). Marks credit work against a markscheme; **grades** judge against defined standards that may account for difficulty. Subjective work may use **criteria marking** or **global impression**; **best-fit** level choice is common. | Build **anchor sets** at key levels; run team calibration; document borderline decisions. Pair learner-facing annotated exemplars with **assessor-only** notes on best-fit reading. Do **not** copy proprietary examiner scripts or live mark schemes. |
| **Cambridge OCR and UK qualification operators** | Official **past papers**, **mark schemes**, and **examiner reports** (via the Past Paper Finder) show how published tasks are structured and how markscheme language maps to observable features—authors must open current materials locally; this handbook does not reproduce them. | Use those materials to learn **format conventions** and feature mapping—then author **original** parallel tasks and classroom anchors. Point teachers to the official **Past Paper Finder** rather than unofficial dumps. |
| **Cambridge Assessment professional standards** | Assessment quality is judged across a cycle: purposes, diagnosis, judgment, improvement. | Review whether your exemplar pack actually shows the construct your rubric claims—anchors are a quality check, not decoration. |

**Authoring workflow (qualification overlay)**

1. Start from **criteria and markscheme language** in `criteria-scoring-materials` (levels, best-fit, dimension vs holistic).
2. Select or write **2–4 anchor responses** spanning meaningful spread, including **borderline** cases teams argue about.
3. Annotate **feature-to-criterion** links in plain language; keep assessor jargon in a separate teacher guide if needed.
4. Run a **calibration session** (see anchor-set outline below) before learners see anchors.
5. If the instrument is a full exam-style package, align anchors with `assessment-instruments` (blueprint, administration, scoring package).

**Limits**

- Board materials are **copyrighted**; summarize conventions independently; do not reproduce full scripts, mark schemes, or grade-boundary tables.
- Qualification anchors optimize **scorer agreement**; novices still need **success criteria in accessible language**—anchors alone are insufficient (Sadler’s descriptors-plus-exemplars pairing).

### 10. Language-performance exemplar packs (ACTFL Can-Do overlay)

When exemplars support **world-language** performance tasks, align to **mode-based** targets—not generic essay models alone.

The **2026 NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements** (ACTFL hub; aligned with *Proficiency Guidelines 2024* and *Performance Descriptors*) organize growth across **Interpretive**, **Interpersonal**, and **Presentational** communication, plus **Intercultural** competence. Downloadable **performance-indicator** benchmarks describe what learners can do by mode and sublevel—use them as templates for what to annotate on a sample (do not copy indicator tables into classroom packs).

**Authoring implications**

- **Exemplars show sustained capability**, not a one-time task tick: learners may be at different sublevels per mode (reading vs speaking, etc.).
- Provide **mode-appropriate models**: interpretive packs might pair an authentic text with annotated sample responses; interpersonal packs might use transcript or video with notes on negotiation moves; presentational packs might show organization, register, and audience adaptation—not only grammar accuracy.
- Use **multiple strong samples** per mode when quality can look different on the surface (different topics, registers, or text types).
- Can-Do statements are **not a checklist** demonstrated once; classroom anchors should illustrate **consistent** performance at a target sublevel across varied evidence.
- Co-design success criteria with `criteria-scoring-materials`; tie speaking/writing tasks to `writing-task-materials` or `discussion-collaboration-materials` when those families own the task shell.

**Intercultural exemplars**

When culture is part of the construct, include samples that show **investigation and interaction** with cultural knowledge—not stereotypes—and pair with reflection prompts inspired by ACTFL’s **Intercultural Can-Do Statements** and **Intercultural Reflection Tool** (separate downloads on the Can-Do hub; paraphrase only).

### 11. Alignment to discipline and age

- **Younger learners**: fewer criteria, clearer indicators, more comparison games, shorter annotations.
- **Older learners**: more peer critique, more abstract criteria, more responsibility for building success criteria from exemplar discussion.
- **Disciplinary genres** (lab report, historical argument, proof, design portfolio) need **genre-faithful** models, not generic “good work” lists.

## Artifact Patterns (Authoring Recipes)

### Success criteria sheet

**Include**

- Learning goal in plain language (what this task is for).
- **Observable** success indicators (what a reader/viewer would see).
- Non-goals or common confusions (“this is not a summary of the article”).
- Optional: proficiency vs stretch indicators.

**Avoid**

- Vague value labels without evidence (“creativity,” “effort”) unless defined observably.
- Criteria that duplicate the entire rubric when a short list would suffice.

### Model answer

**Include**

- Same task or **parallel task** with transparent differences noted.
- Brief **author’s commentary** or teacher notes on choices.
- Reminder that the goal is **understanding quality**, not replication of wording.

**Avoid**

- Model answers for tasks where originality is central, without also showing **range** of strong responses.

### Annotated exemplar

**Include**

- Side-by-side or margin notes linking features to **named criteria**.
- At least one **near-miss** or partial success if helpful for calibration.
- Questions that prompt learner comparison (“Where does this meet criterion 2?”).

### Anchor set (benchmark responses)

**Include**

- **Two to four** responses spanning a meaningful spread (not every grade point).
- Facilitation questions: strengths, weaknesses, rank order *and* why.
- Agreement on **which features** justify placement.

### Team anchor-calibration session (outline)

Use when two or more teachers (or a teacher and teaching assistant) must share the same standard before classroom use:

1. **Distribute materials early** — success criteria, task prompt, and the anchor set; confirm everyone is scoring the same construct.
2. **Silent read** — each participant reviews every anchor response without discussion.
3. **Silent score** — rate each anchor against **named criteria** (or place on an agreed continuum); avoid jumping to a single holistic grade first.
4. **Flag discrepancies** — mark responses where scores differ by more than one level or criterion.
5. **Discuss on evidence** — debate observable features tied to criteria; set aside prestige, handwriting, or prior knowledge of the writer.
6. **Record agreements** — document the feature lists that justify each placement; revise anchor annotations or facilitation questions if samples were misread.
7. **Spot-check (optional)** — score one fresh response together to confirm shared understanding before learners see the anchors.

### Non-exemplar

**Include**

- Clear purpose (diagnose misconception, show common structure error).
- Anonymity and norms for respectful discussion.
- Bridge to **revision tactics** (“If you see X, try Y”).

**Avoid**

- Mocking tone; public shaming; using non-exemplars without teaching how to fix.

### Comparison prompt sheet

**Include**

- Prompts for **similarities and differences** to the model.
- One **priority revision** based on largest gap to standard.
- Space for evidence from the learner’s own draft.

## Classroom Introduction Moves

Exemplar packs work best when learners **use** them, not only view them. The EEF feedback guidance and related formative-assessment practice describe classroom moves this family supports—paraphrased here without copying any branded routine or script.

**Before learners draft**

- Discuss **anonymised work** from a prior cohort (or teacher-built samples) to surface what “good” means for this task.
- Co-build or refine **success criteria** from that discussion so descriptors match what the class actually values.
- Study one or two **strong models** with attention to *why* features work, not only what is on the page.

**During drafting or revision**

- Compare the learner’s draft to a model or anchor set using a **comparison prompt sheet** (similarities, largest gap, one priority revision).
- Use **non-examples** carefully: diagnose a common error pattern, then teach a fix strategy—never as identifiable “bad student” work.
- Present **several attempts** at the same task and ask learners to choose which best meets the criteria and **defend the choice** with evidence from the text or product.
- Invite learners to **improve a weak sample** against the success criteria (strengthening someone else’s draft builds evaluative skill without exposing live classmates).

**Sequencing with feedback**

- Clarify learning intentions and success criteria **before** expecting feedback to move learning forward (EEF “lay foundations”).
- Pair exemplar study with a **revision plan** or feedback-response section so comparison leads to action.

These moves reinforce Sadler’s three conditions (standard, comparison, action) during production, not only after grading.

## Practical Authoring Workflow

1. **Clarify the construct** — What evidence should this task produce? Which criteria are fuzzy vs sharp?
2. **Draft success criteria** — Start from learning intentions; keep indicators observable.
3. **Collect candidate samples** — Prior student work (with permission), teacher-constructed models, or discipline-trusted anchors.
4. **Select and tier** — Choose samples that illustrate distinct quality levels or distinct valid approaches.
5. **Annotate for criteria** — Mark features that exemplify (or miss) each criterion; note moves, not only outcomes.
6. **Add learner-facing protocols** — Comparison questions, peer review steps, self-check, revision planning.
7. **Pair with scoring tools** — Align rubric rows or checklist items to annotations so feedback stays consistent.
8. **Pilot with learners** — Watch for mimicry, intimidation, or misread standards; adjust number of exemplars and annotation depth.
9. **Revise after teaching** — Update anchors when task demands or standards shift; document version notes for teams.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checklist

Before publishing or reusing a success/exemplar pack, review:

**Purpose and alignment**

- [ ] Linked to a **specific task** and learning goal, not generic platitudes.
- [ ] Success criteria match what the rubric or assessment claims to value.
- [ ] Reference level (threshold / proficient / exemplary) is explicit.

**Exemplar set design**

- [ ] More than one strong example **when multiple valid forms exist**.
- [ ] Annotations explain **why**, tied to named criteria.
- [ ] Weak samples, if used, are **anonymous** and used for diagnosis + revision paths.
- [ ] Materials support **comparison**, not passive reading.

**Learner use**

- [ ] Usable **before or during** production for self-monitoring.
- [ ] Includes **action** (revision move, goal, or feedback request).
- [ ] Peer protocols, if any, protect dignity and focus on work features.

**Fairness and access**

- [ ] Examples do not rely on unstated cultural knowledge without support.
- [ ] Formats are accessible (UDL: perception, language clarity, multiple media where helpful); language level fits the cohort.
- [ ] Comparison tasks offer more than one way to demonstrate analysis of the model when the construct allows.
- [ ] Exemplars do not imply a single demographic “ideal” performer unless the task requires it.

**System fit**

- [ ] Not redundant with a rubric alone; adds **concrete instantiation**.
- [ ] Coherent with feedback and revision plans (EEF: learners can **act** on the standard).
- [ ] For projects: connects to **critique/revision** and product audience where relevant.

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|---|---|---|
| Model answer repository | No criteria, no comparison, encourages copying | Criteria + annotated range + revision protocol |
| Single “perfect” essay on the wall | Implies one shape; intimidates | Multiple strong samples + success criteria |
| Rubric handed out alone | Tacit quality stays tacit for novices | Rubric + anchored samples at key levels |
| Exemplars after grades only | Misses self-monitoring during production | Use anchors before/during drafting |
| Unlabeled teacher jargon | Learners cannot map terms to work | Define terms; show in annotations |
| Public “bad example” | Harmful classroom culture | Anonymous non-exemplar + fix strategies |
| Checklist with no models | Hard to picture composite quality | Pair checklist with brief exemplar clips or excerpts |
| Exemplars from a different task | False alignment | Parallel task or adjust criteria explicitly |

## How Frameworks Influence This Family (Without Becoming The Resource)

| Lens | Influence on exemplar materials |
|---|---|
| **Formative assessment** | Exemplars serve eliciting understanding of success criteria and closing gaps—not only summative proof. |
| **Self-regulated learning / metacognition** | Comparison prompts and self-assessment against anchors build monitoring and strategy use. |
| **Universal Design for Learning (CAST)** | Multiple means to **represent** quality (text, audio, video, annotated features); options for how learners **demonstrate** comparison and revision; accessible exemplar media so models do not add construct-irrelevant barriers. |
| **Project-based learning** | Public products, critique, and iteration cycles require **process** and **product** exemplars tied to authentic standards. |
| **Standards-based / criterion-referenced assessment** | Anchors express absolute standards; avoid implying quality is only “better than the classmate next to you.” |

These lenses inform design choices; they are not substitutes for tangible success criteria, models, and annotated exemplars.

## Relationships To Other Resource Families (Quick Reference)

- **Criteria and scoring materials** define the judgment scheme; **success/exemplar materials** show what the scheme looks like in real work. Build in parallel. For IB-style best-fit or markscheme language, start in criteria-scoring; instantiate here.
- **Assessment instruments** package tasks, administration, and scoring; qualification-style **anchor sets** often belong in exemplar materials but must align with the instrument blueprint when one exists.
- **Feedback and revision materials** should reference the same success criteria and anchors so comments are actionable.
- **Writing-task materials** often need **genre-specific** model packs and non-exemplars for common structural errors.
- **Project/performance materials** need **milestone exemplars** (plans, drafts, products) and critique-linked revision, not only final showcase pieces.
- **Question/item sets** sometimes include **exemplar responses** for constructed-response items; keep item-level models distinct from whole-task genre exemplars unless intentionally aligned.
- **Study/revision materials** may reuse condensed success criteria for exam prep; distinguish **learning-oriented** exemplars from **test-taking** templates.

---

## Limits Of Inference

- Exemplars show **instances** of quality; they do not prove a learner can produce independently without practice.
- Anchors calibrated for one cohort or year may **drift** as tasks change; re-norm periodically.
- Heavy use of teacher-built models can feel inauthentic in some disciplines; balance with **student-generated** anchors when safe and instructive.
- Complex creative work cannot be fully automated into checklists; retain space for professional judgment and dialogue.
