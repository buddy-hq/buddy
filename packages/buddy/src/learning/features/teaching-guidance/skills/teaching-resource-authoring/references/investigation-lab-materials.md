# Investigation And Lab Materials

A standalone authoring handbook for teachers and instructional designers who create, review, or improve tangible materials that structure science and engineering investigations: lab sheets, observation and data tables, inquiry planners, claim–evidence–reasoning scaffolds, experiment-design templates, fieldwork forms, and related investigation artifacts.

This family is anchored in official science-standards traditions—especially three-dimensional learning and NGSS-aligned task quality—supplemented by performance-assessment, accessibility, and extended-inquiry guidance where investigations stretch across days or culminate in public products.

---

## What This Family Is And What Job It Performs

Investigation and lab materials are **student- and teacher-facing artifacts that organize evidence-generating activity and explanation-building**. They do not replace instruction; they make the investigation legible, repeatable, and reviewable.

The pedagogical job has four linked parts:

1. **Focus the work** on making sense of a phenomenon or designing a solution to a problem—not on completing a recipe or filling blanks.
2. **Make three-dimensional learning visible** by requiring students to use science and engineering practices together with disciplinary core ideas and crosscutting concepts.
3. **Elicit observable evidence** of what students can do and understand, not only whether they got a predetermined answer.
4. **Support reasoning and revision** so data collection leads to claims, explanations, models, or design decisions that can be evaluated against criteria.

Proficiency in science, according to the NGSS framing drawn from the National Research Council Framework, means being able to investigate and build models and theories—not merely recall facts. Investigation materials should therefore treat practices as **knowledge-rich actions**, not isolated procedural skills.

## Artifacts In This Family

### Core artifacts

| Artifact type | Primary function |
|---|---|
| **Lab sheets / investigation guides** | Step students through an investigation while preserving space for predictions, observations, reasoning, and revision |
| **Inquiry planners** | Help students or classes plan questions, variables, methods, data collection, and analysis before or during an investigation |
| **Observation and data tables** | Structure systematic recording of measurements, counts, qualitative observations, and repeated trials |
| **Experiment-design templates** | Scaffold identification of questions, variables, controls, procedures, and fair-test logic |
| **Claim–evidence–reasoning (CER) sheets** | Organize written or oral arguments that connect claims to evidence and scientific reasoning |
| **Fieldwork forms** | Guide outdoor or community-based data collection, sampling, mapping, or environmental observation |
| **Engineering design packets** | Frame problem definition, criteria and constraints, prototype testing, and iterative redesign |
| **Investigation task cards / station prompts** | Short, focused investigation prompts within a larger lesson or unit |
| **Safety and materials lists** | Specify hazards, PPE, disposal, and equipment—usually embedded in or attached to lab materials |
| **Investigation review / screening checklists** | Author-facing or peer-review tools for checking whether a draft investigation meets quality criteria |

### Teacher-facing companion pieces (still in family when bundled)

- Teacher notes on expected student performances, anticipated misconceptions, and facilitation moves
- Differentiation guidance for reading, writing, representation, or participation supports
- Brief rubrics or scoring guidance tied to investigation products
- Formative checkpoint prompts embedded in the investigation sequence

## Nearby Artifacts That Belong Elsewhere

Keep these separate even when they accompany a lab:

| Nearby family | Examples to keep separate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Practice materials / worksheets** | Vocabulary drills, calculation practice, label-a-diagram sheets with no investigation demand | Practice consolidates skill; investigation generates and interprets evidence |
| **Assessment instruments** | End-of-unit tests, benchmark exams, standalone quizzes | Whole instruments judge broader outcome sets; investigation sheets are usually one performance context |
| **Criteria / scoring materials** | Full analytic rubrics, moderation protocols, grade-band descriptors | Rubrics judge quality; lab sheets structure the task. Link them, but do not collapse them |
| **Project / performance materials** | Multi-week project briefs, exhibition guides, milestone calendars for public products | Extended project systems span many artifact types; a single lab sheet is not a project brief |
| **Discussion / collaboration materials** | Seminar protocols, talk stems, debate briefs | Talk scaffolds are distinct unless the investigation sheet itself is primarily a discourse protocol |
| **Writing-task materials** | Standalone essay prompts, research-paper assignments | A CER section inside a lab is in-family; a separate formal lab report assignment may belong in writing-task materials |
| **Worked-example materials** | Annotated sample lab reports with faded solutions | Worked examples model performance; investigation sheets elicit it |
| **Diagnostic instruments** | Misconception probes with no investigation pathway | Diagnostics locate gaps; investigations apply and extend understanding |

**Boundary rule:** If the main job is to **collect and reason about empirical evidence** in service of sense-making or design, it belongs here. If the main job is **practice, test, talk, or grade**, route to the adjacent family.

## When This Family Is Useful

Use investigation and lab materials when learners need to:

- **Explain phenomena** using observations, models, and scientific principles rather than being told the explanation first
- **Design solutions** to real or realistic problems using engineering practices and science knowledge together
- **Develop investigation competence**: asking questions, planning methods, carrying out tests, analyzing data, constructing explanations, arguing from evidence, obtaining and evaluating information
- **Make thinking visible** through written, oral, pictorial, or kinesthetic artifacts that show reasoning—not just final answers
- **Receive formative feedback** during an investigation because the materials elicit performances teachers can interpret
- **Work across modalities**—hands-on, simulated, media-based, or field-based—while preserving evidence quality

This family is especially strong in **K–12 science**, **STEM courses with inquiry components**, **IB or similar programmes with internal investigation requirements**, and **project-like units** where sustained inquiry replaces single-period confirmation activities.

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit Or Commonly Misused

### Poor fit

- **Pure content coverage** where no empirical question, phenomenon, or design problem anchors the activity
- **Procedural training only**—e.g., how to use a balance—without a sense-making purpose
- **High-stakes summative judging** based on a single short lab with no validity evidence; use a fuller assessment instrument instead
- **Non-empirical tasks** better handled as reading, writing, or discussion materials
- **Subjects without investigable questions** unless reframed as model-based reasoning or design with explicit evidence standards

### Common misuses

| Misuse | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Cookbook lab** | Students follow steps to a known outcome; no genuine question, variable logic, or explanation demand |
| **Observation without interpretation** | Data tables filled in, but no claim, model, explanation, or design decision required |
| **Phenomenon as decoration** | An engaging demo opens the class, then instruction proceeds unchanged; the phenomenon does not drive the lesson |
| **Dimensions taught in isolation** | Separate "methods week" and "content week" instead of integrated three-dimensional performances |
| **Right-answer scoring only** | Correct numerical result treated as mastery without examining reasoning or use of practices |
| **One-size data table** | Columns preset for expected results, removing need to plan measurement or represent uncertainty |
| **Missing safety or equity checks** | Hands-on work planned without hazard review, access alternatives, or language supports |
| **CER as fill-in-the-blank** | Formulaic sentence frames with no genuine evidence selection or scientific reasoning |
| **Over-scaffolded inquiry** | Planner so prescriptive that students never decide variables, methods, or analysis choices |

## Strongest Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

### 1. NGSS three-dimensional learning (core)

The official NGSS implementation framing defines science proficiency as integrating:

- **Dimension 1 — Science and engineering practices (SEPs):** Behaviors such as asking questions, planning investigations, analyzing data, constructing explanations, engaging in argument from evidence, and designing solutions. These are not mere skills; each practice requires domain knowledge.
- **Dimension 2 — Crosscutting concepts (CCCs):** Patterns; cause and effect; scale, proportion, and quantity; systems and system models; energy and matter; structure and function; stability and change. These should be **explicit** in materials because they help students connect ideas across topics.
- **Dimension 3 — Disciplinary core ideas (DCIs):** Focus concepts in physical sciences, life sciences, earth and space sciences, and engineering, technology, and applications of science.

**Design contribution:** Every investigation artifact should make clear which practices, concepts, and core ideas students are using together—not listed separately on a cover page, but required in the task itself.

**Critical distinction:** Scientific inquiry begins with a **question** answerable through investigation; engineering design begins with a **problem** solvable through design. Materials should use the appropriate framing.

### 2. EQuIP Rubric for Science and NGSS lesson screening (core)

The EQuIP suite provides criterion-based review for lessons and units aligned to NGSS. Its purposes include reviewing existing materials for revision, giving developers feedback, identifying quality examples, and informing new design.

The **NGSS Lesson Screener** offers a faster, informal check for multi-period lessons. It is **not** sufficient for full vetting; the full EQuIP rubric or PEEC (for year-long materials) is needed for comprehensive review.

**Design contribution for investigation materials:**

- **Phenomena or problems drive the work.** The lesson or investigation should support students making sense of a phenomenon or designing solutions—not treating explanation as an afterthought or enrichment.
- **Three dimensions integrate in student performances.** Tasks should require students to use SEPs, DCIs, and CCCs together; artifacts should show direct, observable evidence of that integration.
- **Student ideas are elicited and refined.** Materials should provide opportunities to express, clarify, justify, interpret, and represent ideas and respond to feedback.
- **Relevance and authenticity matter.** Student questions, prior experiences, and community contexts should motivate sense-making where appropriate.
- **Monitoring student progress is designed in.** Assessments aligned to three-dimensional learning should be embedded, with rubrics or scoring guidance that help teachers interpret performances and give feedback.

EQuIP organizes review around three columns: alignment to NGSS, instructional supports, and monitoring student progress. Investigation authors should internalize the **monitoring** column as a design checklist, not only a post-hoc review.

### 3. NGSS instruction and assessment resources (core)

The NGSS instruction-and-assessment hub aggregates tools that connect classroom investigations to assessment design:

- **Evidence Statements** — Observable, measurable components that clarify what meeting a performance expectation can look like
- **Classroom Sample Tasks** — Illustrations of integrated science, engineering, and mathematics tasks
- **Example Bundles and storylines** — Models of coherent investigation sequences
- **Phenomena resources** — Guidance on choosing and using phenomena to drive instruction
- **Framework for evaluating cognitive complexity in science assessments** — Helps authors gauge demand level of investigation prompts

**Design contribution:** Use evidence statements and sample tasks to calibrate what "good performance" looks like before writing student sheets. Anchor investigations in **phenomena** students can experience firsthand or through media.

### 4. PBLWorks Gold Standard PBL (supporting)

When investigations extend over days or weeks and resemble projects, PBLWorks distinguishes **main-course** inquiry from **dessert** projects added after content coverage. PBLWorks publishes two complementary Gold Standard models on separate pages—do not conflate them:

| Model | Official page | What it governs | Role in investigation/lab authoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Essential project design elements** | [Gold Standard Project Design](https://www.pblworks.org/what-is-pbl/gold-standard-project-design) | What strong **student-facing** investigation packets must embody | Driving question, sustained inquiry milestones, authenticity, choice, reflection, critique/revision, public product |
| **Project based teaching practices** | [Gold Standard Teaching Practices](https://www.pblworks.org/what-is-pbl/gold-standard-teaching-practices) | What **teacher-facing** notes and checkpoints must support while students use the materials | Design/plan, standards alignment, culture, activity management, scaffolding, assessment, engage/coach |

**Design elements** (student materials) relevant to extended investigations:

- Challenging problem or question
- Sustained inquiry
- Authenticity
- Student voice and choice
- Reflection
- Critique and revision
- Public product

**Teaching practices** (teacher notes, planners, facilitation prompts) that investigation authors should support—not only student handouts:

| Practice | Implication for investigation/lab materials |
|---|---|
| **Design and plan** | Launch, checkpoints, resource lists, and culminating evidence expectations; room for student voice within safety and standards bounds |
| **Align to standards** | Explicit map from investigation prompts to targeted SEP/DCI/CCC elements or performance expectations |
| **Build culture** | Norms for productive struggle, peer critique, and safe risk-taking during open inquiry |
| **Manage activities** | Calendars, grouping, material staging, and “making work public” steps for data and explanations |
| **Scaffold student learning** | Mini-lessons, templates, and language supports so all students can plan methods and argue from evidence |
| **Assess student learning** | Formative checkpoints, aligned criteria references, self/peer review on methods and claims |
| **Engage and coach** | Conference prompts for when to teach a technique vs when students should revise a method productively |

**Design contribution:** Longer inquiry planners, field studies, and design challenges benefit from milestone structures, revision cycles, and audience beyond the teacher—without replacing three-dimensional science criteria. For **single-period or short-cycle labs**, use design elements sparingly; still borrow **scaffold** and **assess** practices in teacher notes when inquiry level is guided or open.

### 5. Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center rubric guidance (supporting)

The Eberly Center describes rubrics as tools that divide work into components with levels of mastery, usable for grading and/or formative feedback. Engineering design and project rubrics exemplify how investigation products can be judged consistently.

**Design contribution:** Pair investigation sheets with **criteria students can see before submitting work**. Track component scores to identify which practices or concepts need reteaching.

### 6. CAST UDL Guidelines (supporting lens)

UDL is a design lens, not an investigation artifact. CAST published **Guidelines 3.0** in 2024 with expanded sub-structure and revised numbering; the three high-level categories—engagement, representation, and action/expression—remain the organizing lens for investigation-material design.

**Design contribution for lab materials:**

- Multiple ways to **engage** with the phenomenon or problem
- Multiple ways to **represent** data, models, and observations
- Multiple ways to **express** explanations and design decisions
- Graduated supports so students can practice investigation skills with decreasing scaffolding

### 7. IB assessment documentation (background; programme-specific)

IB programme documentation describes assessment that seeks **direct evidence** of achievement through varied task types—including **fieldwork, lab work, investigations, and artistic or design performances**—and emphasizes **standards-based judgment** rather than rank ordering alone. Internal assessment and inquiry contexts often require extended investigation artifacts with explicit criteria, moderation, and documented teacher judgment.

**Design contribution:** Useful for **extended investigations**, **moderation-minded scoring**, and **performance tasks** that must meet external standards. Subject-specific internal assessment (for example sciences IA, ESS fieldwork, design technology investigations) has **task-specific criteria, word limits, and ethical rules** that override generic lab-sheet advice—always verify the current **subject guide** and **teacher support material** for the programme and year.

| Resource | URL | How to use in authoring |
|---|---|---|
| *Assessment principles and practices—Quality assessments in a digital age* (IB, 2018) | https://ibo.org/contentassets/1cdf850e366447e99b5a862aab622883/assessment-principles-and-practices-2018-en.pdf | General principles: formative/summative balance, validity, marks vs grades, moderation—**not** IA task templates |
| Diploma Programme — Assessment and exams (web) | https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/assessment-and-exams/ | Starting point for current programme policy links |
| Subject guides and IA criteria | IB programme resource centre (school login) | Authoritative for investigation wording, ethics, and scoring descriptors |

Do not reproduce proprietary IB criteria tables, past papers, or IA exemplars in new materials. Paraphrase design principles only.

### 8. WWC mathematics intervention guidance (background)

The WWC guide for elementary mathematics intervention emphasizes systematic instruction, concise mathematical language, and concrete/semi-concrete representations.

**Design contribution:** Transferable to **STEM data representation**, **measurement instruction**, and **quantitative analysis sections** inside science investigations—not as lab-specific authority, but as representation and language guidance.

## Safety, Compliance, And Duty Of Care

Hands-on and field investigations carry **legal, ethical, and professional** obligations that NGSS task-quality tools do not fully specify. Lab sheets and teacher notes must make hazards and controls visible; district policy and local law remain authoritative over any handbook summary.

### What investigation materials should include

- **Hazard identification** for chemicals, heat, sharps, electricity, pressure, organisms, lasers, and outdoor terrain—aligned to the actual activity, not generic boilerplate.
- **Controls:** PPE, engineering controls (fume hood, barriers), substitution or micro-scale alternatives, and **stop conditions** when something unexpected occurs.
- **Roles:** who may run demonstrations vs student trials; supervision ratios for high-risk work.
- **Preparation:** technician prep room workflows, labeled storage, incompatible chemical separation, and inventory discipline.
- **Disposal and spill response** pointers or references to school protocols—not improvised student disposal.
- **Documentation:** safety acknowledgment forms where your jurisdiction expects them; incident reporting paths for teachers.
- **Equity and access:** alternatives when a student cannot perform a step safely (different role, simulation with explicit limits, teacher-collected data with student analysis)—without removing the student’s obligation to **reason about evidence**.

### Authoritative safety traditions (consult; do not copy long protocols)

| Source | URL | Use in authoring |
|---|---|---|
| **NSTA — Safety resources hub** | https://www.nsta.org/safety | Position statements on school science safety and educator liability; links to minimum safety practices, chemical hygiene officer guidance, overcrowding, PPE, and issue papers |
| **NSTA — Safety position statements** (linked from safety hub) | https://www.nsta.org/safety | Programme-level expectations for training, environments, and educator liability |
| **ACS — Chemical safety for high school** (video series and rubrics) | Linked from NSTA safety hub | Mindset, SDS literacy, dress for lab, emergencies, RAMP framing |
| **State / national regulations** | Local education agency and workplace safety codes | Supersede generic handbook advice |

NSTA explicitly notes that its resources **do not supersede** school, district, state, or federal law—authors and administrators retain **duty of care**.

### Compliance checks before publishing a lab sheet

- [ ] Activity reviewed against current **school science safety policy** and technician sign-off where required
- [ ] Chemical quantities and organisms match **approved lists** for the grade band
- [ ] Demonstrations with fire, pressure, or concentrated acids follow **restricted demonstration** rules where applicable
- [ ] Fieldwork includes **consent, weather, water safety, and biological sampling** ethics as relevant
- [ ] Digital or hybrid variants (below) do not silently introduce hazards (home kitchens, unsupervised burns, unvetted online labs)

## Key Design Decisions Authors Must Make

### 1. Phenomenon-centered or problem-centered framing

**Decision:** Is the anchor a natural phenomenon to explain or an engineering problem to solve?

- **Phenomenon path:** Students experience or encounter something puzzling, develop questions, investigate, and build explanations.
- **Problem path:** Students define criteria and constraints, generate solutions, test prototypes, and iterate.

**Authoring rule:** State the anchor clearly at the top of the material. Avoid switching frames mid-sheet without signaling the shift.

### 2. Level of student agency in planning

**Decision:** How much investigation design do students own?

| Level | When appropriate | Material features |
|---|---|---|
| **Structured inquiry** | Introducing a practice, limited time, safety constraints | Pre-selected question, suggested method, focused data table |
| **Guided inquiry** | Developing competence | Students choose some variables, measurement approach, or analysis path within bounds |
| **Open inquiry** | Advanced students, extended time, strong safety culture | Student-generated questions, methods, and evaluation criteria with teacher checkpoints |

Scaffolding should **fade** across a unit or course, not stay fixed at one level indefinitely.

### 3. Which three-dimensional elements are targeted

**Decision:** Which SEP elements, DCI elements, and CCC elements must appear in student work?

- Select **grade-appropriate elements**, not colloquial labels like "asking questions" without specifying the learning target.
- Require **integration**: a task that could be completed using only vocabulary recall is not a three-dimensional investigation task.
- For engineering, ensure science DCIs from physical, life, or earth/space sciences connect to engineering design core ideas when solving design problems.

### 4. Evidence types the material will elicit

**Decision:** What student artifacts prove learning?

Strong investigation materials elicit more than numerical answers:

- Predictions with reasoning
- Annotated diagrams or models
- Data tables with units, trial counts, and notes on variation
- Graphs or visual summaries
- Written or oral arguments linking claim, evidence, and reasoning
- Reflection on method quality and revisions
- Design iterations with test results

**Authoring rule:** For each section, ask what a teacher could **observe** in the artifact if the student understands the targeted ideas.

### 5. Cognitive demand and complexity

**Decision:** Is the task appropriately challenging for the grade band and prior knowledge?

Use NGSS assessment-complexity resources to avoid tasks that are either trivial confirmations or impossibly open-ended without supports. Match reading load, measurement precision, and abstraction to the intended audience.

### 6. Formative versus summative role

**Decision:** Is this sheet primarily for learning during instruction or for judging attainment at the end?

- **Formative:** Embed checkpoints, self-assessment, revision prompts, and teacher feedback spaces.
- **Summative:** Ensure the task still requires integration of dimensions; provide rubrics aligned to the targeted performances; avoid "one number = mastery" shortcuts.

Many high-quality units include **both**, clearly labeled.

### 7. Accessibility, language, and safety

**Decision:** Who might be excluded if the material stays in one format?

Plan for:

- Vocabulary front-loading or multilingual supports where needed
- Alternative representations for data (tables, graphs, sketches, digital tools)
- Roles that do not assume equal physical access to every manipulation
- Explicit safety instructions, hazard symbols, and disposal notes for hands-on work
- Unbiased contexts, examples, and methods

EQuIP instructional-support criteria explicitly call for differentiation connected to students' languages, reading levels, and community contexts.

### 8. Hands-on, simulated, virtual, or hybrid evidence

**Decision:** Will students gather evidence **in person**, through **simulation or virtual lab**, through **teacher demonstration plus student analysis**, or through a **hybrid** mix?

| Mode | When appropriate | Material requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **Hands-on (preferred when safe and equitable)** | Standards expect students to plan and carry out investigations; equipment and supervision are available | Full planner + data table + method revision; safety section required |
| **Simulation / virtual lab** | Concept focus, hazard substitution, or access limits; tool is **fit for the targeted practice** | Label what is **modeled vs measured**; require students to critique model assumptions, limitations, and variable logic—not only read simulated outputs |
| **Demonstration + student analysis** | Time, safety, or equipment constraints; still teaching argument from evidence | Teacher notes document what was demonstrated; students predict, observe media/recording, or analyze shared class data; individual reasoning still required |
| **Hybrid** | Partial class hands-on plus remote data (citizen science, probeware stream, field video) | Clear provenance of each data source; synchronization rules; equity plan for students absent from the physical component |

NGSS phenomena guidance treats **firsthand experience** as ideal when feasible, but **high-quality representations** (video, simulations, datasets) can anchor sense-making when designed so students still **question, investigate, and explain**. Virtual modes fail when they become **click-through verification** with no planning, uncertainty, or explanation demand.

### 9. Coherence within a sequence

**Decision:** How does this investigation build on prior lessons and toward performance expectations?

Single-period labs can introduce elements of three-dimensional learning, but **units** should show how investigations accumulate toward mastery of performance expectations. Reference prior knowledge in all three dimensions, not only DCIs.

## Practical Authoring Workflow

### Phase 1 — Anchor and align

1. **Choose the phenomenon or problem** students will make sense of or solve. Prefer firsthand experience or high-quality representations.
2. **Identify the target performance expectation(s)** or equivalent learning goals.
3. **Select SEP, DCI, and CCC elements** at appropriate grade granularity.
4. **Consult evidence statements or sample tasks** to clarify what successful performance can look like.
5. **Decide inquiry level** (structured, guided, open) and duration.

### Phase 2 — Design the performance task

6. **Draft the student-facing challenge** in plain language: What must students figure out or design?
7. **Map elicited artifacts** to each targeted dimension.
8. **Design data-collection structures** that require planning—columns not fully pre-filled unless pedagogically intentional.
9. **Build in explanation or design decisions** before, during, and after data collection—not only at the end.
10. **Add revision opportunities** where initial models or designs should change in light of new evidence.

### Phase 3 — Scaffold without removing thinking

11. **Add checkpoints** for predictions, method justification, mid-investigation reflection, and argument drafting.
12. **Include vocabulary and representation supports** without replacing student reasoning.
13. **Write teacher notes** on common misconceptions, expected partial understandings, and facilitation questions.
14. **Attach or reference criteria** students will be judged against.

### Phase 4 — Safety, access, and logistics

15. **Review hazards, materials, timing, grouping, and cleanup.**
16. **Plan alternatives** for students who cannot perform every physical step.
17. **Check reading level, visual clarity, and language demands** against the class profile.

### Phase 5 — Review with quality tools

18. **Run the NGSS Lesson Screener** (or equivalent quick check) if the investigation spans multiple periods.
19. **Apply EQuIP-aligned questions**, especially for alignment, instructional supports, and monitoring student progress.
20. **Pilot with sample student responses** or colleague review; revise scaffolds that over- or under-constrain thinking.

### Phase 6 — Iterate from evidence

21. **Collect student artifacts** from implementation.
22. **Note where students succeeded or struggled** on each targeted element.
23. **Revise prompts, tables, and criteria** for the next iteration.

## Artifact-Specific Authoring Notes

### Lab sheets and investigation guides

A strong lab sheet usually includes:

- **Purpose framed as sense-making or design**, not "to learn about X"
- **Pre-lab prediction or initial model** with reasoning prompt
- **Materials and safety** where relevant
- **Procedure area** that leaves room for student planning when appropriate
- **Data/Observation section** with units, trials, and anomaly notes
- **Analysis prompts** that require mathematical, graphical, or logical processing tied to the phenomenon
- **Explanation or argument section** connecting results to the anchor phenomenon or design criteria
- **Reflection** on method quality, sources of error, or next redesign steps

Avoid numbering steps so rigidly that students cannot justify choices or recognize failed tests as informative.

### Inquiry planners

Inquiry planners help before or during investigation. Useful fields:

- Question or problem statement
- Background knowledge and related concepts
- Variables (independent, dependent, controlled) or criteria/constraints for design
- Method or prototype plan with rationale
- Data to collect and how it will be recorded
- How results will be analyzed and interpreted
- How findings will be communicated

Planners should feed directly into the lab sheet or presentation product, not live as a separate bureaucratic form.

### Observation and data tables

Good tables:

- Label variables, units, and conditions clearly
- Support repeated trials and qualitative plus quantitative data where needed
- Include space for unexpected results
- Prompt students to note measurement precision or observation limitations

Weak tables preset all expected values, turning investigation into verification.

### Claim–evidence–reasoning scaffolds

CER-style organizers support the practice of **engaging in argument from evidence**. Effective scaffolds:

- Require students to **select** relevant evidence, not copy all data
- Separate **claim** (answer to the question), **evidence** (data or observations), and **reasoning** (scientific principles connecting them)
- Allow revision when new evidence appears
- Accept multiple valid claims when evidence supports them, with criteria for quality of reasoning

Avoid reducing CER to a three-sentence template with no content decisions.

### Experiment-design templates

Templates should make **fair testing** and **variable logic** explicit:

- What is being tested and why
- What stays the same and what changes
- How measurements will be taken
- How sample size or trial count supports confidence
- How conclusions will be drawn

### Fieldwork forms

Field investigations add:

- Location, time, weather, and sampling protocol
- Maps, transects, or observation protocols
- Ethical and safety considerations for working outdoors or with living systems
- Connection to local or community contexts where appropriate

### Engineering design packets

Engineering materials should reflect that design differs from scientific inquiry:

- Define problem, criteria, and constraints
- Research and brainstorm solutions
- Plan and build prototypes
- Test, evaluate against criteria, and iterate
- Communicate the solution and tradeoffs

Trial-and-error activities without science or engineering knowledge do not meet the engineering standard of practice.

### Virtual, simulation, and hybrid investigation sheets

When the spine of the work is **not** fully hands-on:

- State **learning target practices** explicitly (for example planning investigations, analyzing data, constructing explanations)—and which parts the simulation cannot elicit.
- Require **comparison** between model behavior and real-world expectations where possible (even a short “limitation” prompt).
- If students cannot manipulate apparatus, scaffold **method critique**: what would they change if they could run the physical trial?
- For **online lab platforms**, cite the tool in teacher notes; verify privacy, accessibility, and whether the platform allows export of student reasoning artifacts.
- For **at-home or hybrid kits**, separate **safe** take-home observations from prohibited hazardous tasks; never relocate high-risk procedures without supervisor oversight.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

Use this checklist when authoring or reviewing investigation materials. It synthesizes EQuIP, NGSS lesson screener, and three-dimensional learning criteria without reproducing proprietary rubric text.

### Alignment and anchoring

- [ ] A phenomenon or problem **drives** the investigation; explanation or design is the goal, not a post-hoc label
- [ ] Students encounter the phenomenon or problem **directly** or through suitable media
- [ ] Targeted SEP, DCI, and CCC **elements** are grade-appropriate and named at useful specificity
- [ ] Student performances **integrate** the three dimensions; isolated skill drills are not mislabeled as investigations
- [ ] Engineering tasks connect science DCIs with engineering design ideas when applicable

### Instructional supports

- [ ] Scenario feels **authentic** and purposeful, not contrived solely for worksheet completion
- [ ] Materials use **accurate, grade-appropriate** scientific information and representations
- [ ] Students can **express, clarify, justify, and revise** ideas in oral and/or written forms
- [ ] The investigation **builds on prior knowledge** in practices, concepts, and core ideas—not only vocabulary from yesterday's lecture
- [ ] **Differentiation** is planned: language, reading, representation, extension, and extra support paths exist where needed
- [ ] **Safety and ethical** considerations are addressed for hands-on or field work (hazards, PPE, disposal, supervision—not generic warnings only)
- [ ] **Virtual or hybrid** modes (if used) label modeled vs observed evidence and still require planning, analysis, and explanation
- [ ] **Qualification routes** (if applicable): apparatus/technique coverage or board records are traceable without reducing the task to recipe completion

### Evidence and assessment embedded in the material

- [ ] Tasks elicit **direct, observable evidence** of three-dimensional performances
- [ ] **Formative checkpoints** appear throughout, not only at the end
- [ ] Expected responses require **reasoning**, not only numeric answers or copied definitions
- [ ] **Rubrics or scoring guidance** align to the targeted dimensions and help teachers plan next steps
- [ ] Methods, vocabulary, and examples are **accessible and unbiased** for the intended student population
- [ ] Students have **multiple opportunities** to demonstrate performance and improve after feedback

### Coherence and sequence (for multi-period investigations)

- [ ] Investigations **connect** across lessons toward performance expectations or unit goals
- [ ] Crosscutting concepts help **link** ideas within and across investigations
- [ ] Mathematics and literacy demands are **intentional**, not accidental barriers

### Extended inquiry additions (when applicable)

- [ ] Sustained inquiry with milestones replaces one-shot confirmation
- [ ] Students have meaningful **choice** where appropriate
- [ ] **Critique and revision** cycles improve methods and products
- [ ] A **public or authentic audience** receives the finding or design when the unit demands it

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| **Verification lab** ("prove the book is right") | Start from a puzzling phenomenon; allow outcomes that force explanation revision |
| **Hook-only phenomenon** | Let the phenomenon structure the entire investigation sequence |
| **Lab report as transcription** | Require analysis, argument, or design evaluation—not only copied procedure and data |
| **Dimension checklist cover page** | Integrate practices, concepts, and core ideas in prompts and expected responses |
| **Single correct graph shape** | Ask students to choose representations and justify whether patterns support the claim |
| **Group lab, individual grade with no roles** | Define collaborative contributions and individual accountability for reasoning |
| **No error analysis** | Prompt students to interpret anomalies, uncertainty, and method limitations |
| **Investigation without criteria** | Pair materials with observable criteria or rubric components |
| **Accessibility as afterthought** | Build multiple representations and response modes into the initial design |
| **Screener score as certification** | Use quick screeners for direction; use full EQuIP or peer review for quality claims |

## How Frameworks Influence The Resource Without Becoming The Resource

| Lens | Role in investigation authoring | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-dimensional learning (NGSS / NRC Framework)** | Defines what integrated science performances look like in materials | Not a lab sheet by itself |
| **EQuIP / lesson screening** | Quality review protocol for alignment, supports, and assessment | Not a student handout |
| **PBL Gold Standard (design + teaching)** | Design elements shape extended student packets; teaching practices shape teacher notes and checkpoints | Not a substitute for science-specific three-dimensional criteria; not the same as a single-period lab screener |
| **Exam-board practical traditions** | AT/PAG coverage, records, written-exam links | Not a substitute for sense-making investigation design |
| **Safety and compliance bodies (e.g., NSTA)** | Duty of care, hazard controls, facility norms | Not a replacement for district policy or technician sign-off |
| **UDL** | Accessibility and variability design overlay | Not a replacement for scientific accuracy or evidence standards |
| **Formative assessment principles** | Emphasizes evidence use during instruction | Not synonymous with any single investigation template |
| **IB / exam-board assessment logic** | Standards-based judgment, extended investigation formats | Not automatically transferable without programme-specific rules |

Apply frameworks during **design and review**. Students should receive **clear, phenomenon- or problem-driven materials**, not framework posters stapled to a cookbook lab.

## Relationships To Nearby Resource Families

| Family | Relationship |
|---|---|
| **Assessment instruments** | Investigation sheets often contain embedded assessment tasks; standalone tests should be authored with assessment-instrument guidance when used for grading decisions |
| **Criteria / scoring materials** | Investigation products frequently need paired rubrics; keep rubrics separate but aligned |
| **Project / performance materials** | Long investigations overlap with projects; use project guidance for milestones and public products, science guidance for three-dimensional performances |
| **Practice materials** | Pre-lab skill practice may precede investigation; do not confuse practice drills with the investigation itself |
| **Writing-task materials** | Formal lab reports or research summaries may be writing tasks; CER sections inside investigations stay here |
| **Discussion materials** | Argumentation may use discussion protocols; when the artifact is primarily a lab or data task, keep it here |
| **Diagnostic materials** | Pre-lab probes can diagnose misconceptions; diagnostics lack the full investigation pathway |
| **Worked-example materials** | Sample investigations with annotations teach method; they do not replace student investigation sheets |

**Common pairing:** Investigation guide + rubric + brief formative exit prompt. **Common sequencing:** Diagnostic probe → investigation planner → lab sheet → CER or report → revision task.

## Reject Or Avoid As Authority Seeds

Do not use these as primary models:

- Cookbook labs from marketplace sites with no sense-making demand
- Generic "scientific method" posters treated as complete investigation design
- Vendor lab kits with no alignment to targeted performances
- AI-generated lab templates with no validity, safety, or fairness review
- Confirmation activities labeled "inquiry" because equipment was used
