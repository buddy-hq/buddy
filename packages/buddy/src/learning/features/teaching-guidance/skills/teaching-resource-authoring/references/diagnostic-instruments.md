# Diagnostic Instruments

## What this family is

Diagnostic instruments are **short, focused artifacts** used to learn where teaching should start, what learners already know, which skills are weak, which misconceptions are active, and whether an intervention is working. They produce **evidence for decisions**, not final judgments of competence.

Typical artifacts include:

- **Screeners** and placement checks (brief, often whole-group, used to flag who needs closer attention)
- **Pretests** and prerequisite checks (before a unit or lesson sequence)
- **Misconception probes** and error-analysis tasks (designed to surface predictable wrong ideas, not only whether an answer is wrong)
- **Diagnostic question sets** (small banks tied to specific constructs or skill components)
- **Interpretation guides** (how to read patterns in responses and what instructional moves they suggest)
- **Progress-monitoring probes** (repeated, comparable measures to track growth during intervention)

The pedagogical job is **decision support**: grouping, reteaching, choosing representations, assigning support tiers, adjusting pace, and checking whether a change in instruction changed learning. A diagnostic that does not connect to a **plausible next step** is incomplete as a resource family member, even if the items are well written.

## Artifacts in the family vs nearby families

| Belongs here | Nearby but separate |
|---|---|
| Pretest on prerequisites for an upcoming unit | End-of-unit summative test |
| Misconception probe after a lesson | General homework problem set |
| Universal reading screener with benchmark cut scores | Full benchmark assessment blueprint |
| Weekly progress-monitoring probe in decoding | Practice worksheet for fluency only |
| Teacher interpretation guide for probe results | Rubric for grading a performance task |
| Student-facing “where I am / my goal” tracker tied to probe data | Generic reflection journal with no evidence link |
| Error-analysis sheet on common wrong approaches | Answer key only |

**Question-item sets** supply individual tasks; **diagnostic instruments** package tasks (or observation protocols) with **purpose, administration logic, scoring or coding rules, and interpretation**. When authoring or reviewing **probe items** (especially selected-response misconception items), use the item-quality rules in `question-item-sets.md`—stems, plausible theory-based distractors, accessibility, and format–construct alignment—then return here for coding bands, cut rules, and instructional pathways. **Assessment instruments** judge broader outcome attainment; diagnostics prioritize **actionable gaps** and often tolerate lower stakes, shorter coverage, and faster turnaround.

**Practice materials** rehearse skills; diagnostics **locate** which skills or ideas need rehearsal. **Feedback and revision materials** respond to work already produced; diagnostics often **precede** or **parallel** instruction to shape what feedback should target.

## When this family is useful

Use diagnostic instruments when:

- Starting a unit, course, or intervention and need to **avoid reteaching what is already secure**
- Learners show uneven performance and you need to **separate knowledge gaps, skill gaps, and misconceptions**
- Implementing **tiered support** or differentiated groups based on evidence, not impression
- Choosing among instructional representations, scaffolds, or intervention paths
- Checking whether an instructional change **moved learning** within days or weeks (progress monitoring)
- Building **learner agency** through transparent goals and visible progress (when student-facing interpretation is included)

Strong diagnostics match **grain size to decision grain size**: a five-minute probe should support a small instructional adjustment; a screener should support coarse routing, not fine-grained placement by itself.

## When this family is a poor fit

Avoid treating diagnostics as the right tool when:

- The goal is **grading, ranking, or high-stakes accountability** with defensible individual scores (use assessment instruments with stronger validity evidence and governance)
- You need **rich, integrative performance** (extended writing, labs, projects)—diagnostics sample constructs; they rarely prove mastery alone
- No time or plan exists to **act on results** (screening without intervention capacity harms trust and can mislabel learners)
- A **single total score** will be over-interpreted for placement (especially across language, disability, or cultural groups without fairness review)
- “Diagnostic” is branding for a **mini final exam** with no interpretation pathway
- Domain experts have not defined **what wrong answers mean** (misconception probes without a coding guide are guesswork)

## Source traditions and what each contributes

### Evidence clearinghouses (primary for classroom practice)

**Using Student Achievement Data to Support Instructional Decision Making** (IES / What Works Clearinghouse) anchors how diagnostics fit an **ongoing cycle of instructional improvement**: collect and prepare evidence from multiple sources; interpret patterns and form **testable hypotheses** about what to change; implement instructional changes; collect new evidence to see whether learning moved. The same guide stresses teaching learners to **examine their own data and set goals**, and warns against using assessment data without interpretation support—especially near proficiency cut scores, where narrow focus can misallocate help.

**Assisting Students Struggling with Reading: Response to Intervention (RtI) and Multi-Tier Intervention in the Primary Grades** (WWC) is the clearest model for **universal screening**, **differentiated core instruction**, **intensive small-group work on a few foundational skills**, and **monthly progress monitoring** for students below benchmark, with escalation when progress is insufficient.

**Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9** (WWC) shows how to **decompose reading difficulty** into decoding (multisyllabic words), fluency, comprehension routines (world knowledge, questioning, gist, monitoring), and **stretch text** practice—useful as a template for skill-specific diagnostics in other subjects.

**Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades** (WWC) identifies intervention dimensions—systematic instruction, mathematical language, representations, number line, word problems, timed activities for fluency—that can structure **math diagnostic probes** and linked supports.

### Professional measurement standards

**Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing** (AERA / APA / NCME) sets non-negotiable constraints: validity is about **intended interpretations for specified uses**, not a single “valid test” label; fairness and accessibility are **central to valid interpretation**; score users must not over-interpret **unstable subscores or single items**; claims about placement or screening require evidence that the **benefit of the decision** matches the claim (e.g., denying access vs providing support). Classroom diagnostics rarely meet full large-scale psychometric bars, but authors should still define **intended use, population, and limits**.

### Feedback and learner regulation (supporting)

**Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning** (Education Endowment Foundation) applies when diagnostic evidence triggers feedback: aim for **clear, actionable** responses tied to the evidence elicited (task- and pupil-dependent), because feedback that misses the mark can harm progress. Diagnostics should make it obvious **what feedback should target**.

**Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning** (EEF) supports **self-monitoring tools** only when learners can connect observations to strategies (plan–monitor–evaluate), not vague reflection prompts.

### Accessibility (supporting lens)

**CAST UDL Guidelines** remind authors to offer **multiple means of representation and action/expression** so diagnostics measure the target construct, not barriers of format, language, or motor demand—unless those are part of the construct.

### Formal assessment programs (light context for cycle logic)

Program assessment principles (e.g., International Baccalaureate *Assessment principles and practices*, open PDF) help when diagnostics sit inside **standards-based systems**: distinguish formative diagnostic use from summative judgment, and keep **criteria alignment** visible. This handbook treats such programme documents as **light supporting context** because classroom diagnostic authoring is grounded primarily in evidence clearinghouses and measurement standards.

## Types of diagnostic instruments

### Screeners

**Purpose:** Quickly identify learners who may need further assessment or support.

**Design features:**

- Brief, standardized administration when possible
- **Benchmarks or cut scores** tied to documented decision rules (what happens to learners above/below)
- Repeat at sensible intervals (e.g., beginning and middle of year for early reading)
- Combine with other evidence before high-impact placement

**Reading example (primary):** Universal screening at least twice yearly; students below benchmark receive intensive instruction on a **small set of foundational skills** in small groups; progress monitored at least monthly for tier-2 students.

### Pretests and prerequisite checks

**Purpose:** Map what learners bring to a **specific upcoming learning sequence**.

**Design features:**

- Target **prerequisites**, not the whole future unit summatively
- Item/cognitive demand aligned to what will be taught next, not only what was taught last year
- Results feed **starting point, pacing, and grouping**—not a permanent label

### Misconception probes and error-analysis tasks

**Purpose:** Reveal **how** learners are thinking, not only whether they are wrong.

**Design features:**

- Items or prompts that **separate** competing wrong ideas (qualitative distractors, explain-your-choice, predict-what-happens-next)
- **Coding guide** for common responses (categories of thinking, not just correct/incorrect)
- Links from each misconception category to **instructional response** (representation, counterexample, structured refutation, practice type)

Without a coding guide, teachers revert to reteaching everything.

### Worked example: misconception probe with interpretation bands

The following sketch shows how the pieces fit together for one **primary decision**—not a copyable item bank. A **Grade 7** teacher starting a **proportional-relationships** unit needs to know whether to reteach **multiplicative comparison** in small groups or move most learners into new ratio work.

**1. Decision and constructs**

- **Decision:** Form two instructional groups for the first two weeks (reteach multiplicative reasoning vs advance with ratio tables).
- **Constructs sampled:** multiplicative vs additive comparison; ratio language in context; reading a double number line (representation).

**2. Sample tasks (original; pilot locally)**

| Task | Elicitation | What weak performance suggests |
|---|---|---|
| A | “A recipe uses 2 cups flour for every 3 cups sugar. Another uses 4 cups flour for 5 cups sugar. Which recipe is sweeter? Explain.” | Additive “difference of 1 cup” reasoning; inability to compare ratios |
| B | MCQ with distractors: treats ratios as equal differences; doubles only one quantity | Documented wrong ideas, not random errors |
| C | “Sketch a double number line for 3 : 5 with one jump labeled.” | Representation gap separate from verbal ratio |

**3. Coding guide (excerpt)**

| Code | Look for in work | Not the same as |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Compares amounts by **difference** only (“5 is 1 more than 4”) | Careless arithmetic |
| M2 | Doubles or halves **one** part of the ratio without preserving the other | General “doesn’t understand fractions” |
| R+ | Correct ratio comparison **and** sensible explanation | Memorized procedure without meaning |
| G0 | Accurate number line with aligned parts | — |

**4. Interpretation bands and actions**

| Band | Pattern on A–C | First-line action | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reteach multiplicative reasoning** | M1 on A and B; C weak or missing | 3–4 small-group sessions: comparison bars, “times as many,” counterexamples to additive shortcuts | Do not label as “low math ability”; snapshot only |
| **Target representation** | R+ on A/B but G0 on C | Short number-line and table bridge before ratio algorithms | Confirm with one homework sample (triangulation) |
| **Advance** | R+ and G0 secure | Enter unit with brief formative check on rates in context | Enrichment if class time allows |

**5. Testable hypothesis (closes the improvement cycle)**

Following the data-use practice guide’s model for **testable hypotheses**: name a specific instructional change and an observable effect you can measure soon.

- **Hypothesis:** “If the reteach group works on comparison-bar and ‘times as many’ tasks for four 20-minute sessions, at least 70% will move from M1 to R+ on a parallel probe (new items, same constructs) administered in week 2.”
- **Cycle position:** administer probe → code with guide → act on bands → **re-administer parallel probe** → compare patterns, not only total score.

If week-2 results do not shift, revise the hypothesis (different representation, language load, or prerequisite fraction work)—do not repeat the same reteach unchanged.

### Interpretation guides

**Purpose:** Turn raw results into **decisions**.

**Minimum contents:**

- What construct(s) each section measures
- How to aggregate or **not** aggregate scores (when subscores are too noisy to interpret)
- Decision thresholds and **recommended actions** (reteach, small group, enrichment, change representation)
- What **not** to conclude (limits of inference)
- How to combine with other data (triangulation)
- Template for **testable instructional hypotheses** (see data-use cycle below)

### Progress-monitoring probes

**Purpose:** Track whether intervention is working **frequently enough** to adjust before time is lost.

**Design features:**

- **Comparable forms** or repeatable tasks at the same difficulty grain
- Schedule matched to tier intensity (e.g., monthly for tier 2 in primary reading)
- Graph or table view of trend, not single snapshots
- Predefined rules for **changing intervention** when slope is flat

## The instructional improvement cycle (backbone for all diagnostics)

WWC’s data-use practice guide describes a cycle that diagnostic authors should embed explicitly:

1. **Collect and prepare** evidence (classroom work, probes, screeners, interim assessments—organized for comparison)
2. **Interpret** and form **hypotheses** about causes and promising instructional changes (often collaboratively)
3. **Act** by changing instruction or support
4. **Re-collect** evidence to test whether the change worked

Hypotheses should be **testable** (e.g., “If we teach subtraction with regrouping-first, interim subtraction scores for this group will improve”) and tied to specific instructional moves. Diagnostics are most valuable when packaged with **where they sit in this cycle** and what evidence will close the loop.

Student-facing diagnostics should include **goal statements, criteria for success, and reflection on evidence**—not only teacher analysis.

## Key design decisions

### 1. Decision and construct target

Define:

- **Decision:** place in sequence, pick intervention, group, reteach topic X, continue or exit intervention
- **Construct:** the smallest useful skill or idea components (e.g., multisyllabic decoding vs general “reading”)
- **Population:** grade, course, language background, prior instruction

Every item should trace to a construct that **changes a decision** if weak.

### 1b. Learning progressions and construct maps

**Learning progressions** describe how understanding of a core idea or practice tends to develop over time—from informal ideas toward more formal, connected, and transferable knowledge. They are not rigid grade-level scripts; they are **hypotheses about order and sophistication** that help authors decide what to probe next and what “weak” looks like at this moment.

For diagnostic authoring, progressions and construct maps support:

- **Decomposing** a broad target (e.g., “fractions,” “ecosystem dynamics,” “linear functions”) into **ordered subskills or ideas** that imply different instructional responses
- **Choosing probe grain**: a screener samples coarse bands; a misconception probe targets a **specific step** where learners commonly stall or revert
- **Writing interpretation bands** that name **qualitative levels** (e.g., informal additive reasoning vs stable multiplicative comparison) rather than only percent correct
- **Sequencing reteach**: if week-2 evidence still shows an earlier progression step, the hypothesis should shift **backward** along the map, not repeat the same later-topic instruction

**Honest limits:** Classroom diagnostics rarely rest on published, validated progressions for every topic. Authors may:

- Use **standards documents, curriculum maps, or team task analyses** as provisional progressions
- Borrow **component structures** from evidence-based intervention guides (e.g., decoding vs comprehension; representation vs word-problem structure)
- Pilot probes and **revise the map** when wrong answers do not sort into distinct instructional categories

Testing standards apply: treat progression-based placement as a **claim** that needs fit-for-purpose evidence when decisions are high-impact. A single probe at one progression step is a **snapshot**, not proof of stable level.

### 2. Elicitation format

Choose formats that surface the needed evidence:

| Need | Often useful formats |
|---|---|
| Prerequisite knowledge | Short constructed response, ordered steps, “which method would you use” |
| Misconceptions | Multiple-choice with distractors based on documented errors; explanation prompts |
| Fluency | Timed, controlled text or operation sets—with purpose explained to reduce anxiety misuse |
| Comprehension | Short passage + questions requiring inference vs recall; monitoring routines |
| Math representations | Tasks showing strategy in pictures, number lines, or language—not only final answers |

Match **cognitive demand** to the decision: screening stays short; deep misconception probes can be longer but still focused.

### 2b. Probe and item quality (cross-reference)

Diagnostic kits often embed **short item sets** or task clusters. The **instrument** (this family) still requires purpose, administration, coding, thresholds, and pathways; **item quality** is governed in depth by the question-item-sets handbook.

| Diagnostic need | Apply from `question-item-sets.md` | Keep in this diagnostic handbook |
|---|---|---|
| Theory-based MCQ distractors | Plausible distractors, one best answer, stem clarity, avoid “all/none of the above” traps | Coding guide: what each distractor **means** instructionally |
| Constructed-response probes | Stem quality, scope, directions, cognitive demand without trickery | Rubric or category codes tied to **next teach** |
| Accessibility | UDL-aligned presentation; reduce irrelevant reading load | Fairness review for **placement** use |
| Fluency-style prompts | Format fit; timing framed for evidence not punishment | Progress-monitoring comparability and slope rules |

**Minimum probe-quality checks before fielding:**

- Each item traces to **one decision-relevant construct** (or one misconception family), not a laundry list of standards.
- Wrong answers are **documented in advance** (research, task analysis, or prior student work)—not invented to fill option slots.
- A knowledgeable learner could understand the question from the **stem alone** (for selected-response).
- Item sets are **reviewed as a set**: pattern of keys, length cues, and duplicate stems do not substitute for knowledge.

University teaching-center item guides (e.g., Waterloo CTE multiple-choice and exam-question tip sheets cited in `question-item-sets.md`) are appropriate **supporting** references for probe drafting; WWC and testing standards still govern **how scores may be used**.

### 3. Administration and standardization

Specify:

- Time limits, materials allowed, read-aloud rules
- Whether collaboration is permitted (usually no for individual placement decisions)
- Environment and accessibility accommodations **without changing the construct** unless the construct includes the accommodated skill

Fair administration is part of valid interpretation.

### 4. Scoring and recording

Decide:

- Machine-scorable vs rubric-coded responses
- **Item-level notes** for qualitative probes
- Whether scores are **norm-referenced** (compared to peers), **criterion-referenced** (compared to a benchmark), or **ipsative** (compared to own prior performance)

Document reliability expectations: single items are unstable; **patterns across items** support inference better than one mark.

### 5. Interpretation rules

Provide:

- Cut scores or decision bands with **empirical or logical rationale**
- Examples of student work per category
- **Triangulation** prompts (“confirm with homework, conference, second probe”)
- Warnings about **construct-irrelevant difficulty** (reading load on a math probe, etc.)

### 5b. Local validation of cut scores and benchmarks

**Screeners and benchmark tools** often ship with **cut scores** or performance bands. Testing standards require that such scores support **intended interpretations for specified uses**—especially when they route learners to more or less intensive support, different tracks, or visible labels.

For **low-stakes classroom routing**, authors may use **logical** or **pilot-based** thresholds if they document:

- **What decision** each band triggers (and what it does *not* trigger)
- **Empirical or logical rationale** (e.g., “below 4/6 on prerequisites implies reteach X because…”)
- **Limits**: single administration, small item count, local curriculum alignment

For **higher-impact placement** (tier assignment, program exclusion, permanent grouping), plan **local validation** rather than importing publisher cuts blindly:

1. **Align** items to local taught content and population (commercial screeners often misalign).
2. **Pilot** on a representative sample; examine **item patterns and band stability**, not only total score.
3. **Check consequences**: do learners placed low receive **benefit** from the extra support (fairness of use)? Do high bands avoid needed help?
4. **Review subgroup patterns**; investigate construct underrepresentation, irrelevant difficulty, or opportunity gaps before attributing deficit.
5. **Revisit cuts** after curriculum or population shifts; stale cuts misroute help.

WWC’s data-use guidance warns against narrow focus on **proficiency cut scores** without interpretation support—especially when a single threshold drives who gets help. Pair cuts with **triangulation** and documented **next instructional steps**.

### 6. Instructional and support pathways

For each major result band, specify:

- **First-line instructional response** (time, grouping, materials)
- **Escalation** if progress monitoring flatlines
- **Enrichment** when secure

Reading interventions in grades 4–9 illustrate **pathway thinking**: decoding routines for multisyllabic words, purposeful fluency practice, comprehension routines (word/world knowledge, Q&A, gist, monitoring), and stretch-text practice—each as a branch when probes show that component is weak.

Elementary math intervention guidance similarly branches into language, representation, number line, word-problem structure, and fluency timing—**only where probes indicate need**.

### 7. Fairness, accessibility, and responsible use

Apply measurement standards mindset even for classroom tools:

- State **intended population** and known limitations
- Review for **construct-irrelevant barriers** (language complexity, cultural context, visual access)
- Plan accommodations that preserve construct meaning
- Avoid high-stakes labels from low-evidence screeners
- Do not treat one score as fixed ability; diagnostics are **snapshot evidence**

When group score patterns differ, investigate **underrepresentation of construct, irrelevant difficulty, or instructional opportunity** before attributing deficit to learners.

### 8. Stakes, frequency, and cost

| Higher frequency | Lower frequency |
|---|---|
| Progress-monitoring probes | Annual placement batteries |
| Short exit tickets tied to lesson goals | Full prerequisite exams |

Balance **information gain** with **learner and teacher time**; WWC notes classroom data can be gathered quickly for immediate feedback, while interim assessments give broader context.

## Authoring workflow

1. **Name the decision** the instrument must support (one primary decision per instrument).
2. **Decompose the construct** into components or progression steps that imply different instructional responses (use domain intervention guides, standards maps, or team task analysis where available).
3. **Document predictable misconceptions or errors** from research, task analysis, or prior student work.
4. **Draft items/tasks** using `question-item-sets.md` for stem, distractor, and accessibility quality; pilot with a small sample if possible.
5. **Write scoring and coding rules** (including exemplar responses per category).
6. **Draft the interpretation guide** with thresholds, actions, and limits.
7. **Map to the improvement cycle**: when administered, who interprets, what change is tested, what probe closes the loop.
8. **Review for fairness and accessibility** (language load, formats, bias in contexts).
9. **Pilot interpretation**: can two teachers reach the same instructional decision from the same results?
10. **Revise** items that do not change decisions or that everyone gets right or wrong.

## Quality criteria and review checklist

### Validity for intended use

- [ ] Intended interpretation and decision are stated in plain language.
- [ ] Items/tasks sample the **claimed construct**, not unrelated difficulty.
- [ ] Wrong answers or weak performance imply **specific instructional responses**, not “study harder.”
- [ ] Limits of inference are explicit (what the tool cannot tell you).

### Technical sufficiency for stakes

- [ ] Low-stakes classroom use: pattern-based interpretation is explained; single-item decisions discouraged.
- [ ] Higher-impact placement: evidence for cut scores and **benefit of treatment** is cited or locally validated (see §5b).
- [ ] Progress-monitoring: forms are **equivalent enough** to compare over time.

### Progressions and probe quality

- [ ] Constructs decomposed so weak performance implies **different** instructional responses (progression or component map documented).
- [ ] Probe items reviewed against `question-item-sets.md` (stems, distractors, accessibility, format–construct fit).
- [ ] Science probes (if applicable) are **three-dimensional** at the intended grain or honestly labeled as fact-only screens with limited inference.

### Interpretation guide completeness

- [ ] Thresholds or coding categories defined.
- [ ] Exemplars for each category.
- [ ] Triangulation guidance included.
- [ ] Testable hypothesis examples provided.

### Actionability

- [ ] Every major result band links to **time-bound instructional action**.
- [ ] Escalation and exit rules exist for intervention tracks.
- [ ] Student-facing goals included when learners use results.

### Fairness and access

- [ ] Construct-irrelevant barriers reviewed.
- [ ] Accommodation guidance does not conflate disability with low construct scores without evidence.
- [ ] Cultural or linguistic load is intentional, not accidental.

### Efficiency

- [ ] Administration time matches decision urgency.
- [ ] Scoring burden is realistic for intended users.

### Anti-misuse guards

- [ ] Document warns against using as summative grade without separate design.
- [ ] Document warns against permanent tracking from one snapshot.

## Common anti-patterns

- **Mini summative test** labeled “diagnostic” with percent score only and no pathway.
- **Trivial pretest** that everyone passes or fails without changing instruction.
- **Misconception items** whose distractors are random, not theory-based.
- **Screener without intervention capacity** downstream.
- **Over-trusting cut scores** from commercial tools without local validation.
- **Single total reading or math score** masking which component to teach.
- **Progress monitoring** on non-comparable tasks, producing fake trends.
- **Diagnostic → gradebook** with no instructional change.
- **Student self-reflection** with no criteria, exemplars, or strategy link.
- **High language load** on content diagnostics for multilingual learners without support.
- **Timed fluency** used punitively rather than to build automaticity with purpose explained.
- **Diagnostic quiz generators** with no domain evidence or intervention pathways.
- **Generic AI prompt lists** with no validity, fairness, or interpretation layer.
- **Worksheet marketplaces** and SEO “diagnostic test” pages with no documented constructs.

## How frameworks influence diagnostics (without becoming the resource)

| Lens | Influence on diagnostics |
|---|---|
| Formative assessment | Emphasizes evidence use **during** learning; diagnostics should feed immediate adjustment. |
| RtI / multi-tier models | Structures **screening, tiered support, progress monitoring, escalation**. |
| Learning progressions | Suggests **ordered subskills** for probe design and interpretation. |
| UDL | Shapes **accessible elicitation** without changing the construct unintentionally. |
| Metacognition | Adds **learner interpretation and strategy selection**, not generic reflection. |
| Data-driven decision making | Requires **hypothesis-testing cycles**, not dashboard consumption. |

These are design lenses; the tangible resource remains the **probe, screener, guide, or monitoring set**.

| Family | Contrast |
|---|---|
| **Assessment instruments** | Broader outcome judgment; higher stakes; fuller coverage; stronger documentation for score use. |
| **Question-item sets** | Items without packaged interpretation, administration, or decision logic; **author probe stems and distractors there**, then package as a diagnostic here. |
| **Practice materials** | Build skill; assume target is known; do not primarily locate gaps. |

## Domain patterns worth imitating

### Early reading (screening + tiers)

- Screen all students at key points in the year.
- Differentiate core instruction by assessed level.
- Below benchmark: intensive, systematic work on **up to three** foundational skills in small groups.
- Monitor tier-2 progress **at least monthly**; intensify to tier 3 when progress is inadequate after reasonable time.

### Reading grades 4–9 (component branches)

When comprehension fails, probe whether **decoding multisyllabic words**, **fluency**, or **comprehension routines** (including vocabulary/world knowledge, questioning, gist, monitoring) are weak—then match intervention branch.

### Elementary mathematics (representation and language)

Weak performance may reflect **systematic instruction gaps**, **mathematical language**, **representations**, **number line understanding**, **word-problem structure**, or **fluency**—diagnostics should help separate these, not only “low math score.”

### Secondary mathematics (grades 6–12)

No WWC practice guide in the current source bundle offers a full **secondary math diagnostic manual** comparable to primary reading RtI. Authors should still **decompose** difficulty using the same logic as elementary intervention guidance and standards-based maps:

| Weak signal on probes | Often implicates | First-line diagnostic follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Procedural slips only on familiar templates | Fluency or careless execution | Short timed check **with purpose explained**; compare to untimed explanation item |
| Correct setup, wrong structure in word problems | Language, schema, or representation | Parallel item with simpler context; diagram or number-line elicitation |
| Stable errors on symbols, graphs, or units | Representation and mathematical language | Tasks that require **two representations** of the same relationship |
| Algebra/calculus “rules” without meaning | Procedural without conceptual tie | Explain-why, predict, or error-analysis item with theory-based distractors |
| Prior-grade ideas resurfacing (fractions, ratio, signed number) | **Progression step** not secure | Prerequisite probe band; reteach at earlier progression before advancing |

Use **course standards or team construct maps** as provisional progressions. Pilot whether wrong-answer patterns sort into **distinct instructional branches**; if not, revise items (see `question-item-sets.md`) before trusting a total score.

### Secondary science (three-dimensional probes)

Science diagnostics sample whether learners can **use practices with core ideas and crosscutting concepts** to make sense of phenomena—not only whether they recall isolated facts. NGSS-aligned assessment resources and Achieve’s criteria for high-quality science assessments inform **task shape**; the diagnostic kit still adds **administration, coding, thresholds, and instructional pathways**.

**When to use which neighbor handbook:**

| Authoring job | Primary handbook | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Short probe before a unit (misconception, prerequisite model) | **Diagnostic instruments** (here) | Decision bands, reteach vs advance, cycle closure |
| Full investigation sequence, lab sheet, CER scaffold | `investigation-lab-materials.md` | Evidence-generating activity structure, safety, three-dimensional performances across days |
| Judging end-of-unit or benchmark **attainment** with full package | `assessment-instruments.md` | Validity, stakes, score-use documentation |

**Design features for science diagnostic probes:**

- Anchor on a **phenomenon or problem** learners can explain, not a vocabulary list disguised as a quiz.
- Require **at least one practice** visibly (model, argue, analyze data, design solution) at a grain matched to the decision.
- Use **interpretation guides** that map response patterns to **instructional moves** (e.g., conflation of correlation and cause; model misuse; lack of evidence linkage)—not only correct/incorrect.
- For selected-response items, distractors should reflect **documented student ideas**; draft items using `question-item-sets.md`, then attach codes here.

**Limits:** Published three-dimensional **screeners** with validated cuts are scarce outside commercial programs; treat local probes as **formative decision support** unless locally validated (see §5b).

## Packaging checklist for a complete diagnostic kit

A publishable kit often includes:

1. **Cover sheet:** purpose, decision, population, time, materials
2. **Task booklet or item set**
3. **Administration script**
4. **Scoring or coding guide**
5. **Interpretation guide** with pathways
6. **Recording sheet** (individual and class view)
7. **Progress graph template** (if repeated use)
8. **Student goal and reflection insert** (optional)
9. **Cycle map** showing when to re-administer and what to change if results stall

## Gaps and cautions for authors

- Few authorities publish **domain-neutral diagnostic authoring manuals**; subject intervention guides and measurement standards must be combined.
- Classroom tools often lack formal **reliability studies**; authors should be honest about uncertainty and avoid high-stakes uses without validation.
- Commercial screeners may not fit local curriculum; **alignment review** and **local cut-score validation** (§5b) are still required.
- **Secondary mathematics** lacks a dedicated WWC diagnostic guide in the current bundle; use standards maps, team task analysis, and elementary intervention **component logic**, not a single total score.
- **Social studies, world languages, arts, and CTE** have no diagnostic shortlist in the authority maps; borrow component-branching patterns from literacy or science only when constructs are analogous.
- **Concept-inventory research traditions** can inform misconception probes but are not core sources here; pair published distractor research with local coding guides when available.

- Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse. *Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9* (Practice Guide 29). https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/29 — decoding, fluency, comprehension routines, and stretch-text branches for component diagnostics.
- Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse. *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades* (Practice Guide 26). https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/26 — math skill components for targeted probes and intervention pathways.
