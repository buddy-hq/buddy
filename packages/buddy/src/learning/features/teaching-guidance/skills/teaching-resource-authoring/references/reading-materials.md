# Reading Materials

Reading materials are tangible artifacts built around texts: passages, source sets, vocabulary supports, comprehension question sets, annotation guides, guided-reading organizers, and document-based inquiry packets. Their job is to structure **purposeful reading and meaning-making**—not to replace instruction, discussion, or assessment, but to give learners and teachers a shared object to read, question, annotate, and interpret.

This handbook is for teachers and instructional designers who create or review reading materials. It synthesizes evidence-based literacy guidance and inquiry-oriented source traditions for authoring text-centered learning artifacts.

---

## What This Family Does

Reading materials help learners:

- Access grade-appropriate or stretch-level texts with enough support to engage productively.
- Build and use vocabulary needed for a specific reading purpose.
- Apply comprehension strategies while reading, not only after reading.
- Discuss, defend, and revise interpretations using textual evidence.
- Evaluate sources and claims when reading informational or digital texts.
- Practice decoding, fluency, and comprehension components where intervention is needed.

The materials should make the **reading task** clear: what to read, in what order, with what supports, what evidence counts as a good response, and how the reading connects to a larger learning goal.

---

## Artifacts In This Family

| Artifact type | Typical contents | Primary function |
|---|---|---|
| Reading packet / text set | One or more passages or documents, sequencing notes, background brief | Organize extended reading around a question or topic |
| Passage or excerpt | Core text, readability/context note, optional glossed terms | Focus reading on a bounded text |
| Vocabulary sheet | Target words, learner-friendly definitions, usage prompts, morphology notes | Prepare for and reinforce word meaning during reading |
| Comprehension set | Text-dependent questions, gist routines, monitoring prompts | Elicit and scaffold understanding while or after reading |
| Annotation guide | Marginal prompts, coding keys, stop points | Make thinking visible during reading |
| Guided-reading / close-reading organizer | Section targets, structure cues, discussion stems | Structure repeated passes through a text |
| Document-based inquiry packet | Central question, primary sources at varied access levels, sourcing/contextualization/corroboration prompts | Historical or disciplinary inquiry through documents |
| Digital-source evaluation task | Web or media sources, lateral-reading prompts, claim/evidence checks | Teach evaluation of online information |
| Intervention reading packet | Decoding practice, fluency routines, comprehension routines, stretch-text plan | Target identified reading difficulties in grades 4–9 or foundational gaps in primary grades |

### Keep Separate From Nearby Families

| Nearby family | Boundary |
|---|---|
| Question and item sets | Standalone items without a coordinated text experience; reading materials center the text first. |
| Practice materials / worksheets | Drill or rehearsal without a primary reading purpose; if the main job is repeated skill practice on decontextualized items, it is not a reading packet. |
| Assessment instruments | Scoring or benchmarking is primary; reading materials may include checks but should not be built only as a test form. |
| Diagnostic instruments | Screening or placement is primary; diagnostics identify needs—reading materials address them in instruction. |
| Discussion and collaboration materials | Talk protocols dominate; reading materials may include discussion prompts but must include texts learners read. |
| Writing-task materials | Production of extended writing is primary; reading-writing integration belongs in both families when writing responds to reading. |
| Study and revision materials | Self-managed review dominates; reading materials are usually teacher-led or text-anchored first. |

## When Reading Materials Fit

Use this family when learners need to **read to learn** something specific: background for a unit, evidence for an argument, disciplinary concepts in exposition, or multiple perspectives on a question.

Strong fits include:

- Content-area units where vocabulary and comprehension barriers block access to the curriculum.
- Adolescent literacy support in grades 4–12 where students must read increasingly complex informational text.
- Intervention blocks for students below grade level in decoding, fluency, or comprehension (especially grades 4–9).
- History and civics work requiring primary sources, contextualization, and evidence-based claims.
- Media literacy units requiring evaluation of who is behind information, what evidence supports claims, and what other sources say.
- World-language programs where **interpretive reading** is a primary performance target—see § World-language interpretive reading (ACTFL Can-Do).
- Science or STEM units needing **argument from evidence in text**, data-rich passages, or phenomena-background reading—see § Science and disciplinary literacy reading sets.
- Multilingual or English-learner classrooms where reading packets must build **academic language and content knowledge together**—see § Multilingual learners and English learners.

## When They Are A Poor Fit

Avoid treating reading materials as the solution when:

- The real need is only a list of topics or “interesting articles” with no reading-level, vocabulary, or comprehension design.
- Generic comprehension questions could apply to any passage (“main idea,” “author’s purpose”) without tying to this text’s meaning or evidence.
- Texts are far above independent level with no scaffolding, stop points, or vocabulary support—frustration without stretch-text design.
- The goal is purely motivational exposure with no comprehension or vocabulary target.
- Screening, grading, or placement is the main outcome—use diagnostic or assessment families instead.
- Extended authentic performance (projects, labs, performances) is the goal—use project, investigation, or writing families as leads.

Common misuses: text packets assembled only for topical relevance; reading homework with no purpose, level check, or follow-up; copying commercial question banks that are not text-dependent; using thinking or discussion routines with no actual text to anchor them.

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

### Core: IES What Works Clearinghouse literacy practice guides

**Improving Adolescent Literacy** (grades 4–12 emphasis) anchors general reading-material design:

1. **Explicit vocabulary instruction** (strong evidence): dedicate regular lesson time; repeated exposure in multiple oral and written contexts; practice using words in discussion and writing; teach independence strategies (context, word parts, reference tools). Select words by **disciplinary importance**, not only frequency tiers—specialized terms that carry content meaning often deserve explicit teaching even if rare in general reading.
2. **Direct, explicit comprehension strategy instruction** (strong evidence): teach routines such as summarizing, questioning, paraphrasing, inferring, and using organizers through model–guided practice–independent practice. Teach **multiple strategies** to the same students. Match strategy to text type (e.g., main-idea summarizing fits informational text better than many narratives). Keep the goal visible: **understanding the text**, not performing strategies for their own sake.
3. **Extended discussion of text meaning** (moderate evidence): prepare engaging texts and authentic questions; use follow-ups that probe reasoning and evidence; provide small-group discussion formats or protocols; discussions should be sustained and interpretive, not rapid recall checks.
4. **Motivation and engagement** (moderate evidence): meaningful disciplinary goals; autonomy-supportive choices where possible; relevance to interests and current events; learning-oriented feedback rather than performance-only pressure; avoid over-reliance on extrinsic rewards for reading.
5. **Intensive individualized intervention** (strong evidence for struggling readers): reliable screening, matched intervention intensity, specialist delivery when classroom instruction is insufficient—materials for tier 2/3 should be tightly aligned to assessed needs.

**Assisting Students Struggling with Reading: RtI in the Primary Grades** anchors early reading packets and screening-linked design:

1. Screen all students at least twice yearly for reading problems.
2. Provide **differentiated reading instruction for all students** based on current level—not only intervention students.
3. Deliver **intensive, systematic instruction** on up to three foundational skills in small groups for students below benchmark.
4. **Monitor tier 2 progress** at least monthly.
5. Provide **daily intensive tier 3** instruction across reading components when tier 2 is insufficient.

Implication for materials: primary-grade reading packets should connect to screening data, target specific foundational skills, and specify group size and frequency—not generic “extra reading.”

**Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9** anchors intervention-oriented and stretch-text materials:

1. **Multisyllabic decoding** (strong): teach letter-sound patterns, syllable division, and practice in words, sentences, and passages.
2. **Purposeful fluency building** (moderate): repeated reading, partner reading, wide reading with purpose—not speed for its own sake.
3. **Comprehension-building practices** (strong), routinely combined:
   - Build world and word knowledge before and during reading.
   - Ask and answer questions to clarify understanding (including question types that move from literal to inferential).
   - Teach a **gist routine** for short sections (often linked to text structure).
   - Teach **comprehension monitoring** during reading.
4. **Stretch text** (minimal evidence per WWC Practice Guide 29, Table I.1): challenging text at or just above what students can read alone, with heavy teacher supports, planned stop points, essential vocabulary, and periodic breaks at instructional level. Sequence difficulty and length gradually; connect topics to subject-area study when possible; use engaging ideas, not only textbook excerpts. The evidence label reflects causal support for stretch-text practice alone—not a judgment that scaffolding complex text is unimportant.

### Supporting: Document-based and digital inquiry traditions

**Digital Inquiry Group — Reading Like a Historian** models document-based reading materials:

- Each lesson centers a **historical question** and a set of **primary documents** designed for a range of reading skills.
- Learners use sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, and close reading—not memorization of facts.
- Materials aim at claims backed by documentary evidence.

Use this tradition when authoring history or civics source sets, not as a template to copy branded lesson structures verbatim. Operational authoring guidance is in § Authoring document-based inquiry packets.

**Civic Online Reasoning** models digital reading evaluation materials organized around three questions: Who is behind the information? What is the evidence? What do other sources say? Collections include intro lessons, lateral reading practice, domain and sponsored-content tasks, and discipline-themed sets. Materials are free with account access; check site licensing for any reuse beyond classroom adaptation. Operational authoring guidance is in § Authoring digital-source evaluation tasks.

### Supporting: Science and disciplinary literacy (NGSS lens)

**NGSS three-dimensional learning** treats reading as one way to engage **science and engineering practices** with disciplinary core ideas and crosscutting concepts—not as generic comprehension divorced from the investigation.

**Authoring implication for science reading packets:**

- Name which **practice** the passage supports (e.g., obtain and evaluate information, analyze data in text, construct explanations, argue from evidence).
- Select texts that include **phenomena, data displays, models, or arguments** learners must interpret—not only declarative definitions.
- Align comprehension prompts to **claim–evidence–reasoning** logic: what claim does the text make, what evidence supports it, how does reasoning connect them?
- Pre-teach **discipline-critical vocabulary** (tier 2 academic words plus science-specific terms) using the intensive, repeated-exposure pattern from adolescent literacy guidance.
- Sequence reading **before, during, or after** hands-on investigation as the unit requires; investigation-lab materials own the empirical task—reading materials supply expository or argumentative texts that feed sense-making.

WWC adolescent literacy (2008) noted limited formal evidence at that time for **discipline-specific discourse patterns** outside general comprehension strategies. For science-specific reading moves, lean on NGSS implementation resources rather than inventing subject-neutral “science worksheets.”

### Core overlay: World-language interpretive reading (ACTFL Can-Do)

The NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements describe what learners can do in **interpretive**, interpersonal, and presentational communication at proficiency levels. For this family, focus on **interpretive reading** (and interpretive listening only when paired with a transcript or repeated oral support in the same packet).

Indicators unpack **function, context, and text type** at sublevels—use them to drive packet design, not only end-of-unit rubrics. Level-specific performance indicators live in downloadable PDFs linked from the ACTFL Can-Do page; open the PDF for your **target proficiency band** when selecting texts and tasks.

**Boundary:** Full analytic rubrics and markschemes belong in **criteria-scoring materials**. Presentational writing or interpersonal speaking tasks belong in **writing-task** or **discussion-collaboration** materials. Reading packets should specify **interpretive evidence** learners produce from texts.

See § World-language interpretive reading (ACTFL Can-Do) for the full authoring chain (target → text → task → evidence).

### Supporting: Accessibility and thinking scaffolds (lenses, not the resource itself)

**CAST UDL Guidelines** inform reading-material accessibility: customizable display; multiple ways to perceive text; clarified vocabulary and symbols; decoding support; connection to prior knowledge; varied response and expression options; graduated practice. Apply as an overlay when authoring any packet.

**Project Zero thinking routines** are short question sequences that scaffold observation, reasoning, perspective-taking, and synthesis during or after reading. They support annotation and discussion design but do not replace evidence-based vocabulary, comprehension, or decoding work. Describe routines in your own words; do not reproduce branded routine posters or matrices.

### Background (use sparingly)

- **Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively** (WWC): when reading materials feed integrated reading-writing cycles.
- **Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning** (EEF): planning, monitoring, and reflection prompts on reading tasks—helpful for annotation and independence, not a substitute for literacy-specific guidance.

### Reject As Authority Seeds

- Topic-only article collections without level, vocabulary, or comprehension design.
- Worksheet marketplaces with generic questions.
- Unsourced blog “comprehension question” lists.
- SEO reading “hacks” without evidence or disciplinary grounding.

## Key Design Decisions

### 1. Define the reading purpose first

State what learners should know, understand, or be able to argue **because they read this material**. Every passage, question, and vocabulary item should trace to that purpose.

### 2. Match text difficulty to purpose and supports

| Design stance | When to use | Material implications |
|---|---|---|
| Instructional level | Teaching new strategies with manageable success | Shorter texts, clearer structure, heavier modeling |
| Independent level | Fluency wide reading, homework with light support | Texts students can read with minimal help |
| Stretch level | Building access to grade-level ideas with scaffolding | Stop points, essential words, gist and Q&A routines, teacher-led segments; sequence easier to harder |

Adolescent and grades 4–9 intervention guidance both warn against assigning complex texts without supports or teaching only at independent level when grade-level ideas are required.

### 3. Select and sequence texts deliberately

For packets and source sets:

- Order documents to build background, complicate simple stories, or sharpen the central question.
- Vary length and complexity within a set so more readers can enter, then increase demand.
- For history inquiry, ensure documents support sourcing and corroboration—not a single authoritative excerpt only.
- For digital tasks, pair suspect or partial sources with lateral-reading prompts.

Prefer engaging, idea-rich texts connected to students’ studies over isolated textbook excerpts when motivation matters.

### 4. Choose vocabulary targets by function

Pre-teach words that unlock **this** reading:

- Discipline-critical terms (even if rare in general language).
- High-utility academic words that appear across texts in the unit.
- Essential proper nouns and concepts needed before reading (brief context for people, places, events).

Provide multiple exposures in discussion, writing, and subsequent readings—not a single definition list with no return to the words.

### 5. Design comprehension work as strategy + meaning

Pair explicit strategy instruction with text-dependent tasks:

- Questions should require evidence from the passage (including inference grounded in text and knowledge).
- Mix question purposes: clarify, connect, challenge author, compare sources.
- Include monitoring and gist routines for dense informational sections.
- In discussion materials, plan follow-up questions that extend reasoning—not only initial prompts.

Avoid question sets that could be answered without reading.

### 6. Plan discussion and collaboration inside the packet

Even in a printable packet, specify:

- Whole-class vs small-group structures.
- Roles or rotating responsibilities in groups.
- Norms for citing text and disagreeing with evidence.
- Minimum time expectations for sustained discussion (research on adolescent classrooms shows very little time is often spent on extended interpretive talk).

### 7. Build motivation into the material design

Connect texts to disciplinary essential ideas, student interests, or current events. Where appropriate, offer limited choice of complementary texts or response formats. Frame feedback on **understanding and effort**, not only grades. Materials should signal that mistakes while using strategies are part of learning.

### 8. Align intervention materials to assessed component needs

For struggling readers, separate material streams for **decoding**, **fluency**, **comprehension routines**, and **stretch-text practice** (the last has minimal WWC causal evidence but remains a named recommendation with explicit scaffolding design). A packet that only asks comprehension questions without addressing word-reading or fluency needs misaligns with grades 4–9 intervention evidence.

### 9. Accessibility and language diversity

Plan alternatives: readable formatting, glossary support, audio or teacher read-aloud where decoding blocks access, first-language resources where they build comprehension, and flexible response modes when handwriting or length barriers exist. Represent diverse perspectives authentically in text selection—not as an afterthought.

### 10. Text-dependent question stems (not generic prompts)

Questions should force use of **this** passage. Paraphrase stems in your own words; do not copy commercial item banks.

| Weak (generic) | Stronger (text-bound) |
|---|---|
| “What is the main idea?” | “According to paragraphs 2–4, what problem does the author say [named entity] faced, and what evidence supports that?” |
| “Do you agree with the author?” | “Which sentence in the passage best supports the claim that ___? Quote or point to the line.” |
| “What did you learn?” | “How does the author’s description of ___ in lines 12–18 change how a reader should interpret ___?” |

Use a mix of clarify, connect, and challenge stems; higher-level prompts still require specific textual anchors.

## Student-Facing Routines (Grades 4–9 Intervention)

Intervention reading packets should include **printable step sequences** learners can follow during small-group or paired work. Model each routine before students use it independently. Pick **one** multisyllabic decoding routine per packet and use it consistently—do not stack competing word-breaking methods in the same unit.

Ground these blocks in WWC Practice Guide 29 (Recommendations 1–3). Paraphrase only; do not copy panel resource tables or scripted lesson dialogue from the guide.

### Multisyllabic decoding (embed on a practice page)

Choose one routine and post its steps where students can see them.

**Affix-and-chunk routine** (when prefixes and suffixes are teachable in the word list):

1. Circle known prefixes and suffixes; note what they mean.
2. Underline the vowel sounds left in the base.
3. Say each chunk aloud, looping under parts as you go.
4. Blend the chunks into a whole word you recognize; confirm meaning in context.

**Syllable-count routine** (when affixes are unreliable or not yet taught):

1. Underline single vowels and vowel teams in the word.
2. Count vowel sounds to decide how many syllables the word has.
3. Break the word at syllable boundaries (each part has a vowel sound).
4. Blend the parts; check that the word fits the sentence.

Follow decoding practice with short **encoding** (spelling) using the same letter patterns, and with sentences or a short passage that recycle taught words.

### Fluency practice block (short passage, instructional level)

Purposeful repeated reading builds ease and meaning—not speed alone. Use content-rich passages tied to the subject when possible.

| Step | What students do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Skim the passage; mark words that look unfamiliar or whose meaning is unclear. |
| 2 | With the teacher, briefly learn how to read and understand those words. |
| 3 | Listen while the teacher reads the passage with natural pacing and expression. |
| 4 | Read the passage aloud together with the teacher. |
| 5 | Partner A reads while Partner B follows along; switch roles. |
| 6 | Discuss one text-based question about what the passage says (not a generic opinion prompt). |

Optional next pass: read with expression at commas, periods, and question marks; compare a flat read to a meaningful read.

### Gist routine (one- or two-paragraph sections)

A **gist** is a short synthesis of the most important information in a section—what the section is mostly about in the reader’s own words.

| Step | What students do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Decide **who or what** the section is mainly about (look at title, headings, and repeated names or ideas). |
| 2 | Mark the details that matter most for that who/what; ignore side facts that do not change the main point. |
| 3 | Combine those details into one main point. |
| 4 | Write one short gist sentence in **your own words** (not copied from the text). |
| 5 | Check: Does the gist include the important ideas? Is it one clear, complete sentence? |

**Marking the text:** Use one consistent symbol for hard words or phrases and a **different** symbol for important information. Teacher marks first; responsibility shifts to students over time. At planned **stop points**, students pause and complete a gist before continuing.

Link gist chunks to **text structure** when helpful (e.g., problem/solution, compare/contrast, sequence)—structure questions can focus what counts as “important.”

### Comprehension monitoring (during and after reading)

Many struggling readers finish pages without knowing whether they understood. Monitoring teaches them to notice confusion and fix it.

**Build the habit (sentence level first):** Practice deciding whether isolated sentences make sense; mark the word or phrase that breaks understanding; discuss fixes.

**While reading a section:**

1. Stop at the end of a section (or at a printed stop point).
2. Ask: **What was this section about?** (Link to the gist routine.)
3. If the answer is unclear, ask: Are there words I cannot read or do not understand? Phrases that do not fit? Should I reread slowly?
4. If a word blocks meaning: use context, word parts, or a glossary; ask a partner or teacher if still stuck.
5. If it reminds you of something you know: What do I already know that helps—or misleads—here?
6. If you think you understand: What are the main points so far? Do I need to reread and mark them?

**After reading (brief exit):** Students note what they learned, what is still confusing, and one action they could take next time (reread, look up a term, ask a question). This is for **self-checking**, not a substitute for text-dependent comprehension questions about content.

### How to place routines in an intervention packet

| Packet zone | Typical routines |
|---|---|
| Word study page | One decoding routine + encoding lines |
| Fluency passage | Fluency block (steps 1–6) |
| Main text with stop points | Gist steps at each stop; monitoring prompts in the margin |
| After reading | Monitoring reflection + text-dependent questions |

Keep decoding, fluency, and comprehension streams **separate but coordinated**—a page of comprehension questions alone does not satisfy intervention design when decoding or fluency is the assessed need.

## Authoring Annotation Guides

Annotation guides turn the **annotation guide** artifact (see table above) into a usable margin protocol—not a second worksheet disconnected from the text.

- **State the reading purpose** at the top so codes tie to what evidence will matter in discussion or writing.
- **Limit the coding key** to a small set (about three to five symbols), each with a plain-language definition—for example: main idea, supporting evidence, question/confusion, vocabulary to revisit, author’s claim. Use **different** marks for “I don’t understand this” versus “this is important for the gist.”
- **Place stop points** where you want a gist, a monitor check, or a turn-and-talk—usually at section breaks, not every sentence.
- **Specify mode:** solo annotation first, then pair compare at stops; or shared document with rotating highlighter roles. Say whether students should cite margin codes when they speak.
- **Bridge to discussion:** Require that interpretive claims point to a marked line or phrase; align with extended-discussion guidance (authentic question, follow-up probes).
- **Accessibility:** Allow oral annotation, sticky notes, or digital highlights when handwriting or dense margins block access.

Review annotation guides with the same text-dependence test as comprehension questions: prompts should not be answerable without reading **this** text.

## World-Language Interpretive Reading (ACTFL Can-Do)

Use this section when the packet’s primary job is **demonstrating interpretive comprehension** in the target language—not when a language class is only using English-language content-area texts (use WWC literacy guidance for those).

### 1. Set the interpretive target first

Choose one or two **Can-Do indicators** at the intended proficiency level (e.g., Novice Mid, Intermediate Low). Write them as student-visible goals in plain language paraphrased from the indicators—do not copy descriptor tables from ACTFL PDFs.

Typical interpretive-reading functions to target in a single packet (pick deliberately, do not stack unrelated levels):

| Function (paraphrased) | What the packet should elicit |
|---|---|
| Main idea and key details | Gist of a short passage; supported summary |
| Word/phrase meaning in context | Inferences about unknown words using text and visuals |
| Author’s purpose or point of view | Why the text was written; whose perspective is centered |
| Comparison across texts | How two short texts agree, disagree, or complement |
| Cultural or pragmatic inference | How context shapes meaning (when culture is a stated goal) |

### 2. Select texts by proficiency, function, and text type

Match **text type** (authentic article, infographic, literary excerpt, adapted news, correspondence) to the indicator’s **context** field. At lower proficiency, favor **highly structured, visually supported, familiar topics**; at higher proficiency, favor **longer connected discourse, implicit meaning, and less glossing**.

| Proficiency band (typical) | Text features | Packet supports |
|---|---|---|
| Novice | Very short chunks; predictable patterns; concrete topics | Images, word banks, bilingual glosses where policy allows, sentence frames for responses |
| Intermediate | Connected paragraphs; some abstraction; mixed known/unknown vocabulary | Chunked subheadings, targeted glossary (5–8 words), guided questions before open summary |
| Advanced | Longer authentic texts; implicit references; multiple perspectives | Fewer scaffolds; comparison tasks; optional L1 bridge only when it unlocks meaning |

**Length rule:** If learners cannot finish with comprehension evidence in the allotted time, shorten or tier the text—do not compensate with English-only summary prompts that bypass interpretive reading in the target language.

### 3. Design interpretive tasks and question sequence

Sequence tasks from **access → evidence → interpretation**:

1. **Access** — brief prediction or vocabulary front-load tied to the passage (not a generic list).
2. **Evidence** — text-bound questions requiring specific lines, phrases, or details (quote or point).
3. **Interpretation** — inference, comparison, or evaluation grounded in cited evidence.

Pair **interpretive reading** tasks with **discussion** only when talk is scripted as evidence rehearsal (who cites which line)—full seminar protocols live in discussion-collaboration materials.

### 4. Specify observable evidence (without writing a full rubric)

State what teachers will **collect** from the packet:

- Written responses (target-language sentences, graphic organizer with cited details, selected-response with justification).
- Annotated text showing confusion vs importance codes (see § Authoring annotation guides).
- Short oral summary with a checklist of required elements (main idea + two details)—record or peer-verify if used for grading.

Include **2–4 student-facing success bullets** paraphrased from the target Can-Do indicator (e.g., “I can state the main idea and point to two supporting details in the text”). For full proficiency rubrics or course-wide grade descriptors, use **criteria-scoring materials**.

### 5. Review checks specific to world-language packets

- [ ] Target Can-Do function and proficiency band are explicit on the packet.
- [ ] Text type and length match the band; glossing fades as proficiency rises.
- [ ] Questions require reading **this** text in the **target language**.
- [ ] Evidence products are named; rubric depth is delegated appropriately to criteria-scoring materials if needed.

## Science And Disciplinary Literacy Reading Sets

Use when learners read **science or technical exposition** to build understanding of phenomena, data, models, or arguments—not when the main job is conducting the investigation (see investigation-lab materials).

### Design moves grounded in NGSS and adolescent literacy

1. **Anchor to a phenomenon or problem** stated at the top of the packet—readers should know what sense-making question the text serves.
2. **Name the targeted science and engineering practice** the reading rehearses (e.g., analyze and interpret data presented in text, construct explanations from evidence in text, obtain and evaluate scientific information).
3. **Select texts with disciplinary features** — procedures, data tables, graphs, diagrams, cause–effect explanations, competing models, or short research summaries—not only definition lists.
4. **Pre-teach vocabulary** that unlocks the passage (science-specific terms plus high-utility academic words such as *analyze*, *evidence*, *variable* when they appear in the text).
5. **Use text-dependent prompts that mirror CER logic:**
   - What **claim** does the author or data support?
   - What **evidence** in the text (numbers, trends, quoted findings) backs it?
   - What **reasoning** connects evidence to claim (mechanism, comparison, rule)?
6. **Include at least one “challenge the text” prompt** when appropriate—limitations of a study, alternative explanations, or missing controls—at a level matched to grade band.
7. **Plan stops** for gist and monitoring on dense informational sections (same routines as § Student-facing routines when readers struggle).

### Sequencing within a unit

| Placement | Typical reading material |
|---|---|
| Before investigation | Background on phenomenon, vocabulary, safety context |
| During investigation | Procedure excerpts, data interpretation passages, comparison of two explanations |
| After investigation | Secondary sources that extend or critique class findings |

### Fairness and language

Science reading for multilingual learners should follow § Multilingual learners and English learners **and** maintain grade-level ideas with scaffolds—not watered-down concepts with no data or argument demand.

## Authoring Document-Based Inquiry Packets (RLH Tradition)

History and civics **document-based inquiry packets** center a **historical question** learners can argue with evidence—not a single correct story. Author fresh materials; do not copy branded lesson scripts or proprietary document sets.

### 1. Craft the central question

- **Investigative** — admits multiple plausible answers supported by sources.
- **Interpretive** — requires weighing evidence, not only recall of one textbook paragraph.
- **Bounded** — answerable with the documents and background you provide in one to three class periods.

Avoid yes/no questions and questions answerable without reading the set.

### 2. Select and modify documents

| Decision | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Count** | Usually **3–6** documents per set; more risks shallow skimming. |
| **Variety** | Mix perspectives, formats (letter, data table, image caption, law excerpt), and difficulty. |
| **Access levels** | Include at least one **entry document** most readers can access; ramp complexity. |
| **Modification** | Shorten length, simplify syntax, define archaic terms, standardize spelling—**preserve** passages needed for sourcing and corroboration. Note modifications in teacher materials. |
| **Sourcing potential** | Each document should allow “who wrote this, when, why, and what might they want readers to believe?” |

### 3. Sequence the set

Typical arc (adjust to question):

1. **Context** — brief background (not a full lecture) or an accessible document that orients time and place.
2. **Complication** — documents that challenge a simple narrative.
3. **Corroboration cluster** — documents that agree, disagree, or qualify each other.

Specify **reading order** and whether learners read all documents before writing or cycle document-by-document with stops.

### 4. Embed historical thinking prompts per document

For each source, assign **one primary thinking move** per pass (do not require every move on every doc):

| Move | Learner-facing purpose (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **Sourcing** | Who made this, when, and how might that shape reliability or bias? |
| **Contextualization** | What was happening at the time that helps interpret words or claims? |
| **Close reading** | What does a key sentence or paragraph say and imply? |
| **Corroboration** | How does this source align or conflict with another in the set? |

Require **citations** (document letter/number + line or phrase) for interpretive claims.

### 5. Culminating task and evidence

End with a **short evidence-based response**: paragraph, structured outline, or oral defense with notes. Success features:

- Answers the central question.
- Uses **multiple documents**.
- Attributes claims to specific evidence.
- Acknowledges tension or limitation when sources conflict.

Extended seminar talk protocols belong in **discussion-collaboration materials**; this packet supplies texts and written evidence anchors.

### 6. Teacher use notes

Include time estimates, suggested grouping (pairs for document passes, whole class for synthesis), and **common pitfalls** (students summarize plots without sourcing; students ignore contradicting documents).

## Authoring Digital-Source Evaluation Tasks (COR Tradition)

Digital-source evaluation materials teach learners to judge **online** claims using strategies aligned with professional fact-checking research: **who** is behind information, **what** evidence supports it, and **what other sources** say—often through **lateral reading** (leaving the original page to verify elsewhere) and **click restraint** (pausing before deep engagement on an unfamiliar site).

### Typical lesson arc (adapt to length)

| Phase | Material contents |
|---|---|
| **Frame** | Real scenario, suspect page, or misleading post; state what learners must decide |
| **Model** | Teacher think-aloud: one COR question answered with visible tabs/sources checked |
| **Guided practice** | Same page or parallel example; learners record checks in a structured log |
| **Independent task** | New URL or screenshot set; learners complete evaluation with evidence trail |
| **Debrief** | Compare conclusions; discuss what evidence changed minds |

### Task types to mix across a unit

| Task focus | What learners produce |
|---|---|
| **Who’s behind it?** | Organization, author credentials, About-page findings, possible motives |
| **What’s the evidence?** | Whether claims match cited studies, data, or images; internal contradictions |
| **What do other sources say?** | Lateral-reading notes from trusted directories, fact-checkers, or expert sources |
| **Domain and format** | Who owns the site, news vs opinion, sponsored content, misleading URLs |
| **Discipline-themed set** | Apply the three questions to science, history, or civics topics (COR publishes themed collections) |

### Student-facing evidence trail (required)

Packets should include a **visible log**, not only a yes/no reliability vote:

- **Source checked** (name of outlet or tool used).
- **Finding** (one sentence per check).
- **Conclusion** (supported claim about credibility or need for more search).
- **Open questions** (what remains uncertain).

### Assessment of evaluation (classroom level)

Judge whether learners **justify** evaluations with cross-source evidence—not whether they agree with the teacher’s prior opinion. Rubrics for sustained COR performance belong in criteria-scoring materials; here, include a short checklist aligned to the three questions.

### Boundaries

- **Reading materials** own texts/screenshots and evaluation logs.
- **Discussion-collaboration materials** own group deliberation protocols when talk dominates.
- Do not treat COR as a substitute for **history sourcing** on archival documents—pair traditions when units mix print primaries and live web sources.

## Multilingual Learners And English Learners

Reading packets for **English learners** and other **multilingual learners** should build **content knowledge and English academic language together**—not delay content until English is “finished.”

Ground design in **Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners in Elementary and Middle School** (WWC Practice Guide 19) alongside adolescent literacy and UDL overlays already in this handbook. For secondary newcomers, the same principles apply even when the WWC guide’s examples skew younger—intensive vocabulary, integrated oral/written language, and structured intervention still matter.

### Four WWC moves applied to reading packets

| Recommendation (paraphrased) | Reading-packet design |
|---|---|
| **Teach a small set of academic vocabulary intensively** | Choose **4–8** high-utility words per passage; multiple exposures in reading, discussion, and short writing; learner-friendly explanations and practice—not one static list |
| **Integrate oral and written English into content reading** | Read-aloud or choral chunking; pair retell; sentence frames for evidence responses; preview and review discussions tied to the text’s concepts |
| **Provide regular structured writing from reading** | Gist sentences, evidence frames, summary paragraphs, or graphic organizers that require complete clauses—not isolated word copies |
| **Small-group intervention for struggling literacy/ELD** | Tiered packet variant or parallel group text at instructional level with extra monitoring; align to screening when available |

### Additional material decisions

- **Match text level to purpose with supports** — stretch toward grade-level ideas using glossaries, chunked texts, and stop points; avoid assigning grade-level density with no scaffolding.
- **Bridge first language when it builds comprehension** — bilingual glossaries, contrastive cognates, or brief L1 summary **before** deep English analysis when policy and pedagogy allow—not as a replacement for English interpretive work.
- **Reduce unnecessary linguistic load** — clarify syntax in teacher-modified excerpts; teach cognates and word parts; do not simplify away the central disciplinary idea.
- **Separate language practice from evidence practice when assessing** — if the goal is science argument from text, do not penalize handwriting or accent; if the goal is English morphology, say so explicitly.

### Review checks

- [ ] Academic vocabulary is **small, intensive, and recycled** in the packet.
- [ ] Oral and written opportunities are **structured**, not left implicit.
- [ ] Content rigor remains visible (learners still engage core ideas and evidence).
- [ ] Supports are documented for teachers (grouping, time, L1 use policy).

## Authoring Workflow

1. **Specify outcomes** — What should readers understand, explain, or argue? What evidence will show that?
2. **Profile the audience** — Grade band, typical reading level, language background, known skill gaps (decoding vs comprehension vs vocabulary).
3. **Select or write texts** — Check length, structure, density, and engagement; for source sets, draft a central question.
4. **Audit linguistic demand** — List blocking vocabulary, proper nouns, and syntax challenges; decide what to pre-teach vs gloss vs teach during reading.
5. **Choose scaffolding layer** — Vocabulary guide, stop points, graphic organizer, student-facing intervention routines (decoding, fluency, gist, monitoring) when applicable, annotation guide with coding key; for specialized packets use § World-language interpretive reading, § Science and disciplinary literacy, § Authoring document-based inquiry packets, § Authoring digital-source evaluation tasks, or § Multilingual learners and English learners as needed.
6. **Draft comprehension and discussion tasks** — Ensure text dependence; sequence from access to interpretation to critique.
7. **Add independence pathway** — Word-learning strategies, optional extensions, related readings.
8. **Pilot with a reader proxy** — Attempt answers without class context; flag questions answerable without the text.
9. **Accessibility pass** — UDL overlay: perception, language, comprehension supports, expression options.
10. **Document use notes for teachers** — Purpose, time, grouping, prerequisite knowledge, what to do if students cannot access the text.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

### Text and set quality

- [ ] Central purpose is explicit and defensible for the discipline or unit.
- [ ] Text difficulty matches stated design (instructional, independent, or stretch with supports).
- [ ] Source sets serve a genuine inquiry or interpretive problem—not random documents.
- [ ] Digital materials include evaluation of source, evidence, and corroboration where relevant.
- [ ] Content is accurate, current enough for the context, and appropriately attributed.

### Vocabulary quality

- [ ] Target words are selected for disciplinary or passage-specific importance.
- [ ] Learners get repeated, meaningful encounters—not one-off definitions.
- [ ] Independence strategies are taught, not only teacher definitions.
- [ ] Proper nouns and essential background are handled before confusion compounds.

### Comprehension and question quality

- [ ] Questions require reading the specific text (including paired passages or sources).
- [ ] Strategy prompts match text genre (narrative vs informational vs argument).
- [ ] Gist, Q&A, and monitoring routines appear where density demands them.
- [ ] Higher-level prompts build interpretation, not only recall—when that is the goal.

### Discussion and engagement quality

- [ ] Discussion prompts are authentic (multiple plausible interpretations or reasoned judgments).
- [ ] Follow-up probes are planned.
- [ ] Small-group structure is specified where needed.
- [ ] Material avoids relying solely on extrinsic motivators for reading.

### Intervention alignment (when applicable)

- [ ] Component target is clear: decoding, fluency, comprehension routine, or stretch text.
- [ ] Primary or grades 4–9 guidance is reflected in routines, not generic “read and answer.”
- [ ] Printable student-facing steps appear for decoding, fluency, gist, and/or monitoring when the packet targets those components.
- [ ] Stretch-text packets include stop points, essential words, and planned teacher mediation.
- [ ] Intensity and grouping notes match tier purpose.

### Annotation guide quality (when applicable)

- [ ] Coding key is small, defined, and distinct for confusion vs importance.
- [ ] Stop points align with gist or discussion goals.
- [ ] Solo/pair structure is specified; discussion requires textual anchors.

### Fairness and access

- [ ] Supports exist for students who cannot decode the text independently but can comprehend with access.
- [ ] Materials do not assume uniform cultural background without bridging context.
- [ ] Response formats allow equitable demonstration where possible.

### World-language interpretive reading (when applicable)

- [ ] Target Can-Do function and proficiency band are explicit; text type and length match the band.
- [ ] Tasks require interpretive work in the target language with named evidence products.
- [ ] Rubric depth is appropriate—student bullets in-packet; full rubrics in criteria-scoring materials if needed.

### Document-based inquiry (when applicable)

- [ ] Central question is investigative and evidence-based.
- [ ] Multiple documents support sourcing and corroboration; modifications are documented.
- [ ] Culminating task cites specific documents.

### Digital-source evaluation (when applicable)

- [ ] Three COR questions are operationalized with an evidence trail, not a single reliability rating.
- [ ] Lateral reading or cross-source checks are required where the task demands them.

### Science disciplinary reading (when applicable)

- [ ] Phenomenon or problem and targeted SEP are stated.
- [ ] Prompts engage data, models, or argument—not only vocabulary definition.

### Multilingual / English learners (when applicable)

- [ ] Academic vocabulary is intensive and recycled; oral and written language are integrated into content reading.
- [ ] Content rigor remains with documented scaffolds.

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|---|---|---|
| Generic comprehension worksheet | Questions transferable to any text; no evidence of reading | Text-dependent prompts tied to specific lines or ideas |
| Vocabulary list without return | Words taught once, never used in tasks | Recycle words in questions, discussions, writing |
| Stretch text without stops | Overwhelming density; passive reading | Planned stop points, essential words, gist chunks |
| Strategy overload without meaning | Students perform steps but do not understand | Reduce strategies per lesson; foreground comprehension goal |
| Single-source history “packet” | No corroboration or sourcing practice | Multiple documents, explicit sourcing prompts |
| Click-only digital literacy | Students stay on unreliable page | Lateral reading and cross-source checks |
| Ignoring decoding in upper grades | Comprehension tasks on text students cannot decode | Multisyllabic work and fluency routines alongside comprehension |
| Discussion prompts as quiz questions | Closed recall, no interpretive talk | Open, evidence-seeking questions with follow-ups |
| Motivation-only packets | Interest without learning targets | Pair relevance with vocabulary and comprehension design |
| World-language packet in English only | Bypasses interpretive target-language performance | Target-language texts, tasks, and evidence aligned to Can-Do |
| Science reading without practices | Treats science like generic recall | Name SEP; use CER-aligned, data-aware prompts |
| COR “reliable/unreliable” checkbox | No visible verification trail | Structured who/evidence/other-sources log |
| ELL packet with huge cold vocabulary list | One exposure, no return | Intensive small set across packet activities |

## How Frameworks Influence (Without Becoming) The Resource

| Lens | Legitimate influence on reading materials | Not a substitute for |
|---|---|---|
| UDL | Display options, glossaries, flexible responses, accessible media | Choosing texts or writing text-dependent questions |
| Bloom / DOK | Cognitive demand of prompts | Passage quality or comprehension strategy instruction |
| Metacognition | Planning, monitoring, reflecting on reading | Explicit vocabulary and decoding teaching when needed |
| Thinking routines | Short scaffolds for noticing and reasoning during reading | Full comprehension strategy sequences |
| ACTFL Can-Do | Interpretive text type, task, and evidence at a proficiency target | General English-only comprehension when WL interpretive reading is the goal |
| NGSS / three-dimensional learning | Science reading tied to practices, phenomena, and CER-style prompts | Hands-on investigation sheets (investigation-lab family) |
| Formative assessment | Embedded checks that inform next instruction | Summative-only test packets |

The tangible resource remains the **text plus designed supports and tasks**, not the framework name on the cover.

## Relationships To Other Resource Families

- **Question-item sets**: Extract items from a reading packet when teachers need only a quiz; keep text and supports bundled when the learning goal is sustained reading.
- **Practice materials**: Use for repeated decoding or fluency drills; reading materials should contextualize practice in meaningful passages when possible.
- **Writing-task materials**: Add prompts that require citing the read text; integrate reading features called out in secondary writing guidance.
- **Discussion-collaboration materials**: Emphasize talk protocols; reading materials supply the texts and evidence anchors.
- **Diagnostic instruments**: Identify which component to address; reading materials implement the instructional response.
- **Feedback-revision materials**: Use when readers revise interpretations or written responses to text—not for first-pass comprehension design alone.
