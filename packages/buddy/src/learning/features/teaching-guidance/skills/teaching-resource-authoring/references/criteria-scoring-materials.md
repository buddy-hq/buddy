# Criteria and Scoring Materials

## What this family is

Criteria and scoring materials are concrete artifacts that make performance expectations visible and guide how work is judged. They translate learning goals into observable qualities, levels of attainment, and scoring logic so teachers, students, peer reviewers, and external assessors can apply standards consistently.

Typical artifacts include:

- **Rubrics** (analytic or holistic) with criteria, performance levels, and descriptors
- **Marking guides** and **scoring guides** that explain how to apply criteria to student work
- **Scoring keys** and **band descriptors** that tie evidence to point ranges or achievement levels
- **Anchor descriptors** and **anchor sets** that illustrate what each level looks like on real or realistic work
- **Moderation sheets** and **calibration notes** that align multiple scorers to a shared standard
- **Checklists** and **success-criteria sheets** when they specify observable quality features (not merely task completion)

The pedagogical job is twofold: **make quality legible** before and during production, and **support defensible, fair judgment** when work is reviewed. Well-designed materials help learners monitor their own work, help teachers give criterion-linked feedback, and help teams mark to the same standard across classrooms or exam sessions.

## Artifact boundaries

### Belongs in this family

Materials whose primary purpose is to **define and apply standards of quality** to learner work:

- Rubrics for essays, projects, labs, presentations, discussions, portfolios, and performances
- Criterion-referenced marking guides used by teachers or trained assessors
- Level descriptors for standards-based or proficiency-based scoring
- Anchor papers or anchor performances with annotated rationale for the assigned level
- Scoring instructions that explain how to combine criteria, handle borderline cases, or apply a best-fit judgment

### Keep separate (nearby families)

| Nearby artifact | Why it is separate |
|---|---|
| **Answer keys** and worked solutions | Judge correctness of specific responses, not holistic quality across criteria |
| **Success criteria / model answers** (without scoring levels) | Show what good looks like; may pair with rubrics but are not scoring tools by themselves |
| **Feedback and revision forms** | Turn judgments into next-step actions; criteria materials may feed them but are not the feedback artifact |
| **Assessment instruments** (full tests, performance tasks) | Elicit evidence; scoring materials interpret that evidence |
| **Diagnostic probes** | Locate gaps or misconceptions; scoring is secondary to placement or instructional response |
| **Project briefs** | Specify the task; rubrics judge how well the task was done |

### Frameworks are lenses, not this family

Bloom levels, depth-of-knowledge labels, UDL, formative-assessment theory, and proficiency frameworks can **inform** criterion choice and descriptor language. They are not substitutes for a tangible rubric or marking guide. A Can-Do statement becomes a scoring resource only when embedded in criteria, levels, and exemplars teachers and learners can actually use while judging work.

## When to use this family

Use criteria and scoring materials when:

- The learning outcome is **judged qualitatively** using multiple features of quality (argument, evidence, craft, reasoning, communication, collaboration), not only right/wrong answers
- **Consistency matters** across time, across teachers, or across a cohort (team grading, external assessment, portfolio review)
- Learners need to **self-assess, peer review, or revise** against explicit standards before submission
- The task is **extended or open-ended** (writing, inquiry, design, performance) where reasonable judges could disagree without shared criteria
- You will use scores for **formative feedback** (where criteria should be actionable) or **summative reporting** (where criteria must support fair, interpretable outcomes)
- A programme uses **standards-based**, **proficiency-based**, or **criterion-referenced** reporting and needs descriptors aligned to those levels

## When this family is a poor fit

Avoid leading with rubrics when:

- Outcomes are **binary** (correct/incorrect, present/absent) and a key or checklist is enough
- The goal is **practice or retrieval**, not judgment of complex performance
- Criteria would be **vague labels** (“excellent / good / fair / poor”) with no observable descriptors
- You have **no shared construct** of quality yet—draft exemplars and clarify goals before building a grid
- Time for **calibration** is zero but stakes are high (high-stakes scoring without moderation invites drift)
- The rubric would **duplicate the task instructions** without adding quality dimensions

Common misuses:

- Publishing a rubric **after** work is submitted, so it functions only as a grading shortcut
- Using **norm-referenced** comparison language in materials meant for formative improvement
- Treating rubric scores as **feedback** without comments tied to criteria and next steps
- **Over-criteria-ing** simple tasks so scoring becomes arithmetic on weakly independent dimensions
- **Under-describing** levels so two teachers assign different levels for the same paper

## Source traditions and what each contributes

### University teaching centers (practical rubric craft)

Carnegie Mellon’s Eberly Center treats a rubric as a scoring tool that **explicitly represents performance expectations**, divides work into **component parts**, and describes characteristics at **varying levels of mastery**. A complete rubric identifies **criteria** (what is assessed), **descriptors** (what performance looks like), and **performance levels** (how well it was done).

That tradition emphasizes:

- Rubrics for papers, projects, oral work, artistic performance, and participation
- **Grading consistency** across time and multiple graders
- **Formative use**: sharing rubrics with the assignment, tracking component weaknesses across a class, helping students monitor progress
- Benefits to students: clearer expectations, directed effort, use of feedback against standards

**K–12 note:** CMU’s guides are higher-education teaching-center resources. They transfer well to secondary and upper-elementary rubric design, but they do not spell out district reporting, qualification mark schemes, or large-scale moderation. Pair CMU craft with the K–12 traditions below when authoring for schools.

### K–12 and school-system rubric traditions

Several open, school-facing sources complement CMU without replacing construct alignment or calibration practice.

**Federal regional lab guidance (REL Northeast).** The IES-hosted *Guidance on Creating Rubrics* defines a rubric as a table that communicates performance expectations and focused feedback along **criteria** (rows) and **levels of performance** (columns). It advises tying criteria to learning outcomes, using observable language in descriptors, and aligning rows to what the assessment actually asks students to do—language that maps directly to classroom analytic rubrics in U.S. schools.

**Australian Curriculum achievement standards (ACARA).** The *Shape of the Australian Curriculum* describes **achievement standards** as statements of what students are typically able to understand and do at each year level or band, with **annotated work samples** as support material for **on-balance judgments**—not as prescriptive classroom rubrics to copy. Teachers still author task-specific rubrics and marking guides, but the standards-plus-samples model is the system-level pattern: progression descriptors, exemplar-grounded moderation, and holistic judgment against a band rather than checklist counting.

**Writing assessment principles (CCCC/NCTE).** The CCCC *Writing Assessment: A Position Statement* (2022) stresses that best classroom assessment is **contextual and locally developed**: learning goals, methods, and success criteria should fit the students and purposes at hand. It recommends inviting students into assessment design where possible, using **multiple measures**, and separating values about language variety from arbitrary penalties unrelated to the rhetorical goal. That supports co-constructed rubrics, proficiency-oriented feedback, and criterion language that does not conflate “standard edited English” with overall writing quality unless conventions are explicit targets.

Together, these traditions fill the K–12 gap noted in authority mapping: they do not replace CMU’s rubric mechanics, but they anchor school authoring in **standards progression**, **on-balance judgment**, and **local, equitable criteria design**.

### Formative assessment and evaluative judgment (why criteria matter)

Royce Sadler’s formative-assessment theory applies wherever **multiple criteria** judge the **quality** of student responses (not merely correctness). For improvement, learners need three simultaneous capacities: an **appreciation of quality**, **evaluative skill** to compare their work to a standard, and a **repertoire of tactics** to close the gap.

Sadler stresses that **summary grades alone** can block formative loops if students cannot act on them. Qualitative judgments use **interlocking criteria**; some criteria are **fuzzy** (gradations like originality) rather than sharp (correct/incorrect). Competent judges also apply **metacriteria**—knowing which criteria matter for a given piece of work.

To externalize standards, Sadler pairs **verbal descriptions** with **exemplars**. Neither alone is sufficient; together they make a reference level concrete. Multiple exemplars help learners see that quality can be achieved in more than one way and reduce slavish copying of a single model.

### Peer-assessment criteria (learners judging peers)

Keith Topping’s peer-assessment synthesis distinguishes a **simple** form (peers assign scores or grades with little discussion) from an **elaborated** form suited to learning: peers specify quality, give **elaborated feedback**, discuss judgments, and the author revises in light of the exchange. Formative peer assessment aims to help learners plan learning, name strengths and weaknesses, and build metacognitive skill—not only to distribute marking load.

Practical implications for **peer-facing rubrics and checklists**:

- **Narrow criteria** (often one dimension per round) so peers can comment with evidence, not global praise.
- **Train before live use**—model criterion-bound comments on shared samples, then run a low-stakes round (see the peer training sequence in `feedback-revision-materials.md`).
- **Separate kindness from scoring**—stems and protocols live in feedback materials; **criteria rows and level language** live here.
- **Discuss and revise**—a peer rubric is incomplete without a revision-plan step tied to criteria.

For **discussion participation** or **collaboration** quality, pair simplified criteria with protocols in `discussion-collaboration-materials.md`. For **writing** peer review schedules and task design, see `writing-task-materials.md`.

### Exam-board and assessment-organization practice (reliability and fairness)

International Baccalaureate assessment documentation stresses that outcomes should be **meaningful, fair, and reliable**: fair in not favouring particular groups; reliable in that a candidate should receive the **same outcome** regardless of examiner or day. Principal examiners set a marking standard; other examiners are trained to mark to that standard through markschemes, training, and a **standard-setting quality model**—definitively marked **practice**, **qualification**, and **seed** scripts used to learn, prove, and monitor alignment to the principal examiner’s standard (IBO 2018 principles, *Standard setting—Preparing examiners for marking*).

IB materials distinguish **marks** (credit against a markscheme) from **grades** (judgment against a defined standard that may account for task difficulty). For subjective work, IB discusses **criteria marking** (separate aspects scored independently) versus **global impression** (holistic balance of features). Criteria marking can improve consistency but may **amplify small differences** across dimensions and sometimes feel less fair than holistic ranking when work is strong overall but uneven on listed criteria.

For **assessment criteria**, IB uses a **best-fit** model: the scorer chooses the achievement level that **overall best matches** the work; not every detail in a level descriptor must be met, and the highest level does not imply perfection. For **holistic markbands**, best-fit also applies, with an additional judgment about **which mark within the band range** best fits the descriptor. Programme-specific examiner guidance may add procedural reading orders for live marking; in the open IBO 2018 principles document, the instruction to **start at the highest mark in a sample and work down** (then up from the lowest) appears in **grade-award script review** when senior examiners set grade boundaries—not as the general definition of classroom markband judgment. Classroom authors should document their own borderline rules (reread adjacent descriptors, moderation on disputed samples) rather than assuming a single IB “read downward” rule for every rubric.

Cambridge Assessment Network **professional standards** frame practitioner competence across an assessment learning cycle: understand purposes and principles; **diagnose** issues in assessments you use; **judge quality** of existing or planned assessments; **improve** usefulness and validity; **innovate** with evidence. That lens helps authors review whether scoring materials actually measure the intended construct and support intended score use.

### Language proficiency descriptors (progression-aligned criteria)

ACTFL’s NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements describe what learners **can do** across proficiency levels in interpretive, interpersonal, and presentational communication (and intercultural competence). They are designed for goal-setting, curriculum targets, and **performance-based grading**—including rubric design aligned to proficiency continua. Indicators unpack **function, context, and text type** at sublevels, which is a useful model for writing criterion rows that stay observable rather than abstract.

### Evidence on feedback linked to criteria (actionability)

The Education Endowment Foundation’s feedback guidance argues that effective feedback should **address misunderstandings and support next steps**, and that **principles matter more than** whether feedback is written or verbal. Feedback can harm learning when poorly targeted. The EEF toolkit notes that high-quality feedback tends to focus on the **task, subject, and self-regulation strategies**, should be **specific and actionable**, and need not be limited to written marking—while workload must be managed.

Criteria materials should therefore leave room for **feedforward**: descriptors imply what stronger work would look like, not only what was missing.

### Professional testing standards (score use and fairness)

The AERA/APA/NCME *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* are the joint professional reference for **validity, fairness, reliability, scoring, and interpretation** in educational measurement. Classroom rubrics are not high-stakes licensure exams, but the standards still caution that **claims made from scores** must match the evidence collected, that **fairness and accessibility** are integral, and that design, administration, scoring, and use all matter. Authors should avoid over-claiming precision from coarse classroom rubrics.

### Science task-quality rubrics (complex performance)

The NGSS **EQuIP Rubric for Science** is a **criterion-based review** tool for lessons and units (not a generic classroom rubric). Its purpose is to review materials, give constructive feedback to developers, identify quality examples, and inform new design. It illustrates how **multi-criterion review** with explicit quality dimensions supports complex performances—useful when authoring investigation or lab scoring guides in science.

### Accessibility overlay (criteria and expression)

CAST’s UDL principle on **action and expression** reminds authors that there is no single optimal way to demonstrate learning. Criteria should not unintentionally punish learners for using **accessible formats** or supports when the construct being measured is not handwriting, oral fluency under stress, or a single media type—unless those are explicit learning targets.

## Key design decisions

### 1. Purpose: formative, summative, or both

Decide whether the material primarily:

- **Supports learning during production** (shared early, used for self- and peer assessment, revision)
- **Certifies achievement** (summative grade, external reporting, selection)
- **Does both** with clear rules (e.g., same criteria for draft feedback and final grade, but only final work scored for reporting)

Sadler’s distinction matters: formative use needs **actionable** criterion information, not only a holistic rank among peers. Summative use still needs **fairness and consistency**, often through moderation.

### 2. Analytic versus holistic structure

| Approach | Structure | Strengths | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** | Separate criteria, each with level descriptors; may sum or profile scores | Diagnostic feedback; can track class weaknesses by criterion; supports multi-grader alignment on dimensions | Can feel bureaucratic; **double-counting** if criteria overlap; summed scores may mis-rank holistic quality |
| **Holistic** | Single scale or markband describing overall quality | Faster scoring; can match how experts balance tradeoffs in complex work | Harder for learners to see what to improve; lower agreement between scorers without strong anchors |
| **Hybrid** | Holistic overall plus a few analytic dimensions | Balances speed and feedback | Must be designed so dimensions do not contradict the holistic judgment |

IB’s discussion of criteria versus global impression is a useful design checkpoint: choose criteria marking when dimensional feedback and scorer agreement on parts matter; recognize tradeoffs when dimensions are correlated or when strong overall work scores low on a checklist.

### 3. Number and independence of criteria

Criteria should map to **important, distinct features** of the construct—not every imaginable feature of good work.

- Aim for **manageable rows** teachers will actually use (often roughly three to six for classroom rubrics, more only when training and moderation are strong)
- Keep criteria **as independent as practical** (penalizing a missing conclusion in both “structure” and “argument” inflates penalty)
- Weight criteria **explicitly** in the task design if some matter more (IB 2018 principles note that more important criteria can carry **higher mark totals** when several criteria are combined)

Sadler warns that decomposing a configuration of qualities can **reduce validity** of the overall appraisal when criteria interlock.

### 4. Performance levels and descriptors

Levels should be **few enough to discriminate** but **few enough to describe well** (three to five is common in classroom materials; more levels demand richer anchors).

Descriptors should be:

- **Observable** (what is present in the work, not vague praise)
- **Parallel** across levels (same dimension at increasing quality)
- **Distinct** between adjacent levels (borderline cases are inevitable but should be resolvable by re-reading descriptors)
- **Aligned to the task** (genre-specific writing criteria differ from lab inquiry criteria)

Avoid empty labels (“good,” “satisfactory”) without behavioral detail.

### 5. Single-point, mastery, and standards-based tools

**Single-point (one-column) tools** list criteria at a **proficient** target only, with space for notes on strengths and gaps rather than pre-written below-level columns. Brookhart, McTighe, and Guskey (ASCD, 2024) argue that a true rubric requires **criteria, a performance scale, and level descriptors**; by that definition, a single-point grid is better named a **criterion-based feedback tool**—useful for formative work, not a drop-in substitute for a multi-level summative rubric unless you add descriptors, anchors, or a separate reporting scale.

| Use | Fit | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Formative self- and peer assessment** | Clear target quality; personalized feedforward in margins | Without training, peers may give vague comments |
| **Draft conferences** | Focus revision on one proficiency bar | Do not imply precision you have not described |
| **Summative grading** | Possible when paired with anchors, a markband, or explicit “exceeds” rules | Summing empty columns or ad hoc “minus” judgments drifts toward hidden holistic scoring |

**Mastery and standards-based reporting** often combine: (a) **milestone rubrics** on specific products, (b) a **proficiency profile** across criteria without a single sum, and (c) reporting categories (e.g., meets / approaches / exceeds) aligned to district or programme language. IB’s marks-versus-grades distinction and best-fit criteria judgment are one high-stakes version of separating **task evidence** from **reporting level**; elementary and secondary SBG systems need the same honesty—coarse level labels should not pretend to be fine-grained psychometric scores.

Practitioner articles on single-point rubrics (e.g., Fluckiger, 2010; classroom adaptations) are **supporting** tradition only; treat multi-level descriptor craft (CMU, Brookhart conference materials) as primary when stakes or multi-grader agreement matter.

### 6. Points, weights, and profiles

Decide whether scores are:

- **Holistic level only** (no numbers)
- **Criterion scores summed** (watch maximum totals and borderline arithmetic effects IB describes)
- **Proficiency profile** (separate ratings per criterion without a single sum)
- **Standards-based levels** (meets / approaches / exceeds mapped to reporting)

Numbers should follow judgment, not replace it—Sadler argues marks come **after** qualitative appraisal for complex outcomes.

### 7. Portfolio and multi-piece judgment

Portfolios and extended projects need **layered** scoring materials, not one grid pasted at the end.

| Layer | Typical artifact | Judgment focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Milestone** | Task-specific analytic or checklist rubric | Did this draft or product meet criteria for this stage? |
| **Cumulative** | Holistic markband or best-fit summary | How does the body of work hang together across revisions? |
| **Reporting** | Proficiency profile or standards levels | What level best describes the learner now, given multiple pieces? |

Authoring sequence:

1. **Align milestones to the arc**—map each deliverable to criteria the final product must show (project briefs in `project-performance-materials.md` define the arc; criteria materials define the rules).
2. **Keep milestone rubrics task-specific**—same construct names where possible so learners see progression.
3. **Reserve holistic bands for summative portfolio review**—judge tradeoffs across pieces (strong research, uneven presentation) with anchors drawn from **multiple** samples.
4. **Document revision credit**—decide whether growth between drafts changes the summative level or only formative feedback.
5. **Moderate on borderline portfolios**—same practice as single-task calibration: paired reads, anchor portfolios, recorded rationales.

Topping includes portfolios among products suitable for peer assessment; peer criteria for portfolios should focus on **one criterion per round** and evidence cited from named pieces, not whole-folder impressions.

### 8. Anchors and exemplars

Plan **anchor sets** at key levels (especially threshold and high quality). Annotate **why** the work exemplifies the level relative to criteria—not only the score.

Sadler recommends **multiple exemplars** per standard so learners see variety of successful approaches. Pair anchors with descriptors, not anchors alone.

### 9. Scorer training and moderation

For any shared scoring (department, district, exam team):

- **Clarify the construct** (“good history essay” = argument and evidence, not length)
- Use **practice scripts** and compare to agreed marks (IB practice / qualification / seed model; see IBO 2018 principles, *Standard setting*)
- Record **borderline decisions** and update guidance
- Separate **formative descriptive marking** from **summative numeric aggregation** when purposes differ

### 10. Learner-facing versus assessor-only materials

| Audience | Emphasize |
|---|---|
| **Students** | Criteria in accessible language, exemplars, self-assessment prompts, revision targets |
| **Teachers / assessors** | Borderline rules, best-fit guidance, tolerance notes, anti-bias reminders, worked scoring examples |
| **Peers** | Simplified criteria, sentence stems for constructive comments, focus on task not person |

Do not dump assessor-only technicalities on students without translation.

### 11. Fairness, access, and bias review

Review criteria for:

- **Construct underrepresentation** (missing a dimension that is part of the goal)
- **Construct-irrelevant demands** (penalizing spelling when the goal is scientific reasoning, unless conventions are explicit targets)
- **Bias toward particular groups** (cultural assumptions in “sophisticated” vocabulary, physical performance norms, access to materials for “authentic” products)
- **Multiple means of expression** where UDL applies

High-stakes use demands stronger review than low-stakes classroom drafts.

### 12. Link to feedback and revision

Criteria should connect to **next actions**: what would move this criterion one level up? EEF principles imply feedback must be usable, not merely evaluative.

Design rubric cells or companion guides with **feedforward prompts** (“to improve evidence, add…”).

## Authoring workflow

### Phase 1: Clarify the construct and evidence

1. State the **learning outcome** and what evidence the task elicits.
2. List **qualities of strong work** (brainstorm without levels).
3. Check alignment with the **task prompt** (criteria must match what students were asked to do).
4. Note **sharp versus fuzzy** criteria (correct citations vs. compelling argument).

### Phase 2: Choose structure and audience

5. Select **analytic, holistic, hybrid, single-point, or layered portfolio** approach based on purpose, time, and need for diagnostic feedback.
6. Decide **student access** timing (ideally with the task).
7. Decide **scoring method** (best-fit band, summed criteria, proficiency profile, milestone-plus-holistic portfolio).
8. For **peer use**, plan training and one-criterion rounds before publishing the peer-facing rubric (see `feedback-revision-materials.md`).

### Phase 3: Draft criteria and levels

9. Draft **criteria rows** (or holistic level descriptions, or single-point proficient targets).
10. Draft **level descriptors** for each row; use parallel language (skip pre-written below-level columns for single-point tools unless you add anchors separately).
11. Add **non-negotiables** (safety, word limit, required sections) separately from quality dimensions if they are pass/fail.
12. For **portfolios**, draft the **milestone rubric** first, then the **summative holistic** band or profile that applies across pieces.

#### Example fragment: three-criterion analytic rubric (original, illustrative)

The table below is a **compact classroom example** for a Grade 10 history argumentative essay—not copied from any publisher rubric. Adapt criteria, level counts, and language to your task and reporting system.

| Criterion | Level 1 — Beginning | Level 2 — Developing | Level 3 — Proficient | Level 4 — Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | Thesis missing, unclear, or unrelated to the prompt | Thesis states a position but is vague or only partly answers the prompt | Clear, defensible thesis that answers the prompt | Precise, nuanced thesis that frames a compelling historical argument |
| **Evidence** | Few or no relevant sources; evidence is mostly assertion | Some relevant evidence; quotations or facts often lack context or support | Multiple relevant pieces of evidence integrated with basic explanation | Well-selected evidence from varied sources; integration explains how evidence supports the claim |
| **Reasoning** | Little or no explanation linking evidence to the claim | Links are stated but incomplete, generic, or partly illogical | Logical links between most evidence and the claim; addresses an obvious counterpoint | Sustained, coherent reasoning; counterargument addressed and weighed |

Use this fragment to check **parallel structure** (each row names the same dimension at increasing quality), **observable language**, and **best-fit** judgment: assign the level that **overall** best matches the paper, then note borderline cases for moderation.

### Phase 4: Ground descriptors in evidence

13. Collect **sample work** (or write realistic samples); for portfolios, collect **anchor sets per milestone** plus one **whole-portfolio** sample.
14. Draft **anchors** at low, mid, and high (and borderline cases).
15. Test descriptors: does each sample land where intended?

### Phase 5: Pilot scoring and calibrate

16. Have two or more scorers mark the same set **without discussion**, then compare.
17. Revise ambiguous descriptors and **document decisions** for borderline cases.
18. Shorten or merge criteria that scorers cannot apply consistently.
19. For **peer rubrics**, pilot with trained students on sample work before live peer rounds.

### Phase 6: Publish and support use

20. Release **student-facing** rubric or simplified checklist with exemplars.
21. Provide **assessor guide** for summative use if needed.
22. Plan **formative checkpoints** where students use the rubric mid-process.
23. After live use, review **score distributions** and student revision patterns; revise for the next cycle.

## Quality criteria and review checks

Use this review before materials go live:

**Alignment**

- [ ] Each criterion maps to an stated outcome and to features of the task prompt
- [ ] Levels describe **quality**, not effort, neatness, or compliance alone (unless those are explicit goals)
- [ ] Construct-irrelevant demands are removed or labeled as separate pass/fail requirements

**Descriptor quality**

- [ ] Descriptors are observable and distinguish adjacent levels
- [ ] Language is parallel across levels within a criterion
- [ ] Jargon is defined or replaced for the intended audience

**Scoring logic**

- [ ] Method (holistic, analytic, best-fit, sum) is documented
- [ ] Overlap between criteria is minimized; double penalties are avoided
- [ ] Borderline and missing-component rules are specified

**Fairness and access**

- [ ] Materials reviewed for bias and inaccessible assumptions
- [ ] Alternative expressions are allowed when they meet the same criteria

**Formative utility**

- [ ] Students can use the tool to plan and revise before submission
- [ ] Criteria support specific next-step feedback, not only a grade

**Reliability (when multiple scorers or high stakes)**

- [ ] Anchor set and moderation plan exist
- [ ] Pilot scoring showed acceptable agreement or descriptors were tightened

**Peer and portfolio use**

- [ ] Peer rubrics are simplified and trained; revision loop is required (`feedback-revision-materials.md`)
- [ ] Portfolio materials distinguish milestone vs summative judgment layers

**Interpretation**

- [ ] Intended use of scores (feedback vs. gradebook vs. external report) is stated
- [ ] Limits of precision are honest (coarse rubrics should not imply false psychometric accuracy)

## Common anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Label-only rubric** (“Excellent / Good / Fair”) | Scorers and students guess what labels mean | Observable descriptors per level |
| **Kitchen-sink criteria** | Everything good writing ever includes | Few criteria tied to this task’s goals |
| **Copy-paste generic rubric** | Misaligned construct; vague feedback | Task-specific criteria from samples of real work |
| **Hidden rubric** | No formative self-monitoring | Share with task; use mid-process |
| **Rubric as comment substitute** | Circled cells without guidance | Brief criterion-linked comments or conferences |
| **Arithmetic without judgment** | Summed scores misrepresent strong uneven work | Best-fit bands or holistic review |
| **Single anchor** | Implies one “correct” way to excel | Multiple exemplars per level |
| **Criteria contradict instructions** | Penalizes students for following the prompt | Align task and rubric in one design pass |
| **Norm-referenced language in formative tools** | Compares students instead of standards | Standards-referenced descriptors |
| **Grading participation as personality** | Subjective and biased | Observable contribution behaviors |
| **Untrained peer rubric** | Vague or personal comments | Training + one criterion + revision plan |
| **Single-point for summative reporting** | Hidden holistic guessing | Multi-level descriptors, anchors, or markbands |
| **One rubric for whole portfolio** | Milestone quality invisible | Milestone rubrics plus summative profile or band |
| **Authority-free rubric templates or marketplaces** | No construct alignment, fairness, or calibration guidance | Source-grounded criteria from task samples and moderation practice |

## How Frameworks And Models Influence (Without Becoming) The Resource

| Lens | Influence on criteria materials |
|---|---|
| **Formative assessment** | Share standards early; emphasize self-monitoring; use scores to close gaps, not only to rank |
| **Standards-based / proficiency reporting** | Level descriptors match reporting categories; avoid mixing norm-referenced curves |
| **UDL** | Separate construct from format; allow equivalent demonstrations where appropriate |
| **Metacognition / self-regulation** | Add self-assessment columns or reflection prompts tied to criteria |
| **Peer assessment** | Simplify language; train with anchors; separate kind comments from criterion scores |
| **Project-based learning** | Criteria for inquiry, authenticity, revision, and product quality—often multiple rubrics over milestones |
| **World-language proficiency** | Can-Do-aligned functions, contexts, and text types as criterion structure |

The deliverable remains a **tangible rubric or marking guide**, not the framework document itself.

## Relationships to nearby resource families

- **Success and exemplar materials** supply the **models**; criteria materials supply the **rules and levels** for judging work against those models. Sadler treats verbal standards and exemplars as complementary.
- **Feedback and revision materials** consume judgments from criteria and turn them into **actions**; weak criteria produce weak feedback even when comments are plentiful.
- **Assessment instruments** define tasks and evidence; criteria materials define **how that evidence is valued**.
- **Writing-task materials** should be co-designed with writing rubrics so prompts elicit the qualities the rubric rewards.
- **Project and performance materials** often need **milestone rubrics** plus a summative holistic judgment of the whole project.
- **Discussion and collaboration materials** may use participation rubrics focused on **observable discourse moves**, not social liking.

## Quick reference: rubric types at a glance

| Type | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic rubric** | Diagnostic feedback, multi-grader projects, tracking class needs by dimension | Overlap, summing distortion |
| **Holistic rubric / markbands** | Fast scoring, performances with tradeoffs, expert holistic judgment | Vague levels; low scorer agreement without anchors |
| **Single-point / mastery tool** | Clear proficient target; formative feedforward in margins | Not a full multi-level rubric by itself (Brookhart); weak for summative use without anchors or bands |
| **Checklist** | Required elements, procedural labs, pass/fail conventions | Confused with quality judgment |
| **Marking guide (narrative)** | Complex tasks, best-fit professional judgment | Requires training and anchors |
| **Anchor set + brief descriptors** | Calibration sessions, moderation | Not sufficient alone for novices without criteria language |
| **Milestone + portfolio profile** | Extended projects, SBG reporting | Layer milestone rubrics with holistic or proficiency summary; see §7 |
