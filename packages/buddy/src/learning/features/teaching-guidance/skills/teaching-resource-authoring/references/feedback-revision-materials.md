# Feedback And Revision Materials

Operational handbook for designing, reviewing, and improving tangible artifacts that help learners receive information about their work, close gaps against standards or goals, and revise productively. This family covers **materials**—forms, trackers, protocols, prompts, and response templates—not feedback as a teaching method in the abstract.

---

## What This Family Is And What Job It Performs

Feedback and revision materials are **concrete tools** that structure how information about performance moves between people (or from a learner to themselves) and how that information becomes **revision action**. Their job is to:

1. Make the **reference level** visible (what good work looks like, what the task requires, what success means).
2. Surface the **gap** between current performance and that reference level in usable form.
3. Support **closure of the gap** through specific next steps, not merely documentation of error.

Evidence-based practice treats feedback as powerful but uneven: it can accelerate learning or undermine it, and it carries **time and motivational costs** for teachers and learners. Well-designed materials therefore focus on **principles and closure of the feedback loop**, not on volume of comments or a single delivery mode (written vs verbal).

A useful distinction from systems theory (Sadler, 1989; Ramaprasad, 1983, as cited there): information about a gap counts as feedback **only when it is used to alter the gap**. Marks recorded for a gradebook, comments learners never read, or peer notes with no revision step are **dangling data**, not feedback—regardless of how carefully they were written.

## Artifacts In The Family

### Included

| Artifact type | Typical function |
|---------------|------------------|
| **Teacher feedback forms / comment frames** | Structured prompts so feedback targets task, process, or self-regulation—not vague praise or personality |
| **Conference / feedback conference notes** | Capture verbal feedback and agreed next steps; link to a revision plan |
| **Revision checklists and response-to-feedback templates** | Require learners to show what changed, why, and against which criterion |
| **Error-analysis sheets** | Classify mistakes, identify pattern, plan correction (especially in procedural domains) |
| **Peer-feedback forms and protocols** | Criteria-bound comments, training scaffolds, and accountable revision steps |
| **Feedforward / “where to next” planners** | Forward-looking actions tied to goals, not only evaluation of past work |
| **Self-monitoring and self-assessment sheets** | Learner compares own work to standards during or after production |
| **Revision trackers / iteration logs** | Document draft cycles, criteria addressed, and evidence of improvement (not mere compliance ticks) |
| **Whole-class feedback capture sheets** | Summarize common gaps and assign targeted follow-up tasks |

### Keep separate (nearby families)

| Nearby family | Contrast |
|---------------|----------|
| **Criteria and scoring materials** (rubrics, mark schemes) | Define standards and levels; feedback materials **apply** those standards to specific work and drive revision |
| **Success exemplar materials** | Show what quality looks like; feedback materials **compare** learner work to goals and prescribe change |
| **Assessment instruments** | Summative judgment and reporting; feedback materials aim at **improvement before or between** high-stakes judgments |
| **Study and revision materials** | Independent rehearsal and scheduling; use when the main job is practice/spacing, not responding to performance on a submitted task |
| **Writing-task / project briefs** | Set the task and success criteria; feedback materials operate **on drafts or products** already in progress |
| **Practice worksheets** | Initial learning and retrieval; feedback materials assume a **performance to evaluate** and revise |

Formative assessment, metacognition, explicit instruction, and accessibility (UDL) may **inform** design but are not themselves this family.

## When This Family Is Useful

Use feedback and revision materials when:

- Learners produce **extended or complex work** (writing, investigations, projects, performances) where quality is judged on multiple criteria, not only right/wrong answers.
- The instructional goal includes **iterative improvement** (draft cycles, redrafting, error correction, skill refinement).
- Teachers need to **close the loop**: ensure comments lead to observable changes in a subsequent attempt.
- **Peer or self-assessment** is intentional, with training and criteria—not as a workload shortcut.
- Formative evidence (from instruction, questioning, or short tasks) has already identified **specific gaps** worth addressing (EEF Recommendation 1).

Materials work best when paired with:

- Clear **learning intentions** and criteria learners can understand.
- **Time and structure** to act on feedback before the next summative checkpoint.
- Trust and motivation sufficient for learners to engage with corrective information (EEF Recommendation 3).

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit Or Commonly Misused

Avoid or redesign when:

- The task is **purely binary** (single correct answer) and a brief correction suffices—full feedback protocols add little.
- There is **no opportunity to revise**; feedback becomes summative labeling only.
- Materials emphasize **grades, scores, or ranking** without actionable detail (Sadler: deeply coded summaries can block formative use; Shute and others warn that grades plus comments may lead learners to ignore comments).
- **Volume substitutes for quality**—long written banks, triple-marking policies, or peer forms filled for compliance.
- Feedback targets **the learner as a person** (“you’re brilliant at maths”) rather than the work and strategies (EEF; Hattie & Timperley self-level; Shute: focus on task not learner).
- Peer feedback runs **without criteria, modeling, training, or accountability** for revision.
- Trackers record **that** feedback happened (signatures, tick boxes) but not **what changed** in the work.
- School policy **over-specifies timing or method** (e.g., mandatory written feedback every lesson) instead of principles and professional judgment (EEF Recommendation 6).

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

### Education Endowment Foundation — teacher feedback guidance and toolkit

**Contribution:** Implementation-facing principles for schools and classrooms; workload and policy cautions; examples of effective vs ineffective comment types.

**Core ideas for authoring:**

- Six recommendations: (1) lay foundations with strong instruction and formative assessment; (2) deliver appropriately timed feedback that moves learning forward; (3) plan how pupils **receive and use** feedback; (4) purposeful, time-efficient **written** feedback; (5) purposeful **verbal** feedback; (6) school policies that prioritize principles over method fads.
- Three underpinning principles: high-quality initial teaching + formative assessment; feedback timed to close gaps; deliberate strategies so pupils **act** on feedback.
- Effective feedback often addresses **task**, **subject** (disciplinary conventions), and **self-regulation strategies**—not personal characteristics or vague generalities.
- Written and verbal feedback can both work; **content and use matter more than medium** (guidance report). On the EEF **Teaching & Learning Toolkit** feedback strand, synthesis meta-analyses report roughly **+7 months** progress equivalent for oral/verbal feedback and **+5** for written feedback (overall strand about **+6**); treat these as strand-level summaries, not classroom guarantees. Schools should still use a **range** of methods and monitor workload.
- Feedback when work is **correct** matters, not only error correction.
- Timing: no single rule—judge immediate vs delayed feedback by task, learner, and class understanding.

### John Hattie and Helen Timperley — *The Power of Feedback*

**Contribution:** Conceptual model for **what** feedback should answer and **at what level** comments operate.

**Core ideas for authoring:**

- Feedback is information about performance or understanding relative to goals; impact varies widely—some feedback harms learning.
- Three questions: **Where am I going?** (feed up / goals); **How am I going?** (feed back / progress); **Where to next?** (feed forward / next steps). Materials should make these questions explicit where appropriate.
- Four levels: **task** (correctness, completeness); **process** (strategies, approaches); **self-regulation** (monitoring, directing effort); **self** (personal traits/affect). Task, process, and self-regulation levels generally support learning better than self-level comments.
- Novices often need more **task-level** guidance; more proficient learners may benefit more from process and self-regulation prompts (as summarized in later practice literature building on this model).
- Feedback fills a gap between current and intended understanding; without a learning context and goal, information has little effect.

### Valerie Shute — *Focus on Formative Feedback*

**Contribution:** Design features of formative feedback messages and timing, with prescriptive guidelines for authors.

**Core ideas for authoring:**

- Formative feedback: communicated to the learner to **modify thinking or behavior** to improve learning; should be nonevaluative, supportive, timely, and specific (among other qualities discussed in the review).
- Design interacts with **instruction** (goals, tasks) and **learner** characteristics—one size does not fit all.
- **Do:** focus on the task; provide elaborated feedback (what/how/why) in **manageable units**; be specific and link to goals; reduce uncertainty between performance and goals; promote learning orientation; give feedback after learners attempt a solution.
- **Avoid:** normative comparisons with peers; relying on **overall grades** without usable comments; discouraging or controlling messages; excessive praise that shifts attention to self; interrupting engaged learners; progressive hints that always end with the answer without learner effort.
- **Timing:** immediate feedback often helps on **difficult new** tasks and procedural/conceptual retention; delayed feedback may support **transfer** on simpler tasks; adapt to prior knowledge and achievement level.

### D. Royce Sadler — formative assessment and instructional systems

**Contribution:** Why feedback materials must build **evaluative judgment**, not only deliver teacher judgments.

**Core ideas for authoring:**

- Learners need (a) a concept of the **standard** aimed for, (b) ability to **compare** current work to that standard, and (c) **action** that closes the gap—these operate together, including during production (self-monitoring).
- Summative grades can be **counterproductive** for formative purposes when they function as one-way ciphers.
- Instructional systems should provide **authentic evaluative experience** so students develop tactics to improve their own work, not depend permanently on external correction.
- Materials should support transition from **external feedback** to **self-monitoring** (e.g., self-assessment against criteria before teacher review).

### Peer feedback — evidence base (sourced)

Reports fold peer protocols into this family rather than naming a peer-only authority. Authoring should still **ground peer materials** in the same loop-closure logic as teacher feedback, with sources that address **training, focus, and revision use**.

**WWC — *Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively* (grades 6–12).** The expert panel treats structured peer response as part of effective writing cycles when partners comment on **specific features** tied to goals (not global opinion), after **models and practice**, and when authors turn input into **goal-based revision** before the next draft. Peer forms should limit each round to **one or two criteria**, supply stems, and require a **revision plan** with evidence of change—mirroring the training sequence in [Artifact Patterns](#artifact-patterns). Full task packets and model–practice–reflect sequencing live in **writing-task materials**; this family owns the **feedback loop** on drafts.

**EEF feedback guidance.** Recommendation 3 requires deliberate strategies so pupils **receive and use** feedback; peer sheets are one delivery mode, not a compliance substitute for teacher judgment. Principles on task/subject/self-regulation focus and workload apply to peer prompts as they do to written comments.

**Sadler — evaluative judgment.** Peer notes are formative only when authors can **compare** work to a visible standard and **act** on the gap. Untrained or criteria-free peer response produces dangling data, not feedback.

**PBL critique and revision.** When peer response sits inside a sustained project cycle (draft critiques, gallery walks, public product revision), use **project-performance materials** for choreography; standalone peer protocols still follow loop-closure rules here.

### Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center — rubrics (background)

**Contribution:** Rubric-mediated feedback: criteria and levels as shared reference for comments and learner self-checks.

- Rubrics can support **formative** monitoring while learners work and when returned with work.
- Feedback materials should **align** to rubric criteria rows, not duplicate entire rubrics.

Use rubric examples to understand criterion-linked feedback; author fresh descriptors and comments matched to the local task.

## Key Design Decisions

### 1. Purpose of the artifact

Decide whether the material primarily:

- **Delivers** feedback (teacher or peer comment structure),
- **Elicits** self-assessment (learner judges own work first),
- **Records** a feedback conversation (conference notes),
- **Forces revision evidence** (response template, redraft log), or
- **Coordinates** class-wide reteaching from common errors.

One sheet can combine roles, but clarity reduces misuse.

### 2. Reference level

Specify what “good” means on this artifact:

- Learning intention / success criteria in learner language
- Link to rubric criterion or mark-scheme bullet (by reference, not pasted proprietary text)
- Annotated exemplar features (describe qualities, do not reproduce copyrighted exemplars)
- Disciplinary conventions (EEF “subject” level)

Without a visible reference level, comments drift to vague encouragement.

### 3. Level of feedback (Hattie & Timperley)

For each prompt or comment bank entry, tag or structure by level:

| Level | Example material prompt |
|-------|-------------------------|
| Task | “Your thesis is not yet stated in the introduction—add one sentence stating your claim.” |
| Process | “Try the strategy we used for source integration: quote, paraphrase, then explain relevance.” |
| Self-regulation | “Before submitting, check your plan against the success criteria—mark each criterion met or not.” |
| Self (usually avoid) | — |

Match level to learner stage: more task support for novices; more process/self-regulation for advancing learners.

### 4. Feed up, back, and forward

Ensure materials address:

- **Goals** (where am I going?)—especially on self-assessment and peer forms used *before* drafting is “finished.”
- **Current performance** (how am I going?)—specific, evidence-linked.
- **Next steps** (where to next?)—actions the learner can take in the **next attempt** or remaining time.

A revision tracker that only lists “received feedback” without forward actions fails the loop.

### 5. Timing and occasion

Design for when the artifact is used:

| Occasion | Design implication |
|----------|-------------------|
| During production | Short self-checks; don’t interrupt deep work with lengthy forms (Shute) |
| Mid-process conference | Space for dialogue notes + one or two prioritized actions |
| After draft submission | Targeted comments + mandatory response section before next draft |
| After summative work | Usually poor fit unless resubmission or exam improvement cycle exists |

EEF: teachers judge immediate vs delayed feedback from task and learner needs—materials should not assume one schedule for all subjects.

### 6. Mode: written, verbal, peer, self

- **Written** materials: optimize for specificity and action; avoid comment length that learners cannot process (Shute: manageable units).
- **Verbal conference forms**: capture agreements; learner restates next steps in writing to reinforce reception (EEF Recommendation 3).
- **Peer**: include criteria, sentence stems, warm/cool or glow/grow structures **tied to standards**, plus signer accountability (“I will change X in section Y”).
- **Self**: require comparison to criteria or exemplar features before external feedback when possible (Sadler).

### 7. Workload and sustainability

- Teacher-facing: comment banks should be **reusable patterns** aligned to recurring gaps, not unique essays per pupil for every draft.
- School-facing: policies embedded in materials should emphasize principles and monitoring **pupil use**, not minimum comment counts (EEF Recommendation 6).
- Prefer **light-touch whole-class feedback** plus targeted individual notes when many share the same gap (EEF practice examples).

### 8. Accessibility and inclusion

Without turning UDL into the resource itself:

- Offer **multiple ways to receive** feedback (written, oral, audio, conference) where feasible.
- Use plain language, sufficient white space, and structured fields for learners with literacy or attention needs.
- Avoid feedback that depends on hidden cultural knowledge unless the goal explicitly teaches that dimension.
- Consider confidence and trust: materials can include brief prompts for learners to note what they need clarified (EEF: motivation and capacity to receive information affect effectiveness).

## Practical Authoring Workflow

1. **Anchor to goals** — State the learning intention and success criteria the feedback will address (from task design or rubric). If goals are unclear, fix the task before authoring feedback forms.

2. **Identify typical gaps** — From prior student work or formative checks, list 3–5 recurring gaps (misconceptions, missing criteria, weak strategies).

3. **Choose artifact type** — Match to instructional moment (mid-draft conference form vs end-of-draft response sheet vs peer protocol).

4. **Draft comment stems / prompts** — At task or process/self-regulation level; include feed-forward actions. Remove personality praise and normative peer comparison language.

5. **Build the learner response section** — Mandatory fields: “What I was told / noticed,” “What I changed,” “Where in my work,” “Which criterion this improves.” For peer feedback, require author revision plan.

6. **Pilot with one piece of work** — Apply the form to a real or realistic sample; check whether a colleague could act on it without asking clarifying questions.

7. **Trim and chunk** — Split overloaded forms; use manageable units (Shute).

8. **Pair with time to revise** — Schedule revision in the lesson sequence; materials alone do not create opportunity.

9. **Review for loop closure** — Ask: If a learner completed only this form, would evidence show the gap closing?

10. **Document version and context** — Age, subject, draft number, and linked criteria—so materials are not reused in mismatched contexts.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

Use this checklist before publishing or classroom use:

**Loop closure**

- [ ] Defines or points to a clear reference level (criteria, goals, or standards).
- [ ] Requires or supports **observable revision** or next-step action.
- [ ] Treats information as feedback only if the learner (or teacher on their behalf) can **use** it to alter the gap.

**Content quality (EEF / Hattie / Shute)**

- [ ] Comments/prompts are **specific** and tied to the work, not generic (“good effort”).
- [ ] Emphasizes task, subject conventions, and/or self-regulation—not learner personality.
- [ ] Includes **where to next** actions, not only error identification.
- [ ] Addresses strengths and correct work where relevant—not errors only.
- [ ] Elaboration is **chunked**; form length is proportionate to learner capacity.

**Peer and self components**

- [ ] Peer forms include criteria, training cues, and accountability for revision.
- [ ] Self-assessment prompts compare work to standards, not vague “rate yourself 1–5.”

**Policy and workload**

- [ ] Avoids mandating inefficient rituals (e.g., evidence of triple marking without impact checks).
- [ ] Teacher time is proportional to expected learning gain.

**Fairness and clarity**

- [ ] Language is accessible to the intended age group.
- [ ] Criteria apply equitably across learners (no hidden cultural bias unless taught).

**Boundary hygiene**

- [ ] Not a disguised rubric-only sheet without feedback use.
- [ ] Not a grade record with cosmetic comments.

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|--------------|--------------|------------------|
| Praise sandwich without criteria | Attention to self; no gap closure | Criteria-linked strength + specific next step |
| Comment banks of vague positives | Low learning impact | Task/process stems tied to subject gaps |
| “Tick and flick” marking codes without learner response | No loop closure | Codes + required student explanation of fix |
| Peer “two stars and a wish” with no standards | Arbitrary peer opinions | Criteria-bound stems + revision plan |
| Revision tracker with dates only | Compliance audit | Criterion-linked change log |
| Feedback on every error at once | Cognitive overload | Prioritize 2–3 actionable targets (Shute) |
| Grade at top, ignored comments | Learners focus on grade (Shute; Sadler) | Separate formative response from summative grade when possible |
| Copying entire rubric onto feedback form | Duplication, not diagnosis | Reference criterion numbers/names; comment on evidence |
| Identical feedback for all pupils when gaps differ | Mismatch to need | Whole-class feedforward + targeted individual notes |
| Digital comments learners cannot access or act on | Dangling data | Confirm access, time, and revision opportunity |

## How Frameworks Influence These Materials (Without Becoming The Resource)

| Lens | Influence on materials |
|------|------------------------|
| **Formative assessment** | Materials embody elicitation → interpretation → action cycles; they are not “the assessment” itself. |
| **Metacognition / self-regulated learning** | Include planning, monitoring, and evaluating prompts on response sheets; link feedback to strategies learners can reuse. EEF metacognition guidance is the usual cross-family companion for these scaffolds. |
| **Visible thinking / reflection routines** | Conference and peer protocols can use repeatable reflection question sets (e.g., Project Zero–style thinking routines) when the goal is structured metacognitive talk—not as a substitute for criteria-bound feedback. |
| **Explicit instruction** | Feedback forms can reference taught strategies and models by name learners already know. |
| **UDL** | Multiple means to receive and act on feedback; clear layout; optional supports—not a separate UDL checklist resource. |
| **Differentiation** | Adjust number of targets, scaffolding density, and level of comment—not different standards without transparency. |

## Relationships To Nearby Resource Families

- **Criteria and scoring materials:** Provide the shared language of quality; feedback materials apply that language to **individual work** and demand revision evidence.
- **Success exemplar materials:** Clarify standards visually or through models; feedback materials reference exemplar **features** learners should approximate.
- **Writing-task and project-performance materials:** Set tasks and milestones; feedback materials attach to **drafts, rehearsals, or products** within those cycles.
- **Practice and study materials:** Build capability through rehearsal; feedback materials respond to **performance attempts** on authentic tasks.
- **Assessment instruments:** Judge attainment for reporting; use feedback materials **before** or between summative events unless the assessment design includes resubmission.
- **Study and revision materials:** **The Learning Scientists** and similar toolkits own **spacing, retrieval, and study-method** posters and packs. Feedback-revision materials may ask how a learner will **rehearse a corrected idea** on a response sheet, but should not duplicate strategy-poster authoring for those tools.
- **Criteria and scoring materials (formal programmes):** **IB** and other exam-board traditions stress **marks vs grades**, **best-fit** criterion judgment, and **marking consistency** (moderation, seed scripts). Feedback forms in formal contexts should reference **markscheme criteria by name**, separate formative response from summative codes when possible, and align peer/self comments to the same criteria rows assessors use—see **criteria-scoring materials** for best-fit and moderation design; IBO open assessment-principles PDFs for programme-level marking logic.

When a single handout mixes brief creation (e.g., a new paragraph frame) with extensive feedback response, consider splitting so each family’s job stays clear.

## Artifact Patterns (Authoring Sketches)

These are **structural patterns**, not copy-ready templates.

### Teacher written feedback + student response

- Section A (teacher): 2–3 prioritized targets tagged task/process/self-regulation; one strength tied to criteria; feed-forward action.
- Section B (student): Restate targets in own words; quote or describe where revised; indicate draft version.

### Peer feedback protocol

Run only after **Peer feedback training (minimal sequence)** below (or equivalent modeling and practice).

- Step 1: Silent read against one criterion.
- Step 2: Comments only on criterion (stems provided).
- Step 3: Author questions peer for clarification.
- Step 4: Author records revision plan before end of lesson.

### Error-analysis sheet (procedural domains)

Use when the performance is **procedural or multi-step** (mathematics, science calculations, coding traces, technical procedures) and the instructional goal is to **close a gap on the next attempt**, not only to mark wrong answers.

**Core fields (all domains)**

- Problem / attempt reproduced or referenced.
- **Gap against reference** — which criterion, subgoal, or taught step failed (link to rubric row or success criterion by name, not pasted proprietary text).
- Error type (see STEM table below)—learner classified with a **hint bank** tied to taught strategies, not invented categories.
- **Corrective strategy** chosen from the **instructional repertoire** learners already know (model, representation, checklist, worked-example subgoal).
- **New attempt** or structurally similar problem, with space to show the changed step or representation.
- **Loop evidence** — what is different in the second attempt and which gap it addresses (Sadler; EEF closure).

**STEM depth — WWC elementary mathematics intervention**

*Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades* (IES/WWC Practice Guide 26) is the strongest federal anchor for **error-linked reteaching** in procedural mathematics. It is **elementary-focused**, but its recommendation set maps cleanly onto error-analysis authoring in secondary STEM when procedures depend on representations and language.

| WWC recommendation | What to surface on the error-analysis sheet |
|--------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **Systematic instruction** | Name the **subgoal** that broke (e.g., “set up equation” vs “solve”); corrective path follows the **same step order** taught in class, not a new shortcut. |
| **Mathematical language** | Learner restates the mistake using **precise terms** from instruction; correction includes a vocabulary or notation fix where language caused the slip. |
| **Representations** | Identify whether the error came from misreading a **diagram, table, concrete model, or symbolic form**; correction shows the representation used correctly on the new attempt. |
| **Number line** | For magnitude, operations, or fraction/ratio work, require a **number-line sketch or interval mark** when that representation is part of the taught method. |
| **Word problems** | Separate **story comprehension** errors from **calculation** errors; correction may require re-understanding the situation before recomputing. |
| **Timed activities (fluency)** | Distinguish **automaticity slips** from **conceptual/procedural** gaps; fluency fixes use brief, targeted practice—not relabeling every mistake as “careless.” |

**Error-type coding (STEM-oriented)**

| Type | Typical signal | Feedback / correction emphasis |
|------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Conceptual** | Wrong idea persists on a new problem of the same type | Reteach with **representation change** or counterexample; link to diagnostic or worked-example materials—not only “try again.” |
| **Procedural** | Right idea, wrong step, algorithm, or order | **Process-level** feedback (Hattie); walk through taught subgoals; faded completion if available from worked-example materials. |
| **Careless / transcription** | Inconsistent with other work; arithmetic slip with sound method | Short **task-level** fix; verify with one similar item; do not skip loop closure—still require evidence on the new attempt. |

**Anti-patterns specific to STEM error sheets**

- Listing every arithmetic mistake without **prioritizing** 2–3 actionable targets (Shute).
- Corrective strategies not in the **taught repertoire** (“use a trick you never learned in class”).
- Error analysis with **no new attempt**—becomes annotation only.
- Treating **diagnostic placement** as feedback; probes **identify** gaps (diagnostic-instruments family), error-analysis sheets **close** them on authentic work.

For **writing** peer and feature-focused revision cycles, use WWC secondary writing (peer subsection above) and **writing-task materials**; for **initial models** after a procedural gap, cross-link **worked-example materials**.

### Revision tracker across drafts

| Draft | Criteria focus | Feedback source | Change made (evidence) | Criterion met? |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|------------------------|----------------|
| 1 | … | teacher / self / peer | … | Y/N |

### Conference note

- Goal for session (feed up).
- Observed gap with evidence from work (feed back).
- Agreed actions and deadline (feed forward).
- Learner signature or initials confirming understanding.

### Whole-class feedback capture sheet

Use after reviewing a batch of drafts or exit tickets—not instead of individual notes when gaps differ sharply.

- **Common gaps (2–4):** Name each gap in criteria language; note approximate prevalence (e.g., “about half the class”).
- **Whole-class reteach / mini-lesson:** One short action for the next lesson (model, worked example, or discussion prompt).
- **Targeted follow-up tasks:** Assign by gap (Group A / B / C) or by checklist tick—learners complete only tasks that match their work.
- **Individual exceptions:** Small space for pupils who need a different target from the class list.
- **Loop check:** Date for a short revisit (exit ticket, spot-check paragraph, or draft section) to confirm the gap closed.

Aligns with EEF workload guidance: light-touch whole-class feedback plus targeted individual notes when many share the same gap.

### Feedforward planner

Forward-looking; usable before, during, or after a draft. Pair with self-assessment when possible (Sadler: compare to standard before external review).

| Field | Feed up / back / forward |
|-------|-------------------------|
| **Goal for this piece** (criterion or learning intention in learner language) | Feed up |
| **What is working so far** (evidence from the work) | Feed back (strength) |
| **Current gap** (one priority; evidence-linked) | Feed back |
| **Next actions** (2–3 concrete steps for the remaining time or next draft) | Feed forward |
| **Strategy or resource** (taught approach, checklist row, conference note) | Supports self-regulation |
| **Evidence I will add or change** (where in the product) | Closes the loop |

Keep one priority gap per planner pass; add another sheet or row for a second cycle if needed (Shute: manageable units).

### Peer feedback training (minimal sequence)

Peer forms fail without training. A short, repeatable sequence satisfies quality-checklist “training cues” and EEF Recommendation 3 (deliberate strategies so pupils **act on** feedback):

1. **Model criteria-bound comments** — Teacher thinks aloud on a sample paragraph or problem: name the criterion, point to evidence, state one feed-forward action. Contrast with vague praise or personality comments (anti-pattern table).
2. **Practice on a shared sample** — Pairs or triads comment on **teacher-provided** work (not a classmate’s live draft first). Use the same stems and criterion focus as the real protocol.
3. **Norm the protocol** — Walk through steps (silent read → criterion-only comments → author clarification → revision plan). Clarify that authors must **change** the work, not only receive notes.
4. **First live round with one criterion** — Narrow scope; teacher circulates and redirects off-criterion or comparative comments.
5. **Debrief and loop** — Two-minute share: what revision will you make? Collect planners or response sections before the next draft.

Re-run steps 2–4 when criteria or genre change. Peer training is not a one-time assembly.

## Miniature Worked Example (Fictive Specimen)

Illustrates **one criterion** on a teacher written-feedback + student response form (Grade 9 persuasive writing, Draft 1). Labels and layout are yours to adapt; content is invented for authoring reference only.

**Criterion (feed up):** C1 — Thesis stated clearly in the introduction.

**Section A — Teacher (prioritized targets)**

| # | Level | Comment |
|---|-------|---------|
| 1 | Task | Your introduction summarizes the topic but does not yet state a claim I can agree or disagree with. Add one sentence that answers: “What do you want the reader to believe?” |
| 2 | Process | Use the class strategy: **claim → because → so what** in the opening paragraph. Your body reasons are stronger than your opening—lift one reason into the intro as a preview. |
| Strength | Task | Your evidence in paragraph 2 cites the source and explains why it matters (meets C3). |

**Feed forward:** Revise only the introduction before Draft 2; leave body paragraphs unless you move a reason into the intro.

**Section B — Student response (mandatory before Draft 2)**

- **What I was told:** I need an explicit claim in the introduction, using claim–because–so what.
- **What I changed:** Added: “Schools should delay first bell by 30 minutes **because** adolescent sleep cycles shift later, **so** attendance and morning focus improve.” Removed the old opening sentence that only defined the issue.
