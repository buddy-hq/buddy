# Writing-Task Materials

Operational handbook for teachers and instructional designers who create, review, and improve **writing-task materials**: the concrete artifacts that specify what students will write, how they will plan and produce it, and how they will evaluate and revise it before submission.

This family’s **strongest causal evidence** sits in secondary writing instruction (grades 6–12). For **elementary and upper-elementary** classrooms (typically grades K–5), pair the secondary guide’s transferable design habits—explicit strategies, process teaching, reading integration, formative use of writing—with the separate WWC elementary writing practice guide (see **Evidence and source traditions**). Do not apply secondary-only recommendations (for example, long source-based argument packets) without developmental adjustment.

---

## What This Family Is For

Writing-task materials do the pedagogical work of turning a writing goal into **actionable student work**. They answer questions such as:

- What must be written, for whom, and toward what purpose?
- What thinking and drafting moves should students use?
- What sources, models, or reading will shape the draft?
- How will students reflect, get peer input, and revise?

Effective writing, in the tradition anchored by the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) secondary writing practice guide, means text that **achieves the writer’s goals**, fits **audience and context**, presents ideas **clearly**, and elicits the **intended response** from the reader. Writing-task materials should make those dimensions visible before students draft—not only on a scoring sheet afterward.

Writing also functions as a **thinking tool** across disciplines: the secondary writing practice guide expects students to practice strategies **across disciplines**, with different topics, audiences, purposes, and evidence types—not a single “school essay” template for every subject. Task materials should name discipline-appropriate evidence norms, genres, and audiences for each assignment.

---

## Artifacts In This Family

| Artifact type | Typical role |
|---|---|
| **Writing prompts and assignment briefs** | Define genre, audience, purpose, scope, sources, length, and submission expectations |
| **Planning and goal-setting sheets** | Elicit topic knowledge, research questions, outlines, thesis/claim development, individualized goals |
| **Strategy instruction handouts** | Teach named moves for planning, drafting, evaluating, revising, and editing (not mere topic reminders) |
| **Model–practice–reflect cycles** | Pair modeled thinking with guided practice and structured reflection on strategy use |
| **Drafting scaffolds** | Section planners, sentence starters tied to cognitive strategies, graphic organizers tied to genre |
| **Peer-review and response protocols** | Focused partner or group feedback on criteria-linked features (not open-ended “what do you think?”) |
| **Revision handouts** | Diagnose–operate routines, comparison to goals, change logs between drafts |
| **Source-based writing packets** | Integrate readings with writing moves (summary, synthesis, argument from evidence) |
| **Reflection and self-evaluation tools** | Post-draft analysis of strategy effectiveness, rubric-aligned self-checks, portfolio reflections |
| **Formative on-demand writing prompts** | Short tasks used to reveal skill before or during instruction (when bundled with interpretation guidance) |

Materials may be **one page or a multi-part packet** (prompt + planner + peer form + revision log). What matters is that they form a **coherent production pathway**, not a pile of disconnected worksheets.

### Keep Separate: Nearby Families

| Nearby family | Boundary |
|---|---|
| **Criteria and scoring materials** | Rubrics, marking guides, and level descriptors judge quality; they may attach to a writing task but are not the task itself |
| **Feedback and revision materials** | Conference notes, teacher comment banks, and revision trackers often follow a task; authoring them is a distinct design job |
| **Reading materials** | Passages, source sets, and annotation guides supply input; writing-task materials **use** reading but should not be mistaken for a reading packet with no writing outcome |
| **Success and exemplar materials** | Annotated model papers and anchor sets teach “what good looks like”; the **assignment** still belongs here |
| **Question and item sets** | Short constructed responses can overlap; full **extended writing tasks** with process support belong here |
| **Assessment instruments** | Summative exams and large-scale tests; use writing-task design for **classroom production and formative checkpoints**, not to duplicate a full test blueprint |
| **Practice worksheets** | Skill drills without a communicative writing product (e.g., isolated grammar pages) are practice materials, not writing tasks |
| **Project and performance materials** | Multi-week authentic projects with milestones and public products; single-class or few-period **writing assignments** stay in this family |

When a unit needs both a **project brief** and **embedded writing prompts**, author each artifact against its family’s handbook rather than blending genres into one undifferentiated document.

---

## When To Use This Family

Writing-task materials are a strong fit when:

- Students need to **produce extended or structured text** (argument, explanation, narrative, correspondence, disciplinary report).
- Instruction intentionally teaches **writing strategies** and expects students to **select and adapt** strategies for audience and purpose.
- The curriculum integrates **reading and writing** so students notice text features they should emulate.
- Teachers will use student writing to **adjust instruction and feedback** during the unit—not only grade a final product.
- Peer collaboration should improve drafts through **criteria-focused** response, not unstructured chat.

They are especially valuable in **writing across the curriculum**, **source-based literacy**, **language learning performance tasks**, and any course where writing is how students demonstrate reasoning.

---

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit

Avoid defaulting to writing-task materials when:

- The learning goal is **recognition or recall** better served by retrieval practice or short items.
- A “writing assignment” is really **note completion** or transcription with no communicative purpose.
- There is **no time** for practice, reflection, or revision—the task becomes performative compliance.
- Prompts lack **audience, purpose, and criteria**, so students cannot self-evaluate or peers cannot give useful response.
- Frames or templates **replace** the thinking the discipline requires (e.g., evidence evaluation in history, data interpretation in science).
- The only feedback planned is a **holistic grade** with no instructional follow-up.

**Common misuses**

- Prompt lists with no instructional cycle, models, or revision pathway.
- Peer review sheets that ask “Did you like it?” without tying comments to task criteria.
- Planning sheets filled once and never revisited although drafts changed goals or evidence.
- Identical prompts across disciplines when **evidence types and genres** differ.
- Exemplar packets shown without teaching **which features** to emulate and how to compare one’s draft.

## Evidence And Source Traditions

### Core tradition: WWC secondary writing (grades 6–12)

The **sole core instructional anchor** for this handbook is *Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively* (IES/WWC, 2016; revised 2019). Other traditions below are supporting overlays (criteria design, feedback, reflection, access, language performance)—not co-equal replacements for the WWC practice guide. It organizes practice around **three recommendations** that should shape how you design task bundles:

1. **Explicitly teach appropriate writing strategies** using a **Model–Practice–Reflect** cycle (strong evidence).
2. **Integrate writing and reading** to emphasize key writing features (moderate evidence).
3. **Use assessments of student writing** to inform instruction and feedback (minimal evidence rating for the recommendation as a whole, but positive effects in studies; still endorsed by the expert panel).

The guide treats writing strategies as **structured series of mental and physical actions** writers use across an **iterative** process: planning and goal setting, drafting, evaluating, revising, and editing. Components may occur in different orders or overlap; **audience and purpose** stay in view throughout.

**Writing outcome domains** the research synthesis tracked include: audience, genre elements, organization, sentence structure, use of evidence, word choice, writing output, writing processes, and overall writing quality. Authoring choices should make clear **which domain(s)** a task primarily targets.

### Supporting traditions (use as overlays, not replacements)

| Tradition | Contribution to writing-task authoring |
|---|---|
| **University rubric guides** (e.g., Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center) | Turn expectations into **component criteria**; support formative self-assessment before submission |
| **Feedback guidance** (e.g., EEF) | Design tasks so feedback can address **misconceptions and next steps**, not only praise or correction volume |
| **Formative assessment / evaluative judgment** (e.g., Sadler) | Students need **appreciation of quality**, ability to **compare** work to standards, and **tactics to close gaps**—task materials should enable all three |
| **Visible thinking routines** (e.g., Project Zero) | Structured reflection and discussion prompts when writing involves interpretation, perspective, or idea exploration |
| **Performance descriptors** (e.g., ACTFL Can-Do) | Language tasks aligned to **proficiency targets** and communicative functions |
| **UDL** (CAST) | Multiple means of **action and expression** (dictation, speech-to-text, graphic plans, varied response formats) without lowering the cognitive demand of the writing goal |

Do not treat any framework row as its own “resource.” Frameworks inform **how** you design prompts, scaffolds, and protocols.

### Elementary and upper-elementary writing (grades K–5; supporting)

*Teaching Elementary School Students to Be Effective Writers* (IES/WWC Practice Guide 17) is the matched evidence anchor when authoring **primary-grade writing-task materials**. It complements—not replaces—the secondary guide for grades 6–12.

The elementary guide organizes practice around four recommendations:

1. **Provide daily time for students to write** — task packets should assume recurring production, not one-off prompts without practice time.
2. **Teach the writing process for varied purposes** — planning, drafting, sharing, evaluating, revising, editing, and publishing; teach **strategies per process component** and **purposes** (inform, persuade, narrate, explain); use **gradual release** from modeled strategy use to independent application.
3. **Build fluency with foundational skills** — handwriting, spelling, sentence construction, typing, and word processing so cognitive load can shift to composing ideas; materials may include brief skill targets **inside** a communicative task, not isolated drills posing as writing assignments.
4. **Create an engaged community of writers** — sharing, response, and collaboration norms belong in the task design (who reads the draft, what kind of response is required).

**Authoring implications for K–5 task materials**

- Shorter **cycles** and clearer **purpose labels** than secondary argument units; fewer simultaneous strategy introductions.
- **Sharing and response** steps are part of the assignment, not optional extras.
- Scaffolds emphasize **sentence- and paragraph-level** clarity before multi-source synthesis.
- When integrating reading, favor **accessible texts** and features students can emulate in their own draft (structure, word choice, evidence in age-appropriate forms).
- Transfer secondary-guide moves (peer criteria review, ranked evidence tables) only when students already control foundational production skills.

### Disciplinary writing across subjects (supporting lenses)

Report guidance for secondary families expects writing-task authoring to combine cross-family evidence with **subject frameworks**. Use the lenses below for genre, evidence, and audience—not as separate resource families.

| Tradition | Contribution to writing-task authoring | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **NGSS three-dimensional learning** | Science tasks should elicit **explanations, arguments from evidence, and model-based reasoning** aligned to practices and core ideas—not generic “lab report” labels without named practices | In-lab **CER scaffolds tied to an investigation spine** → `investigation-lab-materials`; **standalone** science writing assignments (reports, explanations, arguments from data sets) stay here |
| **C3 Framework for social studies** | Inquiry arc supports **claims from evidence**, sourcing, and **communicating conclusions** (Dimension 4) and **taking informed action** when appropriate; compelling and supporting questions can frame written products | Document **reading** sets and sourcing lessons → `reading-materials`; **productive** argument, policy brief, or conclusion writing → here |
| **National Writing Project (disciplinary literacy / WAC)** | Practitioner tradition for **writing across the curriculum** and **writing in the disciplines**—genre, audience, and habits of mind differ by field; “writing to learn” vs “writing to demonstrate disciplinary competence” may need different task briefs | Open educator resources; synthesize principles, do not copy lesson banks verbatim |

**Science (NGSS lens):** Name which **practice** the writing rehearses (construct explanations, argue from evidence, obtain/evaluate/communicate information). Specify what counts as **evidence** (data, models, text) and the **audience** (teacher, peer, public). Cross-link detailed investigation design to the lab handbook when the writing product is embedded in a multi-day investigation.

**History and social studies (C3 lens):** Align prompts to **disciplinary tools** (sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, causal reasoning) and to an **inquiry question** when the unit is inquiry-driven. Written products should require **claims supported by evidence from provided sources**, not unsupported opinion. Action steps (letters, proposals) belong in the brief when Dimension 4 includes informed action.

**NWP lens:** Treat writing in math, science, history, and arts as **field-specific**—different genres, evidence norms, and stances. Task briefs should state the **disciplinary “why”** of the form (e.g., justify a method, explain a mechanism, defend an interpretation) rather than importing a single five-paragraph essay frame.

### Formal programme contexts: IB and similar (background)

Authority maps list **IB assessment types** as background for formal writing-task patterns: varied constructed-response and performance forms (including essays and structured tasks), **internal assessment** written products, and **standards-based** judgment rather than rank ordering alone.

**Authoring implications**

- Match **task type, length, and citation expectations** to programme guides for the subject and level you serve.
- Separate **classroom process** materials (drafts, peer review, reflection) from **summative submission** rules when external moderation applies.
- For assessment-cycle depth (marks vs grades, moderation, validity chain), use **`assessment-instruments`** and **`criteria-scoring-materials`**; this handbook covers **student-facing production pathways**, not examiner manuals.

Formal programme requirements change by subject, level, and session. Prefer current subject-specific **internal-assessment guides** and the open *Assessment principles and practices* PDF cited in `assessment-instruments` when verifying formal requirements.

## Key Design Decisions

### 1. Communicative specification (audience, purpose, genre)

Every substantial task should make **audience**, **purpose**, and **genre or form** explicit enough that students can choose strategies deliberately.

Planning supports often include student-facing questions such as:

- Who is the audience? What do they already know? What do they need? What will persuade or inform them?
- What is the purpose (inform, persuade, explain, reflect, analyze)? What form fits (editorial, lab report, speech, blog, essay)?
- What are the **aspects of effective writing** for this purpose?

**Discipline matters.** The same planning strategy (e.g., gathering and ranking evidence) should produce different evidence types in science (data, methods) versus social studies (primary sources, contextualization) versus ELA (literary or rhetorical analysis).

### 2. Strategy instruction, not only product description

Recommendation 1 has two parts that belong together in materials:

- **Explicit strategy teaching** for each process component (planning, drafting, evaluating, revising, editing).
- **Model–Practice–Reflect**: model thinking aloud, provide practice opportunities, then structured evaluation of **both the draft and the strategy use**.

Students should learn **multiple strategies per component** and how to **select and adapt** them—not memorize one fixed procedure for every assignment.

Typical strategy families (paraphrased categories from the guide’s examples) include:

| Process component | Examples of strategy types (illustrative, not exhaustive) |
|---|---|
| Planning / goals | K-W-L, outlines, compare/contrast organizers, genre-specific planners, individualized goal lists |
| Drafting | Thesis-driven development, mini-arguments, ranked evidence, discipline-specific drafting moves |
| Evaluating | Compare–diagnose–operate at sentence or text level, color-coding for claims/evidence/commentary, WIRMI-style clarity checks |
| Revising | Peer feedback focused on specific features, goal-based revision, response to structured partner comments |
| Editing | Conventions checklists, focused peer editing jobs, substitution and clarity routines |

Author **how to execute** a strategy in student language, when to use it, and which **genres** it fits—without copying proprietary strategy acronyms or branded program sheets verbatim.

### 3. Scaffold strength and fading

Scaffolds should be **strongest when a strategy is new**, then fade as students internalize moves. Over-scaffolding produces dependent writers who cannot transfer strategies to a new audience or discipline—the guide explicitly warns against **rote** strategy use.

Design decisions:

- Which steps are **mandatory** on the handout vs. optional once skilled?
- When does the packet shrink from full planner + reflection to a **short checklist**?
- How will you know students can execute the strategy **without** the sheet?

### 4. Reading–writing integration

When texts feed the task, materials should make **shared reading–writing knowledge** visible: text structure, signal words, main-point logic, use of evidence, voice, and conventions.

Practical patterns:

- **Exemplar study** before drafting: highlight one or two features aligned to the lesson objective (not exhaustive markup).
- **Contrast sets**: strong vs. weak student or published examples so features are distinguishable.
- **Integrated activities**: summarize then argue; read multiple sources then synthesize; story impressions before reading a narrative.
- **Cognitive-strategy sentence starters** for responding to text and for drafting (e.g., revising meanings, analyzing author craft)—used to show how readers and writers share strategic thinking.

Exemplar work connects to **success exemplar** authoring; the writing-task author specifies **which features** the exemplar lesson targets and what students must **emulate** in their own draft.

### 5. Peer review and collaboration design

Peer materials are writing-task artifacts when they:

- Focus on **criteria-linked** features (organization, evidence, clarity of claim, audience fit).
- Use **structured prompts** (what works, what is unclear, one improvement suggestion tied to the task).
- Require a **accountable revision step** (student decides what to change and documents why).

Poor peer forms treat response as opinion only. Strong forms build on **training**, **models of useful comments**, and **limited focus** (one or two dimensions per round).

### 6. Revision and reflection as part of the task, not an add-on

The guide stresses that **practice without reflection** limits transfer. Revision handouts should connect changes to **goals, criteria, or strategy effectiveness**.

Useful revision artifact elements:

- Before/after comparison tied to a **single learning target**
- Change log explaining **what** changed and **how** it improved the draft
- Self-assessment against the same criteria peers or teachers will use
- Optional double-draft requirement with **metacognitive** explanation

### 7. Formative assessment embedded in the task system

Treat **on-demand prompts**, checkpoint drafts, and short constructed pieces as part of the task family when they feed instruction.

Design choices:

- **Assess before teaching** a new strategy to see prerequisites and gaps.
- **Analyze writing** to group students and target feedback (prioritize one dimension per round if time is limited).
- **Monitor progress** at intervals matched to goal breadth—frequent checks for narrow skills, less frequent for broad constructs like overall persuasiveness.
- Pair data with **tracking tools** students can understand (simple progress charts by skill dimension).

Do not confuse a formative writing probe with a **summative exam**; the former exists to change teaching, not only to rank students.

### 8. Accessibility and expression options

Apply UDL as a design lens: offer **multiple ways to plan** (oral rehearsal, graphic map, digital outliner), **multiple ways to draft** (keyboard, dictation, template with expandable sections), and **clear task language** (chunked directions, glossary for discipline terms). Accessibility adjustments should preserve the **construct** being measured (e.g., argument quality), not substitute a different skill.

For **world-language** writing tasks, align prompts and success descriptors to **performance targets** (functions, contexts, text types) rather than generic essay rubrics alone.

### 9. Grade band and developmental fit

Before selecting scaffolds, confirm the **evidence anchor** for the grade span:

| Grade span | Primary evidence anchor | Typical task-material adjustments |
|---|---|---|
| **K–5** | WWC elementary writing (PG 17) | Daily writing time; gradual release; foundational skills inside communicative tasks; sharing/response built in; shorter products |
| **6–12** | WWC secondary writing (PG 22) | Full model–practice–reflect cycles; source-based and disciplinary tasks when readiness allows |
| **Upper elementary (4–6)** | Both guides may inform design | Use elementary process and fluency expectations; introduce secondary-style evidence and audience work only with supports |

When a task spans **reading and writing**, coordinate with `reading-materials` for text sets and with disciplinary lenses above for **evidence and genre** norms.

## Authoring Workflow

Use this sequence when building or overhauling a writing-task bundle.

### Step 1: Anchor the learning target

State what evidence the task will elicit—linked to standards or unit outcomes—and which **writing domain(s)** matter most (e.g., use of evidence vs. sentence variety). One primary target per checkpoint keeps feedback actionable.

Confirm **grade-band fit**: for K–5, prioritize elementary-guide recommendations (daily writing, process strategies, foundational fluency, writing community); for 6–12, use the secondary guide’s strategy and integration patterns. Name any **disciplinary lens** (NGSS practice, C3 inquiry dimension, NWP-style genre) when the task is not generic ELA.

### Step 2: Fix audience, purpose, genre, and discipline conventions

Draft the **assignment brief** first. Include:

- Situation and role (if authentic)
- Audience and what they care about
- Purpose and required form
- Source requirements and citation expectations
- Length, format, and due dates for **drafts** as well as final copy

### Step 3: Select strategies to teach

Choose **one or two new or priority strategies** for the unit—not every strategy in the guide at once. For each, plan:

- Teacher model (what thinking will be made visible)
- Guided practice task (often a section or short piece, not always the full essay)
- Reflection questions on strategy effectiveness

### Step 4: Build the production pathway

Map artifacts to process phases. A typical secondary argumentative unit might include:

1. Audience/purpose probe or short pre-assessment prompt  
2. Planning sheet + individualized goals  
3. Exemplar feature lesson (reading integrated)  
4. Drafting scaffold (optional)  
5. Structured peer review  
6. Revision plan + change log  
7. Editing checklist (conventions focus)  
8. Final self-assessment against criteria  

Not every unit needs every artifact; **coherence** beats completeness.

### Step 5: Attach criteria without duplicating a full rubric handbook

Reference or embed **student-facing success criteria** (bullets drawn from an analytic rubric). Full rubric authoring belongs in criteria-scoring materials; here, include only what students need **while writing**.

### Step 6: Plan formative use

Specify what the teacher will look for in drafts, how often, and what **instructional adjustment** follows (reteach modeling, small group, alternate mentor text, etc.). If using peer review, schedule **training** and a pilot round on a low-stakes piece.

### Step 7: Pilot for cognitive load and time

Read the packet as a student: Is the **ratio** of scaffolding to writing time sensible? Can a struggling writer find the **next action** at each stage? Can an advanced writer **opt out** of redundant steps?

### Step 8: Review against quality checks (below)

Revise for clarity, fairness, and alignment. Remove steps that do not connect to the stated learning target.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

### Assignment brief

- [ ] Audience, purpose, and genre/form are explicit and consistent with discipline norms  
- [ ] Task requires **composition**, not only copying or filling blanks  
- [ ] Source use (if any) is feasible and ethically/scaffolded as needed  
- [ ] Criteria or success features are visible **before** drafting  
- [ ] Draft milestones are specified when process is graded or taught  

### Strategy and model materials

- [ ] Strategies are **taught**, not merely named on the prompt  
- [ ] Model–practice–reflect cycle is complete for at least one priority strategy  
- [ ] Students can explain **why** a strategy fits audience/purpose  
- [ ] Scaffolds can fade across the year or unit  

### Reading integration

- [ ] Linked readings support the **writing features** being taught  
- [ ] Exemplar lessons focus on **observable features**, not passive reading  
- [ ] Activities transfer a reading insight into a **writing move**  

### Peer and revision materials

- [ ] Peer prompts are **criteria-based** and trained  
- [ ] Revision requires **decisions** tied to feedback or self-evaluation  
- [ ] Reflection addresses **strategy use**, not only surface edits  

### Formative assessment

- [ ] Checkpoints align to the stated learning target  
- [ ] Teacher notes what **instruction changes** follow from student work  
- [ ] Monitoring frequency matches goal scope  

### Fairness and access

- [ ] Directions are clear for diverse readers (structure, definitions, examples)  
- [ ] Alternative expression modes do not change the core construct unfairly  
- [ ] Task does not assume out-of-school resources unless provided  
- [ ] Language demands match the **identified learner group** (including multilingual learners)  

### Grade band and discipline

- [ ] Task length, scaffolds, and strategy load match **developmental readiness** (K–5 vs 6–12 anchors)  
- [ ] Elementary tasks include **time to write** and **sharing/response**, not only a final product prompt  
- [ ] Disciplinary tasks name **genre, evidence type, and audience** for the subject—not a generic essay pasted across courses  
- [ ] Science/history/social studies tasks align to named **disciplinary practices** or inquiry dimensions where applicable  

### Anti-pattern scan

- [ ] No generic prompt list without process support  
- [ ] No peer review without focus or accountability  
- [ ] No one-size-fits-all evidence expectations across disciplines  
- [ ] No frames that prevent the targeted thinking

## How Frameworks And Models Influence (Without Becoming) The Resource

| Lens | Influence on writing-task materials |
|---|---|
| **Explicit instruction** | Name steps, model thinking, provide guided practice before independent application |
| **Gradual release** | Match “I do / we do / you do” to model–practice–reflect artifacts |
| **Formative assessment** | Embed checkpoints and interpretation notes for teachers |
| **Metacognition** | Reflection on strategy, goals, and confidence calibrated to criteria |
| **UDL** | Options for planning, drafting, and expressing without changing the learning goal |
| **Collaborative learning** | Structured peer protocols with roles and product accountability |
| **Genre pedagogy** | Genre-specific planners and feature lists tied to purpose |

Bloom levels, DOK, or UbD stages may help **you** plan, but students should receive **tasks and tools**, not framework posters substituted for assignment design.

## Relationships To Other Resource Families (Summary)

- **Reading materials** supply texts; **writing-task materials** specify the **productive response** and how reading informs it.  
- **Success exemplar materials** make quality visible; writing tasks say **what to produce** and **how to practice** toward that quality.  
- **Criteria and scoring materials** judge products; writing tasks may include shortened student criteria but not full scorer training.  
- **Feedback and revision materials** often follow drafts; overlap is intentional—peer sheets here, teacher feedback systems there.  
- **Assessment instruments** evaluate attainment at scale; writing tasks may include **short formative probes** tied to instruction.  
- **Investigation and lab materials** own investigation-spine CER and data-collection sheets; **writing-task materials** own standalone disciplinary writing products and cross-curricular production assignments.  

When authoring a unit package, split artifacts by family even if teachers distribute them together.

## Annotated Mini-Packet Outline (Non-Normative Example)

The sketch below shows how workflow steps and WWC-aligned artifacts fit one unit. Content is **fictive** for layout and sequencing only—adapt genre, sources, and criteria to your context. Full rubrics belong in **criteria-scoring materials**; this outline shows shortened student-facing bullets where the writing packet needs them.

**Unit:** Grade 9 social studies — source-based argumentative letter on a current local policy (e.g., transit funding). **Primary domains:** use of evidence, audience. **Priority strategy:** gather and **rank supporting evidence**, taught with **model–practice–reflect** (WWC Rec 1a/1b).

| # | Artifact | What it contains (sketch) | Handbook anchor |
|---|----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Assignment brief** (≈1 page) | Role: concerned resident. Audience: city council member who knows headlines but not your sources. Purpose: recommend one policy change with reasons. Genre: persuasive letter (≈600–800 words). Two provided articles + one student-found source; cite in-text. Draft 1 due Day 4, peer review Day 6, final Day 10. | § Key Design Decisions §1; workflow Step 2 |
| 2 | **Planning sheet** | Audience/purpose questions; claim box; evidence table (source, quote/data, why it supports claim); **rank column** (strongest → weakest); individualized goal (“I will add context for my best piece of evidence”). | §2 strategy instruction; WWC “rank the evidence” pattern |
| 3 | **Exemplar feature lesson** (teacher-led, not a student handout) | One council letter excerpt: highlight **claim in opening** + **one cited evidence + explanation**; students note what to emulate in their draft. | §4 reading–writing integration; workflow Step 4 item 3 |
| 4 | **Drafting scaffold** (optional fade) | Section headers: context → claim → reasons with evidence → counterargument → conclusion; sentence stems only for **evidence + commentary** if class is new to the genre. | §3 scaffold fading |
| 5 | **Structured peer review** | Partner reads Draft 1. Prompts: (a) Underline the clearest claim. (b) Mark one evidence piece that needs stronger explanation. (c) One revision suggestion tied to **audience** (“What would the council member still not understand?”). Author writes a **revision plan** (three bullets). | §5 peer review; quality checks |
| 6 | **Revision log** | Before/after excerpt for the evidence paragraph the peer flagged; explain what changed and how it better fits the council audience. | §6 revision/reflection |
| 7 | **Editing checklist** | Conventions only: citations, paragraphing, formal register for a letter—not re-teaching argument structure. | Strategy table (editing component) |
| 8 | **Self-assessment** | Three student-facing bullets drawn from the analytic rubric (e.g., claim stated, evidence explained, tone fits audience); met / partial / not yet. | workflow Step 5; criteria-scoring boundary |

**Formative note (teacher, not student-facing):** Short on-demand paragraph before Day 1 (same policy, no sources) to see whether students can state a claim; after Draft 1, prioritize feedback on **use of evidence** one round only (WWC Rec 3—narrow focus when time is limited).

## Quick Reference: WWC Recommendations → Material Types

| Recommendation | Design implication for artifacts |
|---|---|
| **1a — Explicit strategies** | Handouts and mini-lessons per process component; strategy-selection questions; discipline-specific adaptation tasks |
| **1b — Model–practice–reflect** | Modeling scripts for teachers; practice sections; reflection logs after practice |
| **2 — Integrate reading and writing** | Exemplar lessons; sentence starters; source-based prompts; synthesis and comparison tasks |
| **3 — Assess to inform instruction** | Pre-task probes; draft checkpoints; peer/self review tied to one dimension; progress trackers |

Evidence ratings from the guide: Recommendation **1 — strong**; Recommendation **2 — moderate**; Recommendation **3 — minimal** (still panel-endorsed; implement with attention to formative purpose and local context).

- National Council for the Social Studies. *C3 Framework for Social Studies State Standards*. https://www.socialstudies.org/standards/c3 — inquiry arc, evidence use, communicating conclusions, and informed action in social studies writing.
- National Writing Project. https://www.nwp.org/ — writing across the curriculum and writing in the disciplines as practitioner traditions for genre, audience, and field-specific habits.
- Carnegie Mellon University Eberly Center. *Grading and Performance Rubrics*. https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/rubrics.html — component criteria and formative use of rubrics while students draft.
- Education Endowment Foundation. *Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning*. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/feedback — feedback that targets tasks, misconceptions, and next steps on drafts.
- Harvard Project Zero. *Thinking Routines*. https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines — structured reflection and discussion prompts alongside interpretive or exploratory writing.
- ACTFL. *NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements*. https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements — proficiency-aligned functions, contexts, and text types for world-language writing tasks.
- CAST. *Universal Design for Learning Guidelines*. https://udlguidelines.cast.org/ — multiple means of planning, drafting, and expressing without changing the writing goal.
- D. Royce Sadler. *Formative assessment and the design of instructional systems*. *Instructional Science*, 1989. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00117714 — appreciation of quality, comparison to standards, and tactics to close gaps.
- International Baccalaureate Organization. *Assessment principles and practices—Quality assessments in a digital age* (2018). https://ibo.org/contentassets/1cdf850e366447e99b5a862aab622883/assessment-principles-and-practices-2018-en.pdf — formal programme assessment context for extended written products.
- International Baccalaureate Organization. *Diploma Programme — Assessment and exams*. https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/assessment-and-exams/ — overview of internal and external written assessment types in IB programmes.
