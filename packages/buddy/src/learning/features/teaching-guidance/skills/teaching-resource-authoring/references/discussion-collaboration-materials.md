# Discussion And Collaboration Materials

## What This Resource Family Is

Discussion and collaboration materials are **concrete artifacts** that structure how learners talk, listen, reason together, and share academic work in pairs, small groups, seminars, or whole-class formats. They make discourse and group work **productive for learning**, not merely social.

Typical artifacts include:

- **Seminar or discussion protocols** — sequenced steps, time cues, and roles for a structured conversation about texts, problems, sources, or ideas.
- **Talk stems and sentence frames** — scaffolds that help learners build on others’ ideas, cite evidence, disagree respectfully, ask clarifying questions, and explain reasoning.
- **Partner or small-group dialogue sheets** — prompts and recording spaces for paired or trio work before whole-class synthesis.
- **Group role cards or responsibility matrices** — who does what during a collaborative task (e.g., facilitator, evidence tracker, skeptic, reporter), tied to **academic** outcomes rather than logistics alone.
- **Collaborative investigation or jigsaw guides** — how subgroups gather, compare, and integrate information toward a shared product or conclusion.
- **Discussion participation or collaboration rubrics** — criteria for quality of contribution, evidence use, listening, and group process (distinct from full performance-task rubrics unless the discussion *is* the assessed performance).
- **Structured controversy or debate briefs** — positions, evidence requirements, rebuttal rules, and reflection—not open-ended “argue your opinion” prompts.

The **teaching job** these materials perform is to turn talk and collaboration into **visible, revisable thinking** and **shared academic work**: learners articulate reasoning, test ideas against evidence, distribute cognitive load appropriately, and leave a trace teachers can monitor and learners can revisit.

Collaborative learning, visible thinking, metacognitive talk, perspective-taking, and civic or disciplinary reasoning are **design traditions** that inform this family. They are not the artifacts themselves. A thinking routine from a university research center is a **lens** until you turn it into a printable protocol, slide, or facilitation sheet your class can use.

---

## Artifact Boundaries

### Belongs in this family

Materials whose primary learner action is **structured discourse or coordinated group cognition** toward a learning goal: interpret a text, evaluate sources, solve a problem, plan an investigation, critique a draft, or build a shared claim.

### Keep separate (nearby families)

| Nearby family | Why it is separate | Typical contrast |
|---|---|---|
| **Reading materials** | Primary job is access to and comprehension of text | A passage packet may include margin prompts, but a **seminar protocol** belongs here when the artifact is mainly about *how* to discuss |
| **Writing-task materials** | Primary job is producing written work | Peer-review sheets overlap; place them here when the sheet structures **dialogue about writing**, in writing-task materials when the sheet is mainly drafting/revision instructions |
| **Question and item sets** | Primary job is eliciting individual responses | Discussion can follow items, but the item set itself is not a collaboration material |
| **Criteria and scoring materials** | Primary job is judgment against standards | A discussion rubric lives here only when authored as part of discussion design; a generic course rubric bank may live in criteria materials |
| **Project and performance materials** | Primary job is sustained product or performance | Collaboration norms inside a multi-week project brief often belong with the project packet unless you are authoring a **standalone** discussion protocol reusable across projects |
| **Practice materials and worksheets** | Primary job is individual rehearsal | Think-pair-share can sit on a worksheet, but if the sheet is mainly drill, it is practice—not discussion |
| **Assessment instruments** | Primary job is measurement and scoring design | Use assessment references when talk is **scored for accountability** with validity and fairness requirements beyond classroom facilitation |
| **Feedback and revision materials** | Primary job is response to work | Conference prompts overlap; choose by whether the artifact structures **conversation about improvement** vs. written feedback templates |

### Not this family

- Classroom management routines (entry, transitions, attention signals) without academic discourse structure.
- Generic icebreakers or team-building games without tied thinking moves or evidence norms.
- Framework posters (e.g., visible thinking categories) with no task-specific prompts or steps for *this* lesson.
- Unstructured “discuss in groups” lines on a slide or worksheet.

## When This Family Is Useful

Use discussion and collaboration materials when learning goals require learners to **externalize reasoning**, **pressure-test ideas**, **co-construct understanding**, or **distribute complex tasks** among peers.

Strong fits include:

- **Interpretation and argument from evidence** — literary analysis, historical sourcing, scientific claims, civic evaluation of online information.
- **Metacognitive and strategic talk** — planning how to approach a task, monitoring whether a strategy works, evaluating what to try next (especially when materials prompt *why* a method was chosen, not only *what* the answer is).
- **Perspective-taking and fair-minded inquiry** — comparing viewpoints, exploring dilemmas, examining whose voice is centered in a source.
- **Collaborative problem-solving** — mathematics, design, engineering, or open problems where explaining to a partner reveals gaps.
- **Peer review and critique** — structured protocols before revising writing or projects.
- **Language learning** — interpersonal speaking goals where interaction patterns must be explicit (see language-specific traditions below).

Evidence on **collaborative learning** consistently shows positive average impact when collaboration is **structured**—with well-designed tasks, appropriate group size, and opportunities for all learners to talk—not when students are simply seated together. Evidence on **metacognition** similarly links structured reasoning, argumentation, and debate to stronger self-regulation when talk is purposeful and tied to challenging curriculum content.

---

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit

Avoid defaulting to group discussion materials when:

- **The learning goal is individual fluency or recall** and talk would fragment necessary repetition (use practice materials instead).
- **Students lack sufficient prior knowledge** to engage in exploratory dialogue productively; unstructured talk can reinforce misconceptions.
- **Time is too short** for setup, individual think time, and synthesis—surface “turn and talk” without structure often yields shallow exchange.
- **Assessment demands independent demonstration** and the discussion format would prevent valid inference about individual mastery (pair work as the only evidence-gathering mode).
- **Social dynamics are unsafe or inequitable** and the material does not include supports for participation, role rotation, or alternative contribution pathways—adding a role card alone will not fix exclusion.
- **The task is purely procedural** with one correct method and no benefit from peer explanation.

Common misuses:

- Treating **collaboration** as synonymous with **cooperation on logistics** (one student writes while others watch).
- Using **competition between groups** as the main driver without guarding against focus on winning over learning.
- Assigning **debate positions** without evidence requirements, source access, or reflection on quality of reasoning.
- Publishing **discussion question lists** with no specified thinking move, talk norm, or product from the conversation.
- **Role cards** that name jobs (“timekeeper”) but do not connect roles to disciplinary work (evidence, counterargument, synthesis).

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

No single standards body owns this family. Authoring draws on **visible-thinking toolkits**, **evidence reviews of collaborative learning**, **metacognition guidance**, **disciplinary inquiry curricula**, **participation rubrics**, and **accessibility frameworks**. The sections below describe what each tradition contributes to **design decisions**, not what to copy verbatim.

### Project Zero thinking routines (core)

Harvard Project Zero describes a **thinking routine** as a short sequence of questions or steps used to scaffold and support student thinking—making thinking visible to teachers and helping students notice named “thinking moves” they can reuse.

For authoring, this tradition supplies:

- **Routine as repeatable structure**, not a one-off question.
- **Selection by thinking purpose** — routines are browsable by categories such as introducing ideas, digging deeper, perspective-taking, synthesizing, and working with objects or art (filters on the public toolbox reflect this organization).
- **Disposition language** — communicating, comparing, questioning, reasoning, exploring viewpoints, and similar goals help authors align prompts with intended cognition.
- **Reuse for classroom culture** — materials should be lean enough to use often so learners internalize the pattern and treat routines as **habits of mind**, not one-off worksheets.
- **Visible thinking for teachers** — routines help teachers see how learners reason, not only whether they got a right answer.

**Authoring implication:** Your protocol should state the **thinking move** (e.g., surface initial ideas, challenge assumptions, connect across sources) and a **short, repeatable sequence** learners recognize across units—not a unique snowflake worksheet every lesson.

**Authoring caution:** Do not reproduce full routine text, posters, or branded layouts; paraphrase thinking moves into original facilitation language.

### Education Endowment Foundation — collaborative learning (core)

The EEF toolkit defines collaborative learning as pupils working together on activities or tasks in groups small enough that everyone participates—**distinct from unstructured group work**. Approaches may involve separate subtasks contributing to a common outcome, or shared work on one task.

Key evidence-backed design pressures:

| Theme | Practical authoring takeaway |
|---|---|
| Structure | Tasks must be **designed** so collaboration is effective; sitting pupils together is insufficient |
| Group size | **Three to five** learners per group is most promising; pairs and larger groups show smaller average gains |
| Joint outcomes | Shared group goals tend to show **higher impact** than parallel individual subtasks only |
| Talk | Ensure **all pupils articulate thinking**; silent passengers undermine benefits |
| Support | Pupils need **practice and professional learning** to collaborate well—it is not automatic |
| Authoring for teachers | When introducing new collaboration structures to colleagues, plan brief **professional learning or coaching** on task design and monitoring—effective collaboration requires teacher skill, not only new handouts |
| Competition | Between-group competition can help but may shift focus to winning |
| Equity | Lower-attaining pupils need **designed inclusion**; otherwise gaps widen |
| Group composition | **Make-up of pairings and groups** should be deliberate when equity is at stake—not assumed to work if students are merely seated together |
| Teacher modeling | Teachers should **model high-quality collaborative talk** when introducing new structures |
| Monitoring | Teachers should **observe and support** groups that struggle or dominate; watch for passengers and blocked access to materials |
| Digital delivery | Collaborative learning through **digital technology** shows **smaller average impact** (+3 months vs. +5 months overall)—online materials need extra structure and talk accountability |

**Authoring implication:** Every collaboration sheet should make **participation**, **shared outcome**, and **individual accountability** explicit (who talks, what gets produced, how free-riders are prevented).

### EEF metacognition and self-regulated learning (supporting)

Metacognition guidance treats **classroom talk** as a vehicle for planning, monitoring, and evaluating learning when talk is **purposeful**, builds on subject knowledge, and goes beyond closed initiate–respond–evaluate patterns.

Contributions for discussion materials:

- **Question types** that activate prior knowledge, probe decisions (“what makes you think that?”), and invite revision (“could it be something else?”).
- **Peer prompts** used in pairs before whole-class synthesis.
- **Sentence stems** for evaluating answers with evidence.
- **Planning scaffolds** (what I know / what I need to find out / where I will look) that precede or accompany discussion.
- **Reasoning, argumentation, and debate** as mechanisms that promote reflection—when wedded to challenge and content, not dialogue for its own sake.
- **Structured reflection** after talk that feeds the next task (what worked, what to change).

Toolkit summaries also note metacognitive strategies can be effective in **carefully structured** collaborative groups.

**Authoring implication:** Label whether prompts target **planning**, **monitoring**, or **evaluating**, and include a short reflection beat after discourse. When talk happens in groups, note that metacognitive strategies in collaborative settings need **careful structure** to be effective—not unstructured turn-and-talk.

### Accountable Talk — University of Pittsburgh / UNESCO IBE (supporting)

Researchers at the University of Pittsburgh Learning Research and Development Center developed **Accountable Talk** as a framework for academically productive classroom discourse. A UNESCO International Bureau of Education practitioner synthesis describes discussion as **teacher-led but student-owned**: the teacher elicits a range of ideas (often incomplete at first), and students take up one another's claims—building on, challenging, clarifying, or offering alternatives—toward a more developed, evidence-backed conclusion.

Productive talk is organized around **three accountabilities**:

| Accountability | What materials should require |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | Claims grounded in texts, data, or other public information—not unsupported opinion |
| **Reasoning** | Logical links between ideas; justified conclusions; partial concessions where appropriate |
| **Learning community** | Active listening; contributions that respond to peers; respect for others' ideas |

Facilitator **talk moves** from related classroom-discourse research give practical authoring patterns: revoicing to confirm understanding, pressing for evidence or reasoning, asking a student to restate a peer's idea, and strategic wait time so more learners can enter the conversation.

**Authoring implication:** Label which accountability each stem or protocol step serves. Include facilitator notes for eliciting incomplete ideas and inviting peers to take them up—not only pre-written discussion questions.

**Authoring caution:** Accountable Talk is associated with Pitt/IFL research traditions. Do not reproduce branded posters, move charts, or booklet text; paraphrase norms into original protocol language.

### What Works Clearinghouse — adolescent literacy (supporting)

The WWC practice guide on adolescent literacy includes a recommendation to provide **opportunities for extended discussion of text meaning and interpretation**—alongside explicit vocabulary and comprehension strategy instruction.

**Authoring implication:** Text-based seminar materials should connect discussion to **meaning and interpretation**, not only plot recall or opinion sharing, and should assume vocabulary and comprehension supports exist elsewhere in the lesson sequence.

### Civic Online Reasoning — Digital Inquiry Group (supporting)

COR materials teach evaluation of online information using strategies derived from research on professional fact-checkers. Public framing emphasizes three evaluative questions: **who is behind the information**, **what the evidence is**, and **what other sources say**—plus practices such as lateral reading and restraint before clicking.

**Authoring implication:** Collaborative source-evaluation sheets should require **group-visible evidence trails** (what was checked, what was found elsewhere) and shared decision rules, not vague “is this reliable?” prompts.

Use COR materials as design models for source-evaluation tasks; author original classroom logs and prompts rather than reproducing downloaded lesson wording.

### History Lessons / Reading Like a Historian — Digital Inquiry Group (supporting)

Document-based history instruction emphasizes **sourcing, contextualization, corroboration**, and claims grounded in evidence—often through structured small-group and whole-class discussion of primary sources.

**Authoring implication:** Seminar protocols for history should specify **which sourcing move** learners practice in each segment and what counts as sufficient corroboration for the task level.

Use Reading Like a Historian materials as design models for document-based discussion; author original prompts and facilitation language for the local source set.

### Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center — rubrics (supporting)

CMU’s rubric guidance emphasizes explicit performance expectations, component criteria, and descriptions at varying levels of mastery. Example categories include **discussion class** and **advanced seminar** participation rubrics.

**Authoring implication:** Discussion rubrics should describe **observable discourse behaviors** (uses evidence, builds on peers, asks clarifying questions) rather than vague “participates well.” Rubrics can serve formative coaching or summative grading if descriptors are stable enough for consistent application.

Use CMU examples to understand observable discussion criteria; author fresh descriptors matched to the local protocol.

### CAST Universal Design for Learning (supporting lens)

UDL treats engagement, representation, and action/expression as design levers. For collaboration, authors should welcome **variable interests and identities**, offer **multiple pathways to participate** (oral, written chat, shared doc, visual board), and reduce unnecessary barriers to discourse.

**Authoring implication:** Include options for how contributions are made (speak, post, annotate) and how groups are formed (interest, random, strategic) when equity requires it.

### NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements (domain-specific core for language)

For world-language classrooms, interpersonal communication performance descriptors define what learners should be able to do in **spontaneous or structured conversation** at a proficiency level.

**Authoring implication:** Language discussion sheets should tie stems and tasks to **Can-Do targets** (e.g., ask clarifying questions, narrate, compare preferences) rather than generic academic talk frames alone.

Level-specific **performance indicators** live in downloadable PDFs linked from the ACTFL Can-Do page; use the PDF for the target proficiency level when drafting stems, not only the overview landing page.

### NGSS three-dimensional learning (background for science)

Science standards integrate disciplinary core ideas, science and engineering practices, and crosscutting concepts. Argument from evidence and critique of claims are practice-driven discussion goals in investigations.

**Authoring implication:** Science discussion guides should name **which practice** is being rehearsed (e.g., argue from evidence, evaluate competing models) and what evidence counts in the investigation context.

### PBLWorks project-based teaching practices (background)

Gold Standard project teaching emphasizes **collaboration norms, culture, and coaching** across sustained projects.

**Authoring implication:** Long-form collaboration guides for projects should include **norms, checkpoints, and critique cycles**—not only end-of-project presentation roles.

### Dialogic teaching — University of Cambridge (supporting lens)

The Cambridge CamTalk program summarizes Robin Alexander’s dialogic teaching as harnessing talk to **stimulate and extend pupils’ thinking** and advance learning—across subjects, with attention to both teacher and learner roles in purposeful classroom dialogue.

For authoring:

- **Exploratory vs. presentational talk** — distinguish when learners are testing ideas from when they report settled conclusions.
- **Facilitator questioning** — include sample probes that invite reasoning and build-on moves, not only recall checks.
- **Goals and closure** — exploratory quality still needs a clear discourse purpose and a synthesis or reflection beat; dialogic talk is not unstructured chat.

**Authoring implication:** Pair learner-facing protocols with facilitator questions that open thinking, and end with a labeled synthesis segment.

**Authoring caution:** Do not reproduce lesson transcripts, video frameworks, or book excerpts; paraphrase facilitation moves into original language.

### Socratic seminar — structured text dialogue (supporting)

Socratic seminars are a widespread practitioner tradition for **student-facilitated dialogue about a shared text** toward deeper meaning—not toward winning a debate or asserting unrelated opinions. Teaching-center guidance from organizations such as Facing History & Ourselves emphasizes:

- **Text selection** — rich enough for interpretation; short enough to annotate closely before discussion begins.
- **Preparation** — individual annotation and optional student-generated **open-ended** questions before the seminar.
- **Circle architecture** — participants face one another; large classes use **inner/outer circle (fishbowl)** with role rotation so everyone practices both discussion and structured observation.
- **Facilitator restraint** — the teacher observes and intervenes when norms break down or talk stalls; students address **each other**, not the teacher as default audience.
- **Norms beyond generic respect** — cite text evidence, monitor **airtime**, paraphrase for clarity, disagree with ideas rather than people.
- **Purpose clarity** — the goal is shared understanding of the text, not performance debate or opinion sharing.
- **Post-seminar reflection** — evaluate discourse quality, evidence use, and how understanding changed.

**Authoring implication:** Seminar protocols should specify preparation time, opening question type (interpretive, not yes/no), fishbowl observer roles (content notes vs. process notes), and a reflection segment. Facilitator notes should distinguish **when to pause** vs. when to let productive silence continue.

**Authoring caution:** Do not reproduce branded seminar handouts or sentence-stem posters; author original norms and prompts.

### Complex Instruction — Stanford (supporting lens for equity)

Elizabeth Cohen and colleagues developed **Complex Instruction** to make cooperative groupwork equitable in heterogeneous classrooms. Research shows unstructured groups can **widen gaps**: high-status students talk more and learn more; low-status students may be ignored despite sitting in the group.

Key concepts for authoring:

- **Status problems** — unequal participation driven by perceived academic or social rank, not only by missing role cards.
- **Multiple-abilities treatment** — at task launch, name several **intellectual abilities** the activity requires (e.g., interpret visuals, explain in writing, synthesize orally) and state explicitly that no one has all of them—so varied strengths matter.
- **Assigning competence** — the teacher's **public, specific** recognition of a low-status student's intellectual contribution to shift group expectations (must be genuine and tied to the task—not flattery).
- **Groupworthy tasks** — open-ended, interdependent activities with a joint product **and** individual accountability (e.g., individual report or reflection).

**Authoring implication:** Role cards and jigsaw sheets should tie roles to intellectual work, include a **launch script** naming multiple valued abilities, and add facilitator notes for **status interventions** when one member is sidelined—not only generic "everyone participates" lines.

**Authoring caution:** Do not reproduce Complex Instruction activity cards or unit materials; synthesize design principles only.

## Key Design Decisions

Authors should resolve these explicitly before drafting artifacts.

### 1. Discourse purpose

Choose the primary thinking job:

- Elicit and organize initial ideas
- Deepen interpretation or problem-solving
- Compare perspectives or sources
- Evaluate credibility or quality of reasoning
- Plan or monitor strategy use
- Synthesize across inputs into a shared claim
- Critique and improve peer work

Mixed purposes are possible but need **labeled segments** so learners know which mode they are in.

### 2. Group architecture

Decide:

- **Format** — pair, triad, group of 3–5, fishbowl, inner/outer circle, jigsaw expert groups.
- **Outcome type** — joint group product vs. individual products later aggregated (evidence favors joint outcomes when feasible).
- **Duration** — think time before talk, timed rounds, whole-class debrief.
- **Composition** — heterogeneous by skill, homogeneous for targeted practice, stable vs. rotating groups.

Document **why** the architecture fits the goal (e.g., pairs for high talk ratio before groups of four for synthesis).

### 3. Participation and equity

Specify:

- **Minimum talk expectations** (each member responds before group decides).
- **Role rotation** across lessons.
- **Alternative contribution modes** for learners who need them.
- **Teacher monitoring cues** (which groups to visit when).
- **Supports for lower prior attainment** — pre-teach vocabulary, provide text annotations, or give a pre-discussion writing minute so all enter with something to say.
- **Group composition** — deliberate pairing/grouping when equity is at stake; avoid isolating lower-attaining or language-learning students as passive members.
- **Status-aware facilitation** — when one member is repeatedly ignored, use **multiple-abilities framing** at launch or **public, specific competence recognition** during the task (Complex Instruction status treatments).

### 4. Evidence and accountability norms

For academic discussion, define:

- What **counts as evidence** in this discipline.
- How learners **cite** sources or observations (oral, on sheet, on slide).
- Whether **consensus** is required or productive disagreement is the product.
- What **artifact** proves the group worked (notes, chart, one-sentence claim, photo of board).

### 5. Talk scaffolding level

Calibrate scaffolding to learners:

- **Heavy** — sentence stems, step-by-step protocol, modeled example dialogue.
- **Medium** — question sequence without full stems.
- **Light** — norms plus goal only (for classes that have internalized routines).

Plan **fading**: later materials in a unit reduce scaffolds as dispositions strengthen.

### 6. Teacher moves vs. learner-only materials

Separate:

- **Facilitator notes** (when to pause, sample follow-up questions, common misconceptions).
- **Learner-facing sheet** (stems, steps, recording spaces).

Facilitator notes should not clutter the student copy unless co-teaching requires it.

### 7. Metacognitive phase

Align prompts to planning, monitoring, or evaluating:

| Phase | Example learner-facing prompt types |
|---|---|
| Planning | What do we already know? What is our goal? What strategy will we use first? |
| Monitoring | Does our evidence support the claim? What is unclear? Should we change approach? |
| Evaluating | What worked in our discussion? What would we do differently next time? |

### 8. Competition, performance, and affect

If using inter-group competition, state **learning-focused success criteria** alongside competitive framing. Include a debrief that rewards reasoning quality, not only speed or victory.

### 9. Rubric linkage (if any)

If a participation rubric accompanies the protocol:

- Align criteria with **behaviors the protocol actually trains**.
- Distinguish **process** (listening, building on ideas) from **content mastery** unless the discussion is summative evidence of content learning.
- Pilot descriptors with sample transcripts or scenarios to test scorer agreement.

### 10. Digital, hybrid, and asynchronous collaboration

EEF evidence notes that collaborative learning delivered **through digital technology** tends to show **smaller average impact** than in-person structured collaboration—extra structure and talk accountability are warranted online and in hybrid settings.

Specify:

**Synchronous (live video or hybrid room)**

- Co-created **peer engagement norms** (when to use voice vs. chat vs. shared doc; whether parallel chat is allowed during discussion).
- **Single-attention vs. multi-channel** rules—when learners must focus on one speaker vs. when side-channel note-taking or reactions are expected.
- **Airtime parity** — round-robin, timed shares, chat-before-voice, or assigned roles so remote or quiet learners are not drowned out by in-room voices.
- **Hybrid equity** — explicit norms for remote participants (being called on from chat, equal access to shared materials, visibility expectations).

**Asynchronous**

- Deadlines, revision expectations, and **edit permissions** on shared documents.
- Thread or board norms: **ground claims** (quote, link, or cite evidence); build on prior posts; minimum response expectations.
- **Prime dialogue digitally** — individual posts, annotations, or short recordings before live synthesis so talk is richer when the group meets.
- Continuation spaces (LMS threads, shared docs) for discussion after a live session ends.

**Authoring implication:** Digital sheets need the same joint outcome and articulation requirements as face-to-face materials, plus explicit **channel and airtime norms** that physical classrooms often supply implicitly.

## Authoring Workflow

### Step 1 — Anchor to learning goals

Write one sentence each for:

- **Content goal** (what learners should understand or be able to do).
- **Discourse goal** (what kind of talk proves progress toward that understanding).
- **Product** (what remains after discussion: claim, annotated chart, revised paragraph, group decision).

If the discourse goal cannot be stated, the material may be unnecessary.

### Step 2 — Choose traditions deliberately

Map goals to traditions:

- Visible-thinking structure → short repeatable routine pattern.
- Collaborative learning evidence → group size, joint outcome, articulation requirement, deliberate group composition.
- Metacognitive talk → planning/monitoring/evaluating prompts.
- Accountable Talk → three accountabilities labeled on stems and steps; facilitator talk-move notes.
- Socratic seminar → preparation, circle/fishbowl architecture, facilitator restraint, post-seminar reflection.
- Complex Instruction → multiple-abilities launch, status interventions, groupworthy joint + individual accountability.
- Disciplinary inquiry → sourcing/evidence rules (history, science, civic online reasoning, literacy interpretation).

Avoid stacking incompatible structures (e.g., twelve-step protocol plus five unrelated question types on one page).

### Step 3 — Draft the learner-facing protocol

Typical components:

1. **Purpose** — one line learners understand.
2. **Preparation** — individual reading/thinking time.
3. **Norms** — listening, evidence, equitable airtime (brief).
4. **Steps** — numbered, time-estimated if helpful.
5. **Stems** — only where needed; grouped by function (clarify, build on, challenge with evidence).
6. **Recording** — where ideas and evidence are captured.
7. **Synthesis** — whole-class or group output.
8. **Reflection** — short metacognitive close.

Keep language **grade-appropriate** and **discipline-appropriate**.

### Step 4 — Draft facilitator notes

Include:

- Expected misconceptions.
- When to intervene vs. let struggle continue.
- Sample follow-up questions if discussion stalls.
- Differentiation moves (pre-teach, reduce text load, pair strategically).

### Step 5 — Add collaboration mechanics

Explicitly document:

- Group size and formation rule.
- Roles (if used) tied to academic functions.
- Joint vs. individual accountability.
- How the teacher will know all members participated.

### Step 6 — Pair criteria if graded

If used for grades, draft or link rubric criteria **after** the protocol is stable. Test whether each criterion is observable during a live discussion or from a required group log.

### Step 7 — Pilot and revise

Pilot with one class or colleague group. Observe:

- Who talks, who is silent.
- Whether evidence norms are used or ignored.
- Whether time allocations match actual pace.
- Whether the written artifact matches the intended thinking move.

Revise stems, steps, or group size before wider distribution.

### Step 8 — Originality and fit review

Remove context-free prompt lists, generic chat scripts, and classroom-management material that is not tied to the academic discourse purpose. Ensure no copied routine text, branded posters, or lifted rubric tables from restricted sources. Paraphrase all protocol language.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

Use this checklist before publishing or sharing materials.

### Disciplinary and cognitive quality

- [ ] The primary **thinking move** is named and matches the learning goal.
- [ ] Prompts require **reasoning with evidence** where the discipline demands it.
- [ ] Tasks are **challenging enough** to merit talk but not so open that groups lack traction.
- [ ] Text-based discussions target **meaning and interpretation**, not only recall or opinion.

### Collaboration structure

- [ ] Group size and outcome type follow **structured collaborative learning** principles (typically 3–5 with shared outcome when possible).
- [ ] **All learners** have a defined way to articulate thinking (not only a spokesperson).
- [ ] Materials are distinguishable from **unstructured group work**.
- [ ] If competition is used, **learning criteria** remain visible.

### Equity and participation

- [ ] Multiple **pathways to contribute** are available or roles rotate.
- [ ] Supports exist for learners with **less prior knowledge** or language needs.
- [ ] Dominant talkers and free-riders are addressed by design, not only by classroom charisma.
- [ ] **Group composition** and **status-equity** moves are specified when tasks are vulnerable to participation gaps.
- [ ] **Accountable Talk–style** materials make knowledge, reasoning, and community accountabilities visible in norms or stems.

### Digital and hybrid (if applicable)

- [ ] **Channel norms** (voice, chat, doc) and **airtime** rules are explicit for online or hybrid use.
- [ ] Asynchronous discussion requires **grounded posts** (evidence, build-on) and clear deadlines.
- [ ] Digital materials preserve **joint outcomes** and **individual articulation**, not silent co-editing.

### Metacognition and transfer

- [ ] At least one segment supports **planning, monitoring, or evaluating** when metacognitive goals matter.
- [ ] A **reflection** connects this discussion to future tasks.
- [ ] Routines are **reusable** across lessons if culture-building is a goal.

### Clarity and usability

- [ ] Learner copy is readable in the time allotted.
- [ ] Steps are **ordered** and testable in rehearsal.
- [ ] Facilitator notes are separated or clearly marked.
- [ ] Required materials (texts, data, sources) are listed with access expectations.

### Assessment alignment (if applicable)

- [ ] Rubric criteria match observable behaviors in the protocol.
- [ ] Summative use does not confuse **participation** with **mastery** unless intended and valid.
- [ ] Individual accountability is recoverable if grades depend on discussion.

### Legal and ethical

- [ ] No verbatim copying of copyrighted routines, lessons, or rubrics.
- [ ] Source traditions are credited in teacher notes or bibliography without implying endorsement.
- [ ] Civic or media materials avoid partisan framing unless the educational design explicitly requires analyzing partisan sources with clear norms.

## Common Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Better direction |
|---|---|---|
| Question list only | No structure for listening, evidence, or synthesis | Add steps, norms, and a shared product |
| Permanent “reporter” role | One voice; others disengage | Rotate roles; require each member to contribute evidence before reporting |
| Debate without sources | Performance of argument, not learning | Require accessible sources, rebuttal rules, and reflection on evidence quality |
| Talk stems without purpose | Formulaic speech disconnected from ideas | Tie stems to specific moves (clarify, challenge with evidence, connect) |
| Over-long protocols | Time spent on procedure, not thinking | Cut to essential steps; move detail to facilitator notes |
| Copying branded routines verbatim | Copyright risk; may not fit context | Paraphrase structure; name thinking move in your own words |
| Grading “participation” only | Rewards volume or personality | Criteria for evidence use, listening, and building ideas |
| Group work as default | Unnecessary collaboration; free-riding | Use pairs/groups only when talk adds value |
| Ignoring digital asymmetry | Some learners silent in chat rooms | Specify channels, minimum posts, or parallel oral check-ins |
| Status-blind group roles | High-status students dominate; low-status students ignored | Multiple-abilities launch; public competence recognition; rotate intellectual roles |
| Seminar as teacher Q&A | Students perform for teacher, not each other | Circle/fishbowl layout; peer-address norms; facilitator restraint notes |

## How Frameworks Influence These Materials (Without Becoming Them)

| Lens | Influence on authoring | What not to do |
|---|---|---|
| Visible thinking / thinking routines | Repeatable short sequences; thinking-move labels | Paste framework posters as the entire material |
| Collaborative learning evidence | Structure, size, joint outcomes, talk accountability | Label any seating arrangement “collaborative learning” |
| Metacognition | Planning/monitoring/evaluating prompts; think-aloud models | Detached “thinking skills” lessons without subject content |
| UDL | Multiple means to engage and express in discussion | Treat accessibility as an afterthought bullet |
| Dialogic teaching (Cambridge) | Purposeful teacher questions; exploratory talk quality; synthesis closure | Unbounded conversation without goals (see dialogic teaching tradition above) |
| Accountable Talk (Pitt / UNESCO) | Three accountabilities on stems and steps; facilitator elicitation moves | Generic "accountable talk" label with no knowledge, reasoning, or community norms |
| Socratic seminar | Preparation, fishbowl roles, airtime, text-grounded reflection | Debate scoring or opinion sharing without close text work |
| Complex Instruction | Multiple-abilities launch; status treatments; groupworthy joint + individual work | Logistics-only roles that reinforce existing status hierarchies |
| Disciplinary frameworks (NGSS, historical thinking, COR) | Evidence rules and practices | Generic discussion questions unrelated to discipline |
| ACTFL Can-Do | Proficiency-aligned interaction goals | English-only stems in language classes |

Frameworks should **sharpen** artifact decisions; the artifact remains the **protocol, stems, roles, or rubric** learners and teachers hold during the task.

## Relationships To Nearby Resource Families (Summary)

- **Reading materials** supply texts; **discussion materials** supply how to talk about them.
- **Writing-task materials** supply production tasks; discussion materials may structure **peer response** but not replace drafting guides.
- **Project materials** span weeks; discussion materials often **nest inside** milestones as critique or checkpoint protocols.
- **Investigation and lab materials** structure data gathering; discussion materials structure **argument from investigation results**.
- **Criteria materials** generalize scoring; discussion rubrics are **specialized** to discourse behaviors unless merged intentionally.
- **Feedback materials** emphasize response to work products; discussion materials emphasize **interactive sense-making** during learning.

When authoring a unit, expect **combinations**: a reading packet, a seminar protocol, and a short participation rubric are three families working together—each authored with its own handbook logic.

## Example Material Shapes (Original Patterns, Not Copied Protocols)

Authors may use these **generic shapes** as templates. Replace bracketed fields with discipline-specific content.

### Shape A — Text seminar (interpretation-focused)

1. Individual: [prompt about meaning or author choice] — silent 3–5 minutes.
2. Pair: Compare interpretations using stems for **clarify** and **build on**.
3. Group of 3–4: Agree on **two interpretive claims** and **two cited text details** per claim.
4. Whole class: Groups share one claim; class maps agreements and tensions on board.
5. Reflect: What evidence changed your mind?

### Shape B — Collaborative source check (civic or media)

1. Group receives [URL or screenshot set].
2. Roles: Investigator (who/what/others), Recorder, Skeptic (tests alternate sources).
3. Group completes a **three-column log**: claim → evidence checked → other sources say.
4. Group rates confidence and lists what would increase confidence.
5. Whole-class debrief on **process**, not only conclusion.

### Shape C — Metacognitive problem-solving pair

1. Individual attempt on [problem] — 4 minutes.
2. Pair explains method using stems: “I chose … because …” / “I’m unsure about …”.
3. Pair selects revised approach or confirms solution with **justification**.
4. Reflect: Which question from the planning list helped most?

### Shape D — Structured peer critique

1. Writer reads [section] aloud or silently to partner.
2. Partner uses **warm + cool** prompts tied to [rubric criterion 1 and 2] only.
3. Writer paraphrases feedback before responding.
4. Writer notes **one revision commitment**.

### Shape E — Jigsaw (distributed expertise)

1. **Home groups** of 4 assign each member a **specialist letter** (A–D) tied to a distinct subtopic, source set, or problem slice.
2. **Expert groups** — all A’s, all B’s, etc. — meet to master their slice using focused prompts and evidence norms; produce a **one-page expert summary** or three teaching bullets.
3. **Return to home groups** — each expert teaches their slice; group records a **combined claim** or comparison chart on the shared sheet.
4. **Whole class** — groups post one tension or unanswered question for debrief.
5. **Reflect** — Which expert segment changed the group’s conclusion most? Why?

### Shape F — Socratic seminar (text-centered, fishbowl-capable)

1. **Individual prep** — Annotate [text]; write one open interpretive question (not yes/no).
2. **Norms review** — Peer talk, text evidence, airtime balance, paraphrase before disagreeing.
3. **Inner circle** — Open with [interpretive question]; discuss 15–20 minutes; facilitator intervenes only if norms break down.
4. **Outer circle** (if used) — Observers record evidence use, build-on moves, and one question for the inner circle.
5. **Role swap** (optional) — Outer circle joins; prior discussants observe or debrief process.
6. **Reflect** — What did you understand differently about the text? What discourse move helped most?

These shapes illustrate **structure**; they are not official branded routines.
