# Worked-Example Materials

Worked-example materials are tangible learning artifacts that show **how** to carry out a target procedure, solve a problem type, or produce a quality response—usually with visible steps, decisions, and reasoning—not merely **what** the final answer looks like. They exist so novices can study expert performance before or while they practice, with support that can be **reduced over time** until learners work independently.

This handbook is for teachers and instructional designers who author, adapt, or review worked-example sheets, annotated solutions, faded examples, example–problem pairs, and related scaffolds. It is grounded in evidence-based practice guides, worked-example research, and accessibility frameworks.

---

## What This Resource Family Does

The pedagogical job is to **make solution structure visible** and to **manage cognitive demand** during early skill acquisition. When learners face unfamiliar multi-step tasks, they often spend limited working memory on search, dead ends, and guessing rather than on learning the method they need. A well-designed worked example reduces unnecessary load so attention can go to subgoals, sequencing, and rationales.

Worked-example materials also support a **planned transition** from observation to production: learners study a model, complete partially worked items, solve paired problems, and eventually perform without a visible solution trace. Used well, the family improves learning and near transfer compared with long stretches of unguided problem solving for **novices** on **structured** tasks.

The family does **not** replace teaching judgment, live modeling, or feedback. It supplies **persistent artifacts** learners can revisit—on paper, slides, or screen—between and after lessons.

---

## Artifacts In This Family

| Artifact type | What it contains | Typical use |
|---------------|------------------|-------------|
| **Full worked example** | Complete solution with steps shown (and ideally labeled) | First exposure; reference during practice |
| **Annotated solution** | Worked steps plus brief notes on *why* a step was taken, what subgoal it serves, or what was ruled out | Deepening understanding; reducing blind copying |
| **Completion example** (incomplete / partially worked example) | Early or middle steps provided; later steps left for the learner | Early fade; shifts from reception to generation |
| **Faded-example sequence** | Series of similar problems with progressively fewer worked steps | Gradual release of responsibility on one problem type |
| **Example–problem pair** | One worked item immediately followed by a similar item to solve | Alternation within a lesson or worksheet |
| **Interleaved example–practice set** | Multiple types or instances mixed: example → problem → example → problem (not one block of examples then one block of problems) | Stronger learning than massed blocks in many studies summarized by WWC |
| **Error-analysis example** | Incorrect work shown with diagnosis of what went wrong and how to fix it | Teaching correct structure via contrast—not placement (see **diagnostic-instruments**) or revision on learner work (see **feedback-revision-materials**) |
| **Representation-rich model** (especially mathematics) | Concrete, semi-concrete, or visual models integrated with symbols and language | Linking procedures to meaning |

### Nearby Artifacts To Keep Separate

| Nearby family | How it differs |
|---------------|----------------|
| **Practice materials / worksheets** | Emphasize learner-produced answers and repetition; may include no model or only an answer key |
| **Success / exemplar materials** | Show what strong **products** look like for open tasks (essays, projects); criteria and quality features matter more than step-by-step procedure |
| **Criteria / scoring materials** | Define levels of quality for judgment; not primarily instructional models for solving |
| **Study / revision materials** | Optimize memory and review (spacing, retrieval); may mention examples but are not built around modeled solution paths |
| **Diagnostic instruments** | Elicit current understanding; not designed as step-by-step models for acquisition |
| **Answer keys** | Final outcomes without reasoning; weak as worked-example materials unless annotations are added |

**Frameworks are not this family.** Cognitive load theory, explicit instruction, metacognition, and Universal Design for Learning inform **how** you design worked examples; they are not themselves handouts learners study as worked examples.

## When Worked-Example Materials Help

Use or author this family when most of the following apply:

- Learners are **novices on this specific procedure or problem type**, even if they are capable in the subject generally.
- The task has **ordered, dependent steps** where mistakes compound (mathematics, structured writing, lab calculations, code patterns, instrument procedures).
- Learners **cannot start productively** without seeing what “working through” looks like.
- The learning goal includes **procedural fluency, problem-solving method, or near transfer** to similar items—not only appreciation of a finished product.
- You can pair examples with **practice**, **explanation prompts**, and **fading** rather than stopping at one demonstration.
- Representations can be **integrated** (words plus diagrams, concrete linked to abstract) where the domain benefits from dual forms.

Subject-specific fits include: first introduction to an equation type, multi-step word problems, genetics or chemistry algorithms, structured argument templates, software or lab protocols, and science tasks that must integrate practices with core ideas (see NGSS-oriented notes below).

## When This Family Is A Poor Fit Or Commonly Misused

**Poor fit**

- Learners already solve similar items accurately; full examples may become **redundant** and less efficient than problem solving alone (**expertise reversal**; see Kalyuga et al., 2007 in Sources).
- The primary need is **open-ended judgment**, creativity, or ethics without a stable solution path.
- The bottleneck is **motivation or relevance**, not missing method—learners need a compelling problem before a walkthrough.
- The failure is a **stable misconception** about meaning; learners need prediction, conflict, and explanation, not another correct walkthrough of the right procedure.
- The domain is so **ill-structured** that step lists mislead (e.g., “steps” for design thinking that hide real iteration).

**Common misuses**

- Publishing **final answers only** or dense solutions with hidden expert leaps.
- **One demo, no fade plan**—learners never generate the steps that matter.
- **Massed blocks**: many examples, then many problems, with no alternation.
- **Passive copying** without prompts to explain steps or predict next moves.
- **Cluttered layouts** that split attention (e.g., diagram far from the step that uses it).
- Using worked examples as **substitutes for feedback** on the learner’s own work.
- Treating **answer keys** or **model essays** as worked examples when annotations and subgoals are absent.

## Source Traditions And What Each Contributes

| Tradition | What it contributes to authoring |
|-----------|----------------------------------|
| **IES / What Works Clearinghouse practice guides** | Sequencing (interleaving examples with problems), spacing, graphics plus words, abstract–concrete links, deep explanatory questions, systematic math instruction with representations |
| **EEF metacognition guidance** | Explicit modeling, thinking aloud, scaffolding that fades, strategy instruction embedded in subjects |
| **CAST Universal Design for Learning** | Graduated support, differentiated models, scaffolds released as fluency grows, formative checkpoints during practice |
| **The Learning Scientists (research translation)** | Student-facing language for practice, retrieval, spacing, and interleaving alongside classroom materials |
| **NGSS three-dimensional learning** (science) | Examples and tasks should engage **practices** with **core ideas** and **crosscutting concepts**, not isolated “skills” drills |
| **Doing What Works / WWC multimedia summaries** | Practitioner-facing packaging of “alternate worked examples with problem-solving practice” and related practices |

No single standards body owns “worksheet-quality worked examples”; quality comes from combining **learning-science sequencing** with **discipline-accurate models** and **accessibility**.

## Design Decisions Authors Must Make

### 1. Learning target and grain size

Define the **one procedure or problem type** the sequence teaches. Decompose it into **subgoals** learners must recognize (e.g., “identify known quantities” before “select equation”). For **multi-part** items, plan subgoals before drafting the visible solution—breaking complex answers into components for partial credit (CMU *Creating Exams*, background) parallels subgoal-labeled examples. Steps should match **novice** grain size—not expert compression.

### 2. Example difficulty and similarity

Initial examples should be **close to near-transfer practice items** in structure and complexity. Increase variability **gradually** as competence grows; WWC evidence suggests benefits from thoughtful variability between successive examples and problems, not random difficulty jumps.

### 3. Annotation depth

Decide what must be visible besides operations:

- **Subgoal labels** at phase boundaries  
- **Brief rationales** for non-obvious steps  
- **Decision points** (why this strategy, not another)  
- **Common wrong moves** (for error-analysis variants)  

Annotations turn display into instruction; omitting them produces mimicry without understanding. WWC Practice Guide 1 notes that the **optimal level of annotation varies** across studies and learners: some designs favor examples with **minimal** added explanations when subgoals are obvious, while others benefit from **subgoal labels** and brief rationales on non-obvious steps—match depth to what novices on this problem type actually miss, rather than annotating every line by default.

Distinguish **process-oriented** annotations (why a step was taken, what was ruled out) from **product-only** display (operations without reasoning). Process-oriented notes support schema building; product-only traces risk blind copying even when steps are visible (van Gog et al., 2008, in Sources).

### 4. Representation choices

For quantitative and visual domains, plan **integrated** representations:

- Combine **graphics with verbal descriptions** (WWC).  
- **Connect abstract and concrete** representations of the same concept (WWC).  
- In elementary mathematics intervention, use a **well-chosen set of concrete and semi-concrete representations**, teach **clear mathematical language**, and use **number lines** and deliberate **word-problem** instruction where relevant (WWC math practice guide).  

Avoid split attention: place the diagram, table, or model **next to** the step that uses it.

### 5. Sequencing: interleaving vs blocking

**Interleave** worked solutions with problem-solving exercises: learners alternate between studying a worked solution and attempting a similar problem (WWC Recommendation 2; Doing What Works “Examples With Practice”). Blocking all examples before all practice is weaker in the studies summarized in that guide.

Practical patterns include: example 1 → problem 1 → example 2 → problem 2; or every other item worked on a practice sheet.

WWC classroom guidance assumes **example before matched practice** within a pair. Example-based learning research finds **example–problem** pairs preferable to **problem–example** pairs when alternating study and practice (Van Gog et al., 2011); keep items within a pair **structurally aligned** and use a consistent sequence across a lesson. If you alternate, document the pattern on the handout.

### 6. Fading design

Plan how support disappears across **similar tasks**:

- **Completion examples**: omit the **last** step first, then more steps, until only the prompt remains.  
- **Backward fading** (omit final steps first) often supports near transfer better than an abrupt jump from one full example to full problems (Renkl et al., 2002). Direction can depend on which steps learners must own first—prioritize fading the steps that matter most for independence.  
- Avoid an **abrupt jump** from one full example to full problems with no intermediate completions.  

EEF’s scaffolding tools describe **verbal, visual, and written scaffolds** promoted over time toward independent use—apply the same logic to written worked steps.

### 6b. Element interactivity, study, and generation

**Element interactivity** is how much steps in a task depend on one another in working memory. On **high**-interactivity problem types (many dependent steps, interacting quantities, chained inferences), **studying** a well-integrated worked example usually beats asking novices to **generate** solutions from scratch—generation raises extraneous load before a method is stable (Sweller, Kalyuga, & Chen, 2015).

On **low**-interactivity items (few interacting elements, familiar single-step moves), **completion or generation** can be efficient earlier; a full annotated model may add little beyond a short prompt. Match artifact design to interactivity: use **full models + fade** when steps interact; use **lighter completions or paired practice** when the bottleneck is recall of an isolated move, not method search.

Do not treat “always make learners produce first” as a virtue on complex novices tasks—that pattern belongs in **practice materials**, after or interleaved with models, not as a substitute for an initial visible solution path.

### 7. Learner explanation and metacognition

Build **self-explanation** into the artifact or adjacent prompts: “Why was this step taken?” “What subgoal does this serve?” “What would happen if we skipped this?” EEF recommends **explicitly teaching** metacognitive strategies, **modelling** them, and using a structured cycle (plan → monitor → evaluate) in subject lessons.

### 8. Paired practice and checking

Every example sequence should land in **learner attempt + feedback**. Materials may include:

- Space for work beside the model  
- Check-your-understanding items  
- Deep **explanatory** questions (WWC Recommendation 7), not only recall of the final answer  

### 9. Accessibility and variability (UDL)

CAST Guideline **5.3** (*Build fluencies with graduated support for practice and performance*) recommends:

- Scaffolds **gradually released** as independence grows  
- **Differentiated models** showing the same outcome via different approaches where useful  
- **Formative assessment** embedded during practice, not only at the end  
- Attention to process as well as product  

Apply to worked-example packs: optional supports (glossary, read-aloud friendly structure, enlarged graphics), multiple model approaches when legitimate, and clear release criteria.

### 10. Science-specific alignment (when applicable)

NGSS-oriented tasks should reflect **three-dimensional learning**: science and engineering **practices**, **disciplinary core ideas**, and **crosscutting concepts** woven together—not “skills” drills divorced from context. Worked examples for investigations should show **practice-rich reasoning** (e.g., argument from evidence) within a meaningful phenomenon, not only formula manipulation.

### 11. Domain notes beyond elementary mathematics

Report core bundles emphasize **WWC elementary math intervention**; the same design logic applies across domains with discipline-specific representations:

| Domain | What the modeled path should make visible | Common mis-route |
|--------|---------------------------------------------|------------------|
| **Structured writing** | Outline → claim → evidence → commentary moves; sentence frames as **procedure**, not final essay quality | Unannotated “model essay” (**success-exemplar-materials**) |
| **Computer science / algorithms** | Trace execution, state at decision points, why one branch was taken | Code dump without invariant or test reasoning |
| **Lab / technical protocols** | Ordered steps, safety or calibration gates, what to record when | Checklist with no rationale at judgment steps |
| **Quantitative science** | Unit tracking, model choice, link between representation and claim | Formula-only walkthrough without phenomenon context (pair with §10) |
| **Humanities argument** | How evidence is selected and woven—not only thesis wording | Product rubric without visible reasoning moves |

When domain-specific representation guidance is thin in clearinghouse sources, lean on **discipline teaching centers** and published exam responses only for **synthesis** (paraphrase; do not copy proprietary items). **Writing-task materials** own task cycles; this family supplies **procedural models** inside those cycles where steps are teachable.

## Authoring Workflow

### Phase A — Analyze the target performance

1. State the **learning outcome** in observable terms.  
2. List **steps and subgoals** novices need; note where they typically stall.  
3. Collect **student errors** to feed error-analysis examples.  
4. Choose **representations** (symbolic, visual, concrete) the discipline expects.

### Phase B — Draft the anchor worked example

1. Solve a **representative** item completely at novice grain size.  
2. **Label subgoals**; add short rationales for non-obvious moves.  
3. Integrate graphics and text; align concrete and abstract where WWC recommendations apply.  
4. Have a discipline expert check **accuracy** and **terminology** (e.g., concise mathematical language in math materials).

### Illustrative format sketch (fictitious two-step equation)

Use as a layout reference only—not a normative template for all domains.

**Item 1 — full worked example**

| Subgoal | Step | Note |
|---------|------|------|
| Isolate variable | Subtract 7 from both sides → 3x = 12 | Inverse of +7 |
| Solve for x | Divide both sides by 3 → **x = 4** | |
| Check | Substitute 4 into 2x + 7 = 19 ✓ | Catches arithmetic slips |

**Fade plan (same problem type, interleaved with practice)**

| Item | Provided in model | Learner completes |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 2 | Subgoals 1–2 worked | Check + explain “why divide by 3?” |
| 3 | Subgoal 1 only | Steps 2–3 |
| 4 | Prompt only | Full solution + check |

### Phase C — Build the fade and practice sequence

1. List **3–8 similar items** increasing slightly in variation.  
2. Map **fade steps** (which steps disappear on which items).  
3. Insert **example–problem alternation** per interleaving guidance.  
4. Add **explanation prompts** beside completions.  
5. Plan **feedback** channels (teacher, peer, answer key with reasoning, self-check against criteria).

### Phase D — Add metacognitive and study supports

1. Include **think-aloud** prompts or teacher notes for live use (EEF modeling).  
2. Where homework or revision continues the sequence, align with **spacing** and **retrieval** (WWC; Learning Scientists materials)—worked examples are for **acquisition**; spaced practice sustains memory.

### Phase E — Accessibility and format pass

1. Check reading load, visual contrast, and layout split attention.  
2. Offer **optional scaffolds** (word bank, formula reference, bilingual glossary) without changing the core example sequence.  
3. Confirm **graduated release** is obvious to learners (what they must do on each item).

### Phase F — Review and pilot

1. Run the **quality checklist** below.  
2. Pilot with a small group; watch for **passive copying**, **split attention**, and **too-fast fade**.  
3. Revise annotations where learners cannot explain steps in their own words.

## Quality Criteria And Review Checks

### Model solution quality

- [ ] Solution is **correct** and uses conventions expected in the course.  
- [ ] Steps are **complete for novices**—no hidden leaps.  
- [ ] **Subgoals** are labeled; non-obvious steps have **brief rationales**.  
- [ ] Final answer is clearly marked but not treated as the whole lesson.

### Annotation and explanation

- [ ] Annotations explain **why**, not only **what**.  
- [ ] Prompts require **learner explanation** or prediction on at least some items.  
- [ ] **Deep explanatory questions** are included where appropriate (WWC).

### Representation

- [ ] Graphics and text are **integrated** (WWC).  
- [ ] **Concrete and abstract** links are explicit when both appear (WWC).  
- [ ] Mathematical or technical **language** is clear and taught deliberately in math-related materials (WWC math guide).

### Sequencing and fading

- [ ] Worked examples are **interleaved** with practice, not only massed at the start (WWC).  
- [ ] A **fade plan** exists from full example → completion → independent problem.  
- [ ] Similarity and **variability** between items are intentional, not random.

### Practice and feedback

- [ ] Every studied example connects to a **learner attempt**.  
- [ ] Teachers know how **feedback** will be given on practice items.  
- [ ] Optional **error-analysis** examples target predictable mistakes and are not mislabeled **diagnostic** or **feedback** artifacts (see §Relationships).
- [ ] **Element interactivity** judged: full model + fade for high-interactivity types; lighter generation only when appropriate (§6b).

### Accessibility and inclusion (UDL)

- [ ] Supports can be **released** as fluency grows (CAST 5.3).  
- [ ] Multiple **legitimate model approaches** are acknowledged where applicable.  
- [ ] Layout does not create **split attention** or unnecessary reading barriers.

### Science (if applicable)

- [ ] Task engages **practices + core ideas + crosscutting concepts**, not isolated drill (NGSS).

### Boundary hygiene

- [ ] Artifact is not mislabeled **answer key**, **rubric**, or **study guide** without the needed structure.  
- [ ] No **product-only** exemplar passed off as a procedural worked example.

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|--------------|--------------|
| **Answer-only sheet** | No model of reasoning; reinforces guessing |
| **Expert-compressed solution** | Skips steps novices need |
| **Wall of examples, then wall of problems** | Weakens interleaving benefits described in WWC |
| **Fade too fast** | Learners fail before they internalize subgoals |
| **Fade never happens** | Dependence; no transfer to independent performance |
| **Decorative highlighting** | Color without subgoal or rationale |
| **Split diagram / text** | Increases extraneous cognitive load |
| **Copy-and-repeat without prompts** | Mimicry without schema building |
| **Wrong-level example** | Too hard or too unlike practice items |
| **Permanent cheat sheet** | Used after competence; blocks expertise development |
| **Unannotated model essay** | Belongs in success-exemplar family with criteria, not procedural worked-example family |

## How Frameworks Influence The Resource (Without Becoming It)

| Lens | Influence on worked-example materials |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| **Cognitive load / worked-example research** | Justifies full models for novices, integrated representations, fading, and when generation is premature (see §6b); canonical depth below |
| **Explicit instruction** | Teacher may demonstrate live; **artifact** still holds the persistent modeled steps |
| **Metacognition (EEF)** | Modeling, self-explanation prompts, scaffold fade embedded in subjects |
| **UDL (CAST)** | Graduated support, differentiated models, accessible formats, formative checks |
| **Formative assessment** | Completion items and probes reveal when to fade; not the same as summative tests |
| **Retrieval practice** | Follow-up **quizzing** on steps and concepts after study (WWC); complements but does not replace examples |

### Cognitive load and example-based learning (canonical depth)

Classroom practice guides (WWC, EEF) recommend **what** to schedule; cognitive load theory and worked-example research explain **why** those schedules help novices:

- **Worked-example effect:** For unfamiliar multi-step tasks, studying a complete, integrated solution typically supports learning and near transfer better than unguided problem solving, because extraneous search load is reduced (Atkinson, Derry, Renkl, & Wortham, 2000; Sweller et al., 2019).
- **Fading and completion:** Gradually removing steps transfers control while preserving subgoal structure; backward fading (final steps first) is a common design when near transfer to full problems is the goal (Renkl, 2005, 2013; Renkl et al., 2002 in Sources).
- **Example-based learning:** Alternating **study** of a model with **practice** on a similar item, plus **self-explanation**, is the core instructional unit—not a block of passive viewing (Van Gog & Rummel, 2010).
- **Expertise reversal:** As procedure familiarity grows, full examples can become redundant; fade or remove models so practice load targets what learners still need (Kalyuga et al., 2007).
- **Element interactivity:** When many elements must be processed together, worked study dominates early; when interactivity is low, earlier generation or minimal models may suffice (Sweller, Kalyuga, & Chen, 2015; §6b).

Use this lens to defend design choices to reviewers; WWC/EEF remain the primary **authoring** authorities for sequencing and representations in classrooms.

## Relationships To Nearby Resource Families

**Practice materials / worksheets** often contain the **problems** that follow worked examples. Author them together when possible: interleave on one handout or in one lesson arc. Worksheets without models are a different family.

**Success / exemplar materials** show **quality of outcomes** (essays, posters, lab reports). Add **criteria** and commentary on features of strong work. Worked-example materials show **process** for constrained problem types.

**Criteria / scoring materials** help judges score work. Use the same subgoals in worked examples and rubric rows for alignment.

**Feedback / revision materials** respond to **learner-produced** work. Worked examples precede or accompany revision when the gap is “does not know the method,” not “does not know how to improve against criteria.”

**Study / revision materials** emphasize spacing, retrieval, and self-regulation over time. Export **faded sequences** or **key worked steps** into revision packs; do not duplicate entire lesson models without fade or retrieval plan.

**Diagnostic instruments** identify **where** to start. Use diagnostic results to choose **which** example sequence a learner needs, not as a substitute for modeling.

### Error-analysis: one label, three jobs

“Error-analysis” appears in several families; route by **primary job**:

| Family | Job | When to use |
|--------|-----|-------------|
| **diagnostic-instruments** | **Elicit** how the learner is thinking (probes, pretests, misconception tasks) | Before or while choosing instruction |
| **worked-example-materials** (this family) | **Teach** correct structure via a modeled wrong path + diagnosis + fix | During acquisition; predictable class errors |
| **feedback-revision-materials** | **Revise** after the learner has produced work; close gap on their attempt | After submission; ties to criteria and next draft |

Typical arc: **diagnose** → **model** (worked example, including error-analysis variant) → **practice** (**practice-materials-worksheets**) → **feedback** on learner production. Do not use a diagnostic probe when the learner needs a visible correct method, or a worked error-analysis sheet when the task is to score and revise an individual draft.

---

## Quick Author Checklist (One Page)

1. **Target one procedure** and list subgoals.  
2. **Draft one fully worked, annotated example** with integrated representations.  
3. **Interleave** with similar problems—do not block all examples then all problems.  
4. **Fade** steps across a short sequence of completions.  
5. **Prompt self-explanation** and deep questions.  
6. **Plan feedback** on learner attempts.  
7. **Release scaffolds** as fluency grows (UDL).  
8. **Reject** answer-only layouts and expert-compressed leaps.
