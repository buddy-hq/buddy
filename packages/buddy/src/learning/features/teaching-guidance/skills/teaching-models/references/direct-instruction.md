# Direct Instruction

## What This Model Is

Direct instruction is an instructional model for **explicitly teaching targeted knowledge and skills** when learners need clear explanation, demonstration, structured practice, and corrective feedback before they can perform on their own. The teacher begins with full responsibility for showing what success looks like, then gradually transfers responsibility through guided practice and independent practice until learners can demonstrate the lesson objectives reliably.

The model solves a specific teaching problem: **learners lack a clear, accurate, practicable version of a skill or body of knowledge**, and unstructured exposure will not reliably build competence. It is built for outcomes that can be stated precisely, modeled, practiced in small steps, checked frequently, and corrected when performance drifts from the target.

Direct instruction is often summarized as **“I do → we do → you do”**: the teacher models, the class practices together with monitoring and feedback, then learners practice with decreasing support. That pattern is the spine of the model, not an optional embellishment.

## Terminology And Lineage

Several related terms overlap in practice and in research literature. Treating them as interchangeable causes weak design and unfair evaluation.

| Term | Typical meaning | How it relates |
|------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Direct instruction (instructional model)** | A six-phase lesson architecture for explicit teaching, gradual release, and practice with feedback | Common in teacher-education texts on instructional models; emphasizes design flexibility in how new content is introduced |
| **Explicit instruction** | Teacher-led, unambiguous teaching of content and procedures, with modeling, checking, and practice | Often used interchangeably with direct instruction in everyday speech; some authors treat explicit instruction as the broader family and direct instruction as one structured enactment |
| **Gradual release of responsibility** | Shifting cognitive and procedural load from teacher to learner across a lesson or unit | Overlaps heavily with the “I do / we do / you do” structure inside direct instruction |
| **Rosenshine’s principles of instruction** | Research-derived teaching functions such as review, small steps, questioning, modeling, guided practice, high success rates, scaffolding, independent practice, and periodic review | Describes patterns observed in effective teaching of **well-structured** content; aligns with direct instruction but is not identical to any single lesson script |
| **Direct Instruction (DI)** | A **programmatic** tradition associated with Siegfried Engelmann: field-tested curricula, tight sequencing, scripted presentation, fast pacing, choral responding, and mastery-based advancement | Shares the logic of explicit teaching but is **more prescriptive** than the general instructional model; evidence from Project Follow Through and similar studies often refers to these programs, not to every teacher-designed explicit lesson |

**Practical distinction:** “Direct instruction” in curriculum-design handbooks usually means **a reusable lesson architecture** teachers adapt across subjects. “Direct Instruction” with capital letters often means **published programs and scripts** in the Engelmann lineage. Rosenshine’s work names **observable teaching functions** that effective teachers use when teaching stepwise, definable content. All three stress clarity, modeling, practice, and checking—but only the programmatic tradition requires fixed scripts and commercial materials.

**Rosenshine’s five meanings (2008):** Educational literature uses “direct instruction” in overlapping senses that are easy to conflate:

1. **General teacher-led instruction**—any academic teaching led by the teacher, regardless of quality.
2. **Effective-teacher procedures**—patterns from process–product research (review, small steps, questioning, guided practice, checking, independent practice, periodic review).
3. **Cognitive-strategy instruction**—explicit procedures for teaching learners how to execute a strategy (e.g., reciprocal teaching, self-questioning).
4. **Programmatic Direct Instruction**—scripted **Distar** / Engelmann-style curricula with tight sequencing and choral responding.
5. **Pejorative “direct instruction”**—passive lecture with little learner engagement, often contrasted with “active” learning.

The table above maps mainly to meanings 1–2 (instructional model) and 4 (programmatic DI); meaning 5 is a misuse to avoid, not a model to enact.

The model draws on multiple learning traditions: clear targets and stepwise task analysis from behavioral approaches; **modeling and observational learning** from social learning theory; and **connecting new learning to prior knowledge** with timely feedback from cognitive perspectives. Research on teaching and learning often associates explicit, well-structured instruction—with modeling, guided practice, checking, and correction—with stronger outcomes than minimally guided approaches for **novice learners** and **well-defined, foundational objectives**, especially when practice and feedback are substantial rather than when “direct” only means extended talk (Rosenshine, 2012; Archer & Hughes, 2011; Kirschner et al., 2006). That advantage is **not universal**: discovery, inquiry, and problem-based approaches remain appropriate when prior knowledge is sufficient, when goals emphasize investigation or conceptual change, or when the task itself requires productive struggle rather than procedural fluency first.

## When Direct Instruction Fits

Direct instruction is a strong default when:

- **The learning target is definable**—procedures, rules, vocabulary, conventions, algorithms, safety steps, or factual structures that can be demonstrated and practiced.
- **Prerequisites are missing or unstable**, and the blockage is “I have not been shown a clear version of this yet,” not “I disagree with the idea” or “I do not see why it matters.”
- **Sequence and accuracy matter**—foundational skills that later learning depends on, computation, decoding, lab technique, standard forms, or any domain where errors compound.
- **Cognitive load is high** for the learner relative to the task; breaking the task into small public steps reduces overwhelm.
- **Time-to-competence** matters and discovery would be inefficient for the specific objective (though discovery may still follow once the base skill exists).
- **Whole-class or small-group efficiency** is needed for a shared baseline before differentiation or transfer tasks.

It also works as a **frame inside which other presentation strategies sit**. The “present new material” phase may be deductive (rule then examples) or inductive (examples then rule), and may include brief lecture segments, demonstrations, or concept-focused activities—so long as the lesson still moves into monitored practice with feedback.

## When Direct Instruction Is A Poor Fit

Avoid treating direct instruction as the only tool, or as the right tool, when:

- **The objective is primarily conceptual restructuring**—learners hold a coherent but wrong mental model that must be challenged, not filled in by explanation alone.
- **The goal is open investigation, argument, or interpretation** where premature telling short-circuits the reasoning the lesson is meant to produce.
- **Learners already received a clear explanation** and still cannot perform; the failure may be faulty assumptions, missing discrimination between cases, lack of relevance, or need for worked examples—not another lecture.
- **Motivation and meaning are the bottleneck**; learners need a problem, case, or purpose before they can absorb steps.
- **The “presentation” becomes a long monologue** with little practice, questioning, or checking—this violates the model even if it is labeled “direct.”
- **Independent practice is assigned before guided success**—homework or seatwork on skills learners cannot yet perform accurately reinforces error.
- **The content is too broad or vague** to state as lesson objectives learners can recognize and demonstrate.

Direct instruction is **necessary but not sufficient** for a full instructional repertoire. Exclusive use tends to narrow tasks and dull engagement; absence of the model leaves teachers without a disciplined way to build foundational skills.

## Architecture Of A Direct Instruction Lesson

The model is commonly enacted in **six phases**. Phases are public and logical so both teacher and learners know where they are in the lesson. Movement is conditional: later phases depend on evidence from earlier ones.

### 1. Review Previously Learned Material

**Purpose:** Activate and verify prerequisites; link new learning to what learners already control.

**Teacher moves:** Briefly revisit skills or ideas the new lesson depends on; use homework review, short drills, oral recall, or diagnostic questions; connect yesterday’s learning to today’s target; **reteach prerequisites** if diagnostics show gaps—especially when the new skill is hierarchically dependent.

**Learner moves:** Retrieve prior knowledge; participate in review; use metacognition to notice what is solid versus shaky.

**Readiness to advance:** Learners show adequate mastery of prerequisite material, or the teacher adjusts the lesson to close gaps before introducing new content. Diagnostic information (informal questioning, short tasks, or formal prechecks) informs pacing for the class and for individuals.

### 2. State Objectives For The Lesson

**Purpose:** Make the instructional contract visible—what learners will know and be able to do by the end.

**Teacher moves:** State objectives in **learner-friendly language** (not only institutional wording); distinguish outcomes from activities (“you will be able to…” rather than “you will practice…”); relate objectives to prior learning; optionally use an **advance organizer**—a higher-level idea that connects known to new (not a summary of everything coming).

**Learner moves:** Understand what success looks like; connect the lesson to prior experience.

**Readiness to advance:** Learners can paraphrase the goal in their own words at a basic level. Objectives are within reach of the group given prerequisite review.

### 3. Present New Material (“I Do”)

**Purpose:** Model what learners should eventually do, with enough structure that novices can see the steps, decisions, and standards involved.

**Teacher moves:** Present content in **small, ordered segments**; combine clear explanation with demonstration, media, or worked examples; use think-alouds for procedures; limit scope (few significant points with illustrations rather than wide coverage); punctuate with questions and comprehension checks; anticipate novice difficulties (experts often skip steps learners need); draw on **pedagogical content knowledge**—how to teach this particular content—not only subject expertise.

Presentations should be **clear, focused, and checkable**: instructions and explanations, attention-focusing moves, and systematic monitoring of understanding. Brief lecture can serve this phase if kept short, organized from general to specific, illustrated with examples, and summarized back to the organizer—but long, digressive talk without practice is a common failure mode. Attention spans for passive listening are limited; younger learners typically need shorter presentation segments than adults, consistent with Rosenshine’s emphasis on teaching new material in small steps.

**Learner moves:** Attend to modeling; ask clarifying questions; connect to prior knowledge; respond to formative checks during presentation.

**Readiness to advance:** A representative sample of learners can answer convergent check questions about the modeled content, or the teacher has evidence that the modeled version of the task is understood well enough to attempt supervised practice.

### 4. Guide Practice, Assess Performance, Provide Corrective Feedback (“We Do”)

**Purpose:** Gradual release—learners attempt the task while the teacher monitors, questions, corrects, and retrains.

**Teacher moves:** Structure **choral, paired, or individual practice** with immediate feedback; ask prepared questions (including recall and comprehension for basic skills); allow **wait time** of roughly **three seconds** for convergent recall or comprehension checks before accepting answers (longer when the question requires reasoning); track participation so a few volunteers do not mask class confusion—optional structures such as **cold calling** (calling on learners who have not volunteered) or **call-and-response** (whole-group choral answers) can broaden participation when the classroom climate treats wrong answers as normal steps in learning; reteach or remodel when error rates are high; aim for a **high success rate** during supervised practice—Rosenshine (2012) links effective guided practice to roughly **about 80%** correct responses as a balance of learning and challenge, while IMA-style direct instruction urges accuracy **as close to 100% as possible** before advancing and reteaches rather than blaming learners when objectives are missed. Treat both framings as **very high supervised success**: enough correct performance to justify independence, with reteaching when error rates stay high—not perfection on the first try, and not moving on while most learners still miss the target; keep feedback **timely, specific, and tied to objectives** (what happened, distance from target, what to try next).

Useful participation structures include having learners repeat directions, summarize for a partner, supply additional examples, or apply the rule to a novel item under supervision. Reteaching with a **different representation or explanation** helps learners who missed the first presentation; early successful learners can sometimes articulate steps for peers in their own words.

**Learner moves:** Practice each step; answer and ask questions; act on feedback; reflect on what is still difficult.

**Readiness to advance:** Most learners perform with few errors under supervision; persistent errors are addressed rather than ignored. The teacher has evidence that unsupervised work would not mostly practice mistakes.

### 5. Independent Practice, Assess Performance, Provide Corrective Feedback (“You Do”)

**Purpose:** Build fluency and ownership—learners rehearse without immediate teacher scaffolding, toward automaticity appropriate to the objective.

**Teacher moves:** Assign independent work only after guided success; circulate or monitor; ensure learners can **self-check or access checkpoints**; correct systematic errors before they stabilize; give feedback on completed work; align tasks with objectives and with what was modeled and guided (guided tasks should closely match the demonstration; independent tasks may vary more to support transfer while still staying within practiced element types).

Independent products may include problem sets, writing, diagrams, games, or authentic tasks—as long as objectives and prior practice align.

**Learner moves:** Rehearse and consolidate; work toward smoother, more automatic performance where fluency is a goal; use feedback and self-assessment.

**Readiness to advance:** Independent work shows mostly accurate performance, or errors are caught and remediated. For graded work, tasks must include only elements learners had opportunity to practice.

### 6. Review Periodically, With Corrective Feedback As Needed

**Purpose:** Retention, overlearning, and transfer maintenance across lessons and time.

**Teacher moves:** Build **spaced review** into plans; check homework when it is assigned; reteach when review shows decay; at term boundaries, verify retention of foundational skills; use review to connect skills that stack (each skill enabling the next).

**Learner moves:** Demonstrate retained knowledge; respond to reteaching when needed.

This phase is both **within-lesson consolidation** and **ongoing instructional hygiene** across days and weeks.

## Checks For Learning Across The Model

Assessment is embedded, not reserved for the end:

- **Diagnostic review** before new teaching establishes starting points.
- **Objective-aligned checks** during presentation and guided practice reveal partial understanding.
- **Guided practice functions as formative assessment** when tasks mirror modeled performance.
- **Independent practice yields performance data** for feedback and grading decisions.
- **Periodic review** tests whether learning persists.

When learners fail, the model expects **analysis of instruction**, not blame: Were prerequisites solid? Were steps small enough? Were objectives clear? Was presentation too long or too shallow? Was guided practice sufficient and inclusive? Was independent work assigned too early? Was review built in?

Rubrics and criteria can clarify expectations for independent products, but they must reflect what was actually taught and practiced in the lesson sequence.

## Differentiation Within The Model

Direct instruction supports differentiation without abandoning shared objectives:

- **Flexible grouping** during practice—homogeneous for targeted skill work, heterogeneous for peer explanation—provided groups change based on evidence, not fixed labels.
- **Varied questioning** by readiness, interest, or background while holding all learners accountable to the same lesson objectives (e.g., lower cognitive demand for recall and comprehension checks; higher demand for analyze/evaluate/create when foundations are secure).
- **Adjusted pacing** from diagnostic data—reteaching, extra guided rounds, or extension for learners who demonstrate early mastery.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

- Objectives are **outcome-based and learner-visible**, not hidden in teacher plans.
- New content arrives in **small steps** with checks between steps.
- Modeling shows **expert thinking**, not only final answers.
- Guided practice involves **most learners**, not a volunteer subset.
- Feedback is **frequent, specific, and neutral** about performance relative to targets.
- The class achieves a **high success rate** before independent work.
- Independent practice is **monitored** for error, not assigned as default homework regardless of readiness.
- Review and reteaching are **planned**, especially for hierarchical skills.
- The lesson ends with evidence learners can **do** the objective, not only hear it.

## Signs Of Shallow Or Mislabeled Implementation

- Long teacher talk with minimal learner action (“direct” as lecture-only).
- Objectives describe activities instead of demonstrable learning.
- Skipping review or diagnostics, then introducing content learners cannot anchor.
- Moving on because the material was “covered,” though learners did not perform successfully.
- Guided practice that is **too short, too easy, or dominated by a few students**.
- Independent work that practices **errors at speed**.
- Confusing **Engelmann-style scripted programs** with any explicit explanation.
- Using direct instruction when the learning goal requires **productive struggle, debate, or discovery** as the primary mechanism.

## Common Misuses And Category Confusion

| Misuse | Why it fails | Better direction |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| Re-explaining the same way after failure | Learners may need discrimination, inquiry, worked examples, or misconception repair—not volume | Change representation, task, or model |
| Skipping “we do” | Independence without supervised success encodes mistakes | Extend guided practice with feedback |
| Treating all teacher talk as direct instruction | Without modeling, checking, and practice, it is not the model | Add structure and evidence of learning |
| Citing DI program research to defend any chalk-and-talk | Program effects depend on sequencing, scripts, and correction routines | Match claims to the actual implementation studied |
| Using direct instruction for ill-defined “understanding” goals | Vague targets cannot be modeled and checked stepwise | Clarify objectives or choose a concept/inquiry model |
| Exclusive reliance on the model | Narrows pedagogy and engagement | Pair with inquiry, cases, discussion, and transfer tasks when goals require them |

## Relationships To Nearby Models

- **Advance organizer:** Often used in early phases to connect prior knowledge to new content at a higher level of generality; supports but does not replace practice.
- **Worked example with fading:** Overlaps the presentation and early guided phases when the target is multi-step performance; emphasizes completed examples before partial completion tasks.
- **Guided practice / gradual release:** Names the responsibility shift that direct instruction embeds across phases 3–5.
- **Concept attainment / concept development:** Better when the learning goal is **category boundaries and features** from examples; direct instruction may still deliver a definition afterward, but should not replace inductive concept formation when that is the objective.
- **Inquiry / predict-check-explain:** Better when a **faulty model must be confronted** through evidence; direct instruction builds skill and knowledge that inquiry may presuppose or follow.
- **Mastery learning loop:** Emphasizes **repeat-until-criterion** cycles; direct instruction supplies the initial teach-practice structure mastery loops extend.
- **Case-based / problem-based teaching:** Better when **relevance and context** drive learning; direct instruction may teach tools needed inside a case later.

Rosenshine’s principles overlap direct instruction but are **research generalizations**, not a single lesson template. Engelmann’s Direct Instruction is **curriculum and script intensive**. The instructional model described here is the **design architecture** teachers adapt—closest to explicit teaching with disciplined release of responsibility.

## Design Reminders For Instructional Designers

- Align objectives, presentation, guided tasks, independent tasks, and assessments on the **same target performances**.
- Prefer **fewer points, more examples and checks** over broad coverage.
- Plan questions before the lesson; use them diagnostically.
- Budget time for practice and correction; shorten presentation if needed.
- Reteach prerequisites and lesson content when success rates fall—**going forward without learning wastes more time later**.
- Use the model for **foundational skills and clear knowledge**, then deliberately hand off to discussion, application, inquiry, or projects when those goals dominate.
