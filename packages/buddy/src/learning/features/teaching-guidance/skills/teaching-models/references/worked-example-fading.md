# Worked Example With Fading

## What This Model Is

**Worked Example With Fading** is an instructional sequence for teaching **multi-step procedures and problem-solving methods** when learners are novices on the target skill. The teacher first presents a complete solution with visible reasoning (subgoals, decisions, and why each step matters). Learners study that model, then work through **completion examples** in which one or more solution steps are omitted, and finally solve **similar problems** with increasing responsibility until they can perform the task without a visible solution trace.

The model addresses a specific instructional problem: beginners asked to solve unfamiliar problems often spend working memory on search, false starts, and strategy choice instead of learning the procedure they need. Showing a fully worked solution reduces that extraneous load so learners can attend to the structure of the method. **Fading** then structures the handoff from studying examples to generating solutions, so the transition is gradual rather than abrupt.

Research on the **worked-example effect** (cognitive load theory) shows that, for novices, studying worked examples often leads to better learning and transfer than spending the same time on unguided problem solving. **Fading worked solution steps** (successively removing steps from the example until only the problem statement remains) is often more effective than alternating isolated example–problem pairs in structured domains with novice learners, because fading smooths cognitive demand across the transition from observation to production—though the advantage depends on domain structure, fade design, and prior knowledge.

This handbook treats **worked example + fading** as one enactment pattern. It is **not** a named standalone model in *Instruction: A Models Approach* (IMA) or *Models of Teaching* (MOT) crosswalks, though IMA offers **partial** overlap—scaffolding in Chapter 2 and worked or partially completed examples in the Chapter 11 inquiry/PBL discussion; MOT preview does not add a dedicated worked-example chapter. The pattern synthesizes cognitive-load and worked-example research commonly enacted inside explicit lessons (with partial overlap to scaffolding and direct instruction presentation). A single worked example without a planned fade can help briefly, but the named model includes the deliberate reduction of support across near-transfer tasks.

## Terminology And Lineage

| Term | Meaning | Notes |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Worked example** | A problem already solved, with steps shown (ideally with subgoal labels and brief rationales) | Can be written, visual, or demonstrated; not the same as pure observation without segmented steps |
| **Completion example** (completion problem) | A partially worked example: early steps are shown, later steps are left for the learner | Also called an **incomplete example**; the learner completes the sequence |
| **Fading** | Successive removal of worked steps across a sequence of similar tasks until the learner solves a full problem alone | Contrasts with a single jump from full example to full problem |
| **Example–problem pair** | One worked example followed by one similar problem to solve | Common baseline in research; fading often outperforms abrupt pairing for novices in structured domains when fade design matches what learners must own |
| **Subgoal** | A meaningful intermediate aim within a larger procedure (e.g., “isolate the variable” before “simplify”) | Labeling subgoals supports transfer; missing subgoals yields brittle imitation |
| **Self-explanation** | Learner-generated talk or writing about *why* a step was taken | Increases benefits of worked examples, especially for conceptual understanding |
| **Expertise reversal effect** | Worked examples help novices but can become redundant or harmful for advanced learners who would learn more from solving | Fading and timely release to problem solving mitigate this |

**Lineage.** The model grows from **cognitive load theory** (limited working memory; extraneous vs germane load) and decades of **worked-example research** in mathematics, science, and other well-structured domains. **Renkl** and colleagues formalized **fading procedures** that integrate problem-solving elements into example study until learners solve independently. **Hattie and Yates** summarize worked and completion examples for practitioners; **Atkinson, Derry, Renkl, and Wortham** synthesize instructional principles from the research base.

**Common confusions:**

- **One demo on the board** is not the full model unless it is followed by completion tasks and a fade plan.
- **Worked examples are not “giving answers.”** They are temporary scaffolds for novices; the instructional goal is eventual independent performance with understanding.
- **Fading is not the same as gradual release (I do → we do → you do).** Gradual release emphasizes joint live performance and teacher monitoring during practice; worked-example fading emphasizes studying a solution trace, then completing increasingly incomplete traces, before full generation.
- **Scaffolding** (general support that is removed over time) is the broader idea; worked-example fading is a specific, research-backed form of scaffolding for procedural learning.

## When Worked Example With Fading Fits

Use the sequence when most of the following are true:

- **Learners cannot start** — they stare at the task, guess randomly, or repeat a familiar but wrong procedure.
- **The task has several dependent steps** — order matters and errors compound.
- **Cognitive load is high** — multiple representations, unfamiliar notation, new tools, or several component skills at once.
- **Learners need to see expert thinking before trying** — they lack a mental model of what “working through” looks like.
- **The learning target is procedural or problem-solving fluency** in a **well-defined domain** (clear moves and goals: algebra, genetics crosses, lab technique, software steps, etc.).
- **Learners are novices on this specific procedure**, even if they are capable in general.

Strong fits include: first exposure to a new equation type, multi-step lab calculations, structured writing frames, instrument or software procedures, proof templates, and any skill where research shows novices learn more from examples than from unguided practice.

The model also fits when **interleaving** is planned: a worked example, then a similar problem, then the next faded example—so each study episode connects to immediate application.

---

## When The Model Is A Poor Fit

Avoid relying on worked-example fading when:

- **Learners are ready for independent practice** — they already solve similar items accurately; more examples add redundant load (**expertise reversal**).
- **The primary goal is conceptual boundary-setting** — defining what belongs in a category, what counts as an instance, or resolving a misconception about meaning (concept attainment, contrasting cases, or inquiry may fit better).
- **Learners passively copy without explaining decisions** — the sequence becomes mimicry; without self-explanation or checks, understanding and transfer suffer.
- **The domain is ill-defined** — no stable subgoals or solution path (open design, ethical judgment, creative production without criteria).
- **The real bottleneck is motivation or relevance** — learners need a reason to care before studying steps (problem-first or case-first framing may come first; worked examples supply the method afterward).
- **The failure is a stable faulty model**, not missing procedure — learners need prediction, conflict, and explanation, not another correct walkthrough of the right method.
- **Only fluency is missing** — learners already understand when and why to apply the method but need speed and accuracy (focused practice loops fit better).

A poor fit is also likely when the “worked example” is **cluttered** (split attention between distant text and diagrams, irrelevant labels, expert-compressed steps) so learners cannot see the structure they are meant to learn.

## Phases And Sequence

Think of the model as a **support gradient on similar tasks**, not a one-time demonstration.

### Phase 1 — Select the target procedure and analyze steps for novices

**Purpose:** Ensure the example shows what novices must actually learn—not what an expert sees in one glance.

**Teacher moves:** Identify the performance goal and success criteria; break the procedure into **subgoals** and steps at a grain size novices can follow; remove extraneous information from displays; align the first example closely with the near-transfer problems that follow. Use cognitive task analysis when needed: ask “why this step?” until hidden moves are visible.

**Learner moves:** (Minimal in this phase.) If consulted, surface where they get stuck.

**Transition signal:** You can name the subgoals learners should use and predict common error points.

### Phase 2 — Present one fully worked example with reasoning (*model*)

**Purpose:** Make expert problem-solving visible: what is done, in what order, and why.

**Teacher moves:** Walk through a **complete** solution; label **subgoals**; give brief rationales for non-obvious steps; use integrated text and diagrams (avoid unnecessary split attention); match decomposition to current novice level (more intermediate steps for beginners, fewer for those with partial knowledge). Prompt **self-explanation** (“Why was this step taken?” “What subgoal does this serve?”).

**Learner moves:** Follow the solution; explain steps in their own words; predict the next step before it is revealed when appropriate; ask clarifying questions.

**Transition signal:** Learners can paraphrase the solution path and identify subgoals—not only nod along.

### Phase 3 — Completion example(s): partial solution, learner finishes (*early fade*)

**Purpose:** Shift from reception to generation while keeping most of the structure visible.

**Teacher moves:** Present a **similar** problem with **one or more later steps omitted** (completion example). Common research sequences fade **backward** (remove the final step first, then more steps) or **forward** (remove early steps first); Renkl et al. (2004) found that **which** solution principles are faded matters more than rigid fade position alone. Fade direction can be domain- and knowledge-dependent; prioritize fading the steps learners must own. Omitted steps should require the learner to practice the parts they need to own. Increase omitted steps across items until only the problem statement remains. Give **corrective feedback** on completed work.

**Learner moves:** Fill in missing steps; self-explain each addition; check against the logic of the full model.

**Transition signal:** Accurate completion with reasons, not just correct final answers.

### Phase 4 — Near examples with shifting responsibility (*continued fade*)

**Purpose:** Smooth the transition so cognitive demand rises gradually rather than jumping from full support to none.

**Teacher moves:** Offer a short series of **structurally similar** problems with decreasing scaffolding (more blanks, fewer hints, shorter rationales). **Interleave** study and practice: faded example → similar problem → next fade level. Monitor for passive copying; require explanation of decisions.

**Learner moves:** Take on more steps independently; compare new problems to the original model; notice what varies and what stays the same.

**Transition signal:** Learners complete near-transfer items with few errors and can state when the procedure applies.

### Phase 5 — Independent practice and light variation (*release*)

**Purpose:** Build fluency, retention, and beginnings of transfer.

**Teacher moves:** Assign problems learners solve without a visible trace; introduce **controlled variation** (slightly different surface features, one contrasting non-example, or two solution paths to the same outcome) once the core procedure is stable. Space practice over time.

**Learner moves:** Solve independently; self-check using criteria; seek feedback when stuck after an honest attempt.

**Transition signal:** Stable accuracy on independent items; appropriate application—not only on items that look identical to the first example.

## Example–Problem Pairs Versus A Fading Sequence

| Approach | What happens | Typical novice experience |
|----------|--------------|---------------------------|
| **Example–problem pair** | Full example, then full problem | Abrupt jump; high error rate on the problem |
| **Fading sequence** | Full example → completion → increasingly incomplete → full problem | Smoother load; fewer unproductive errors during learning |

Both beat **problem-only** practice for novices in many studies. When time allows, plan a **fade ladder** on similar problems rather than a single handoff.

---

## Teacher Moves And Learner Moves (Summary)

**Teacher:** Analyze steps for novices → design a clean full example → prompt self-explanation → run completion and faded items → interleave with similar problems → give specific feedback → release to independent practice with variation and spacing → stop fading when learners show expertise.

**Learner:** Study the model → explain steps → complete missing steps → solve with increasing independence → practice until fluent → recognize conditions for use.

Expertise for the teacher includes **pedagogical content knowledge** (what makes this procedure hard for beginners) and **example design** (subgoals, extraneous load, grain size of steps).

## Checks For Understanding And Transition Criteria

| Phase | What to check | Practical checks |
|-------|---------------|------------------|
| Full worked example | Comprehension of path and subgoals | Paraphrase; “why this step?”; predict next step |
| Completion example | Accurate generation of omitted steps | Worked partial solutions; oral explanation |
| Faded series | Stable performance as steps disappear | Error rate across similar items |
| Independent practice | Fluency and appropriate use | Mixed items; explain when procedure applies |

**Decision rules:**

- If learners **cannot explain** steps after a full example, do not fade yet—reteach with clearer subgoals or smaller steps.
- If learners **copy without reasoning**, add self-explanation prompts or a completion step before more fade.
- If learners **succeed on identical-looking items only**, add variation and prompts about **when** the method applies (condition recognition).
- If learners **already solve accurately**, skip or shorten fading—more problems beat more examples (**expertise reversal**).
- **Extra teacher talk** during silent study of a clear worked example often adds little; feedback on **learner-produced** steps matters more.

## Design Principles For Strong Examples

- **Reduce extraneous load** — integrate text and diagrams (multimedia design principles; Clark & Mayer, 2016); remove irrelevant labels; avoid making learners hunt across multiple sources.
- **Signal subgoals** — name intermediate aims explicitly when novices need them.
- **Explain when it helps** — rationales support transfer; if learners reliably self-explain, some overt explanations can be trimmed.
- **Match decomposition to prior knowledge** — experts underestimate steps (**expert blind spot**); show hidden operations beginners miss.
- **Keep near-transfer similarity** — early fade items should mirror the model’s structure; far transfer comes later.
- **Interleave example and practice** — a similar problem after study strengthens memory and motivation.
- **Encourage self-explanation** — especially for linking procedure to concepts and conditions of use.
- **Match representation to the task** — on story problems and quantitative reasoning, how the problem is represented (diagram, familiar context, equation-first) affects what novices can parse before they study steps (Koedinger & Nathan, 2004).

## Signs Of Good Implementation Versus Shallow Implementation

| Good implementation | Shallow implementation |
|---------------------|------------------------|
| Subgoals and rationales visible in the full example | Steps listed with no “why” or structure |
| Planned fade ladder on similar problems | One demo, then unrelated homework |
| Learners explain decisions, not only answers | Silent copying of the model |
| Completion steps placed where learners need practice | Random blanks that do not target key moves |
| Near problems before far or varied problems | Only identical surface items |
| Fade stops when expertise appears | Endless examples for learners who already solve |
| Clean displays; integrated resources | Split attention and clutter |
| Feedback on generated steps | Only final answer checked |

## Common Misuses And Category Confusion

| Misuse | Why it fails | What to do instead |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Worked example as answer key only** | No reasoning, no fade | Model thinking; sequence completion tasks |
| **Fade too fast** | Cognitive overload returns | More completion steps; smaller step size |
| **Never fading** | Dependence; poor transfer | Plan omitted steps through to full problems |
| **Wrong grain size** | Steps too large (expert compressed) or too small (trivial) | Task-analyze with novice lens |
| **Cluttered example** | Extraneous load blocks learning | Integrate and strip irrelevant detail |
| **Only identical problems** | Navigation-system risk (Schwartz et al., 2011; cf. Schwartz, *The ABCs of How We Learn*, worked-examples chapter): learners follow steps but miss **when** to apply | Vary surface features; prompt condition recognition |
| **Examples after mastery** | Redundant for experts | Shift to problem-heavy practice |
| **Conceptual goals via procedure only** | Categories and principles need different models | Pair with contrasting cases or inquiry when needed |
| **Unguided discovery for novices** | High load, low learning | Worked examples first; problem solving later |
| **Equating with “I do” lecture** | No learner generation on partial traces | Use completion and fade, not only teacher talk |

**Navigation-system analogy (risk; Schwartz et al., 2011; cf. Schwartz, *The ABCs of How We Learn*, worked-examples chapter):** Step-by-step guidance can produce route-following without landmarks—learners follow turns but do not learn when the route applies. Combine fading with **self-explanation**, **when-to-use** prompts, and later variation so learners connect procedure to problem conditions.

## Contrasts With Nearby Models

| Nearby approach | How it differs | When to prefer the other |
|-----------------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Guided practice / gradual release** | Emphasizes live joint performance: teacher and learners co-construct steps in real time | When learners partly understand and need confidence, hints, and correction during shared work |
| **Direct Instruction (six-phase model)** | Full lesson architecture: review, objectives, present, guided practice, independent practice, review | When prerequisites, objectives, and sustained monitoring across a whole lesson matter |
| **Generic explicit teaching** | May explain once without example–fade architecture | Brief clarification for learners who already have a method |
| **Discovery / unguided problem solving** | Learners search for solution paths | When prior knowledge supports exploration; not for typical novice procedural introduction |
| **Inquiry / predict–check–explain** | Prioritizes prediction, evidence, and revision of ideas | When a faulty mental model must be surfaced and changed |
| **Concept attainment** | Learners infer category rules from yes/no examples | When boundaries between concepts are the learning goal |
| **Advance organizer** | Front-loads a map of ideas before detail | When overwhelm is from missing structure, not missing step-by-step procedure |
| **Mastery learning loop** | Repeat–feedback–advance until competency | When the method is understood but fluency is the bottleneck |
| **Case-based / problem-based teaching** | Starts from authentic context and need-to-know | When relevance must precede tool instruction |
| **Just-in-time telling** | Learners struggle first, then receive efficient explanation | When productive struggle is possible and “need to know” is already felt |

Worked Example With Fading is the preferred entry when **the learner cannot start** and **cognitive load is high** on a multi-step target. Guided practice is often the **next** move once a basic solution model exists and errors are small enough to correct during joint work.

---

## Outcomes And Evidence (What To Expect)

- **Near-term:** Faster accurate performance on **similar** problems; fewer wasted errors during initial learning compared with problem-only conditions for novices.
- **Medium-term:** Better recall of solution steps when fading is combined with practice and, where needed, self-explanation.
- **Limits:** Strongest in **well-structured** procedural domains; conceptual depth and **far transfer** usually require additional activities beyond fading alone (self-explanation, contrasting cases, application across contexts).
- **Synthesis finding:** Providing worked examples shows a meaningful, **stage-dependent** effect in meta-analytic syntheses (e.g., *d* ≈ 0.57 in Hattie’s Visible Learning band—medium, not top-tier—strongest when learners are new to the method).
