# Case-Based and Problem-Based Teaching

## What This Model Is

Case-based and problem-based teaching are context-first instructional approaches. Instead of introducing abstract principles and only later applying them, the teacher anchors learning in a realistic situation—a case narrative, scenario, or open problem—that demands judgment, explanation, or action. Learners work from the situation toward concepts, evidence, and principles that help resolve it.

Both approaches address the same instructional problem: learners who cannot see why content matters, who disengage from decontextualized explanation, or who need to reason from evidence and constraints before they can name or use a general idea. The approaches also support durable understanding when learners construct knowledge through investigation rather than receiving finished answers.

They are related but not identical. **Case-based teaching** typically centers on a rich, bounded story (often with incomplete information) and emphasizes analysis, discussion, and decision-making from multiple perspectives. **Problem-based learning** typically centers on an ill-structured problem that organizes the unit; learners identify what they need to know, pursue resources, and refine solutions over time. In practice, teachers often blend them: a case supplies context and stakes; problem-solving structures supply inquiry, resource use, and iteration.

**Crosswalk scope:** In *Instruction: A Models Approach*, **problem-based inquiry** is the named Chapter 11 model (5E cycle and inquiry chart). **Case-based teaching** in professional fields (law, business, medicine) is a distinct tradition; IMA Chapters 13–15 are backward-design **unit-planning exemplars** that combine several models—they are not the same as Harvard-style or professional-school case method.

## Terminology and Lineage

**Case method / case-based teaching**  
A discussion-oriented tradition, widely used in law, business, medicine, and other professional fields, in which learners study accounts of real or realistic situations. Cases present unresolved issues; learners assume a decision-maker’s role, analyze constraints, and defend a course of action. The method is participatory and often inductive: generalizations emerge from comparing multiple cases or positions rather than from a single lecture.

**Problem-based learning (PBL)**  
A broader family of approaches in which learning is organized around open-ended problems rather than around coverage of topics in isolation. Lineage includes progressive education (experience and authentic problems), long-standing use in medical education (often **tutorial PBL**: small groups, facilitator, iterative self-directed study blocks), and later adoption across disciplines. Core idea: involvement in searching for solutions supports retention and understanding better than transmission alone when the problem is meaningful and well designed.

**Acronym disambiguation:** **PBL** may mean (1) problem-based **learning** (the broad family above); (2) problem-based **inquiry** (the named IMA Chapter 11 model below); or (3) the **PBL inquiry chart** (IMA artifact for questions, hypotheses, sources, and solutions). Use context to tell which sense applies.

**Problem-based inquiry (IMA structured model)**  
One explicit instructional design maps problem-based work onto a five-phase learning cycle (engage, explore, explain, elaborate, evaluate) and uses an inquiry chart to track questions, hypotheses, sources, observations, and emerging solutions. This variant is distinct from other inquiry formats (for example, teacher-mediated yes/no questioning or web-quest structures) even though all are “problem-centered.” Pattern B below describes this school-based unit design, not a full enactment guide for medical tutorial PBL.

**IMA “case studies” (Chapters 13–15)**  
Backward-design unit exemplars that integrate multiple instructional models (direct instruction, concept development, inquiry, and others). They illustrate how to plan a coherent unit; they are **not** a dedicated chapter on professional case method or case discussion protocol.

**Case-based learning (CBL)**  
In health professions and some other fields, “case-based learning” often denotes guided work with clinical or professional vignettes, sometimes more structured and teacher-led than classic PBL. Terminology overlaps across programs; the important distinction is how much the teacher structures the case, resources, and discussion versus how much learners define their own learning path.

**Names that are often confused**

| Term | Typical emphasis | Common confusion |
|------|------------------|------------------|
| Case-based teaching | Narrative, roles, discussion, judgment | Treated as “any example at the start of a lecture” without decision pressure |
| Problem-based learning | Problem drives the unit; need-to-know | Called “PBL” when the task is actually a short word problem with one answer |
| Project-based learning | Extended product over time | Long projects without a central problem or case |
| Discovery learning | Learner finds a principle | Unguided exploration without cases, resources, or accountability |
| Inquiry (Suchman-style) | Puzzling event; constrained questioning | Same as full PBL units (shorter, more teacher-controlled) |
| WebQuest / resource quests | Curated sources + open problem | Technology plan without authentic problem or synthesis |

## When This Model Is Useful

Use case-based or problem-based teaching when:

- **Relevance is weak.** Learners ask why the content matters or tune out abstract exposition.
- **Principles are easier to grasp in context.** The idea makes more sense as a tool for resolving a situation than as a definition on a slide.
- **Reasoning must precede labeling.** Learners need to diagnose, decide, predict, or justify before they can name the underlying concept.
- **Transfer depends on judgment.** The goal includes applying ideas under constraints, incomplete information, or competing values—not only executing a procedure.
- **Engagement requires stakes.** A compact scenario with a real decision, tradeoff, or explanation creates purposeful effort.
- **Disciplinary practice matters.** The field itself (medicine, law, engineering, policy, ethics) routinely works from cases and problems.

Strong fits include ethics dilemmas, policy choices, design constraints, historical interpretation, data interpretation in context, troubleshooting, client scenarios, and any standard that can be reframed as “What problem does this knowledge solve?”

---

## When This Model Is a Poor Fit

Avoid or postpone case/problem-first teaching when:

- **The learner needs a fast procedural fix.** A short, direct demonstration or worked example will resolve the immediate barrier.
- **The scenario adds noise.** Complexity does not serve the target concept; it only increases cognitive load.
- **Background knowledge is insufficient.** Learners cannot engage with the case without vocabulary, representations, or facts they do not yet have.
- **The “case” is decorative.** A thin story at the start of a lecture, with no learner decision or return to the situation, is not case-based teaching.
- **Time and resources cannot support inquiry.** Open problems without access to information, discussion, or feedback collapse into guessing.
- **Safety or accuracy requires direct instruction first.** Some content (conventions, hazardous procedures, foundational definitions) should be established before exploratory problem work.

## Shared Design Principles

Across case-based and problem-based designs, several principles recur in authoritative descriptions:

1. **Authentic or realistic context.** Situations resemble what practitioners face: messy details, stakeholders, constraints, and incomplete information.
2. **Ill-structured problems.** The task is not solved by a single formula; new information may change the problem; multiple plausible responses may exist.
3. **Learners as problem solvers; teachers as facilitators.** The teacher coaches, questions, and monitors—not solely transmits answers.
4. **Need-to-know drives learning.** Gaps exposed by the case or problem motivate concept introduction, reading, and practice.
5. **Articulated thinking.** Learners explain reasoning, cite evidence, and compare solutions.
6. **Assessment aligned to process and product.** Evaluation includes how learners investigated, not only a final label or numeric answer.
7. **Return to the context.** After new ideas are introduced, learners apply them back to the original case or problem.

Problem-based designs also stress that knowledge is actively constructed by the learner, that integration across disciplines is possible, and that intrinsic motivation can replace answer-seeking when the problem feels worth solving.

## Two Enactment Patterns

### Pattern A: Case-based discussion

Suited to shorter cycles and rich narrative.

**Typical flow**

1. **Prepare the case.** Select or write a situation with enough detail for analysis but without revealing “the answer” upfront. Identify the decision or question learners must address.
2. **Individual preparation.** Learners read or hear the case and note facts, uncertainties, and options (often before class).
3. **Small-group analysis.** Groups compare interpretations, test assumptions, and draft a position.
4. **Whole-group discussion.** The teacher facilitates: clarifies facts, probes reasoning, surfaces alternatives, avoids premature closure.
5. **Concept extraction.** After debate, the teacher helps learners name principles, frameworks, or content that clarify the decision.
6. **Transfer check.** A parallel case, changed constraint, or “what if” tests whether learners generalize beyond the original story.

**Teacher moves**

- Ask questions that expose reasoning (“What evidence supports that?” “What would change your view?”).
- Keep facilitation balanced so multiple voices contribute.
- Reframe confusion as analysis of missing information, not as failure to guess the teacher’s answer.
- Summarize tensions and link them to disciplinary ideas after learners have struggled productively.

**Learner moves**

- Distinguish facts from assumptions.
- Take a defensible position with reasons.
- Listen and revise in response to evidence and peer argument.
- Connect the case outcome to broader rules or concepts.

**Readiness to advance**

- Learners can state the decision and tradeoffs in their own words.
- Arguments cite case details, not only prior opinion.
- At least two plausible lines of reasoning have been examined.
- Learners can apply the extracted principle to a variant scenario.

---

### Pattern B: Problem-based inquiry unit

Suited to multi-lesson arcs tied to curriculum objectives.

This pattern aligns with a five-phase cycle and an inquiry chart that tracks the problem statement, sub-questions, hypotheses, knowledge sources, observations, and emerging solutions.

**Phase 1 — Engage with a problem**

- Present an open, researchable problem linked to standards and prior knowledge.
- Spark curiosity through short activities or discussion.
- Co-develop sub-questions learners must answer.
- Introduce the inquiry chart as a shared workspace.

*Readiness:* Learners can restate the problem, see why it matters, and propose initial questions—not only wait for assigned tasks.

**Phase 2 — Explore the problem**

- Learners propose hypotheses and plan how to gather information.
- The teacher supplies activities, resources, and feedback on key concepts embedded in the problem.
- Learners record sources and evidence on the chart; they may run preliminary investigations.

*Readiness:* Sub-questions are clear; hypotheses are testable; learners are collecting and updating the chart, not only brainstorming without evidence.

**Phase 3 — Explain and share information**

- Learners synthesize solutions from multiple sources, noting agreement and conflict across sources.
- Explanations reference specific evidence; the chart supports accountable discussion.

*Readiness:* Proposed solutions are justified with cited information; learners can explain discrepancies between sources.

**Phase 4 — Elaborate and take action**

- Learners extend solutions to real-world implications: recommendations, presentations, letters, designs, or community-facing products when appropriate.
- The teacher presses on feasibility, conflicts, and use of new knowledge.

*Readiness:* Action plans are concrete; learners can defend choices under challenge from peers or the teacher.

**Phase 5 — Evaluate the process**

- Assess content objectives, reasoning, collaboration, and inquiry skills.
- Debrief what worked in the process and what to improve next time.

*Readiness:* Learners can reflect on both product and process; assessment data match stated unit objectives.

## Compact “Teach Through the Case” Cycle

When time is limited, a single session can still use context-first teaching without a full unit arc:

1. Present a **compact case or problem** (one paragraph to one page).
2. Ask what **decision, diagnosis, or explanation** is required.
3. Let learners attempt reasoning with what they know; surface gaps.
4. Introduce the **concept or procedure as the tool** that resolves the gap—not as front-loaded lecture.
5. **Return to the case** with the new tool and revise the answer.
6. **Check transfer** with a near variant (changed number, stakeholder, or constraint).

This cycle is the minimal viable form of the model and is appropriate when engagement or meaning—not extended research—is the main barrier.

## Designing Strong Cases and Problems

**Cases**

- Include concrete details, roles, and constraints.
- End with a genuine decision or interpretive question.
- Leave intentional gaps; not every fact should be supplied upfront.
- Align with objectives: the case should require the knowledge you intend to teach.

**Problems**

- Frame as ill-structured: criteria for success may be multiple or debatable.
- Anchor in curriculum by asking what real-world question the standard answers.
- Scale scope to time, reading level, and resource access.
- For younger learners or novices, provide worked examples or structured sub-steps before expecting full open inquiry.

**Illustrative problem framings** (patterns teachers can adapt; not a discipline checklist)

- Organizational or civic dilemma: What should stakeholders recommend when goals conflict and data are incomplete?
- Interpretation under constraint: Which account of an event or text best fits the evidence when sources disagree?
- Design under limits: What solution meets stated criteria when materials, budget, or time are fixed?
- Quantitative judgment: Which model or representation best supports a defensible comparison or forecast for this dataset?
- Performative or aesthetic choice: Which technique or revision best serves the creator’s stated intent for this audience?

## Evidence of Learning Within the Model

Look for learning at three levels:

**Within the case/problem**

- Reasoning uses details from the situation.
- Learners revise positions when new information appears.
- Competing solutions are compared with explicit criteria.

**Conceptual**

- Learners name principles, definitions, or procedures that were motivated by the case.
- They explain *why* the tool fits the situation, not only *what* the tool is called.

**Transfer**

- Success on a novel case or changed constraint.
- Ability to set up a new sub-question or hypothesis without step-by-step prompting (in longer PBL units).

Formative checks include chart updates, discussion contributions, drafts of recommendations, peer critique, short written reflections, and targeted knowledge checks when foundational facts are prerequisites for inquiry.

---

## Signs of Strong Implementation

- The case or problem comes **before** the principle, not after as an optional illustration.
- Learners do substantive thinking **before** the teacher delivers the “answer.”
- The teacher **facilitates** investigation and argument rather than validating guesses.
- Resources and scaffolding match **readiness** (including worked examples when novices need them).
- Discussion or charts show **evidence**, not only opinions.
- Instruction **returns** to the original context after new content is introduced.
- Assessment matches **process and product** (reasoning, collaboration, and outcomes).

---

## Signs of Shallow Implementation

- A story at the beginning of a lecture with no learner decision or revision.
- One obvious “right answer” disguised as open inquiry.
- Unguided group work without roles, milestones, or accountability.
- Endless brainstorming without moving to evidence, hypotheses, or solutions.
- The teacher rescues too early, converting inquiry into a lecture with a decorative prompt.
- Cases that require specialized knowledge learners were never given access to learn.
- No debrief linking the activity to transferable concepts.

## Common Misuses and Category Confusion

| Misuse | Why it fails |
|--------|----------------|
| “Case” = any word problem | Lacks narrative, roles, and judgment; may be well suited to worked examples instead |
| PBL without resources | Learners guess; frustration replaces inquiry |
| Case discussion as debate performance | Winners on rhetoric, losers on evidence; no concept extraction |
| Skipping individual preparation (in case method) | Discussion dominated by a few prepared voices |
| Treating facilitator role as passivity | Weak monitoring, unclear objectives, no synthesis |
| Confusing PBL with project-based learning | Products without a driving problem or need-to-know |
| Confusing with pure discovery | No scaffolding, no accountability, no return to principles |
| Using complexity to impress | Cognitive load blocks the target learning |

**Versus nearby models**

- **Direct instruction:** Use when learners lack prerequisites or need precise, efficient explanation; case/PBL comes after or with embedded mini-explanations when gaps appear.
- **Worked example with fading:** Use when the barrier is starting a multi-step procedure, not relevance or judgment.
- **Inquiry / predict–check–explain:** Use when a short, testable prediction can expose a misconception; case/PBL when context, stakes, and extended reasoning matter.
- **Socratic questioning:** Use to surface thinking; case/PBL supply the situation that questioning analyzes.
- **Concept attainment:** Use when the goal is category boundaries from examples; case/PBL when the goal is reasoning in realistic settings.
- **Project-based learning:** Extended creation over time; combine with PBL when the project must solve a defined problem, not only produce an artifact.

---

## Integrating With Other Instructional Models

Comprehensive unit planning often **combines** models rather than replacing them with a single case or problem arc: vocabulary front-loading for precision, direct instruction for new procedures, concept development for relationships, cooperative structures for group work, Socratic seminar for text, and inquiry for open problems. In *Instruction: A Models Approach*, Chapters 13–15 show this integration through full unit exemplars; the case or problem provides **integration**—a reason to use tools in sequence—not a license to open a lecture with a thin story and call it case method.

Backward design still applies: objectives and assessments are chosen first; the case or problem is selected because it requires those outcomes. Model choice should follow the learning goal, not the reverse.

---

## Practical Planning Checklist

Before teaching:

- [ ] What decision or explanation must learners produce?
- [ ] What prior knowledge is required—and how will gaps be handled?
- [ ] What resources (text, data, experts, simulations) are available?
- [ ] How will small-group and whole-group time be structured?
- [ ] What artifact or position will show interim progress?
- [ ] When will concepts be introduced relative to struggle?
- [ ] How will learners return to the case after instruction?
- [ ] What variant will test transfer?

During teaching:

- Monitor chart or discussion quality, not only participation.
- Delay closure until reasoning is visible; then synthesize.
- Differentiate process (interviews, reading, field observation) when possible.

After teaching:

- Debrief process and content.
- Note which case details carried learning and which were noise for redesign.
