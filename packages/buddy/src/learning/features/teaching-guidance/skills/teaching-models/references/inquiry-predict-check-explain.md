# Inquiry / Predict-Check-Explain

An operational handbook for inquiry teaching organized around a short cycle: learners commit to a prediction and its reasoning, confront evidence that confirms or challenges that prediction, and rebuild an explanation that accounts for the mismatch. The cycle makes prior thinking visible, creates productive surprise when predictions fail, and turns revision into the main learning event rather than passive reception of a correct answer.

This handbook treats **predict-check-explain** as a compact enactment pattern that appears inside several established inquiry traditions—not as a separate trademarked program or a standalone chapter title in *Instruction: A Models Approach* or *Models of Teaching*. In the teaching-models crosswalk (`resources/docs/artifacts/teaching-models.md`), it names one enactment pattern for **inquiry-based learning** (IMA Chapter 11 and related MOT inquiry material). It draws especially on **Suchman-style inquiry** (structured questioning toward hypotheses), **predict-observe-explain** in science education, and the hypothesis-testing logic common to problem-centered and scientific inquiry.

---

## What Problem This Model Solves

Schooling often rewards correct answers without surfacing the ideas behind them. Learners can repeat explanations they do not believe, or hold stable misconceptions that never meet contradicting evidence. Telling alone frequently fails because the learner’s existing model filters what they hear.

Predict-check-explain addresses a specific instructional problem: **the learner has a plausible but wrong mental model, or an untested assumption, that will not change until it meets a clear test.** The model creates conditions where:

- Prior thinking must be stated before the outcome is known
- Observation or evidence is interpreted against that prior commitment
- Cognitive conflict between prediction and result motivates explanation revision
- The teacher learns what learners actually think before choosing the next move

The approach fits Bruner’s argument that material the learner works out is more available to memory than material only transmitted, and that inquiry practice teaches problem posing—not only answer retrieval. It also aligns with problem-based learning’s emphasis on ill-structured problems, learner construction of knowledge, and accountability to standards-aligned objectives (as framed in IMA’s problem-based inquiry section).

## Terminology And Lineage

**Inquiry-based learning** is a broad family. In *Instruction: A Models Approach*, inquiry appears as multiple models: Suchman inquiry (teacher as constrained information source), WebQuest (learner gathers resources), and problem-based inquiry tied to the 5E learning cycle. This reference focuses on the **short, teacher-facilitated cycle** useful when a single puzzling event or question can be resolved in one or a few lessons—not on extended unit-length WebQuests or full problem-based units unless noted.

**Suchman inquiry model** (Richard Suchman, 1961; *Instruction: A Models Approach* Ch. 11 cites Suchman, 1962): Learners investigate a discrepant or puzzling situation through yes/no questions, develop and test a hypothesis, explain accepted hypotheses with generalizable rules, analyze the inquiry process, and evaluate transfer. The teacher controls process and information access but not the hypothesis learners construct.

**Predict-observe-explain (POE)** (Richard White and Richard Gunstone, 1992): Learners predict an event and justify the prediction, observe what actually happens (often via demonstration or hands-on activity), then amend explanations to reconcile prediction and observation. POE was developed to probe understanding and surface misconceptions in science; similar sequences appear in mathematics (e.g., statistics).

**Predict-check-explain** (this handbook’s label): **Check** generalizes **observe** to any evidence step—demonstration, measurement, simulation, thought experiment, historical source, counterexample, or structured data gathering (including Suchman-style yes/no testing of a stated hypothesis). The logic is the same: a committed prediction meets a result that learners must account for.

**Inquiry Training Model** (*Models of Teaching*, Joyce, Weil & Calhoun): Begins with puzzling situations and trains learners to ask questions and evaluate responses. Full chapter detail is limited in preview sources; treat it as a related skills-building line, not as the only definition of inquiry.

**Scientific inquiry** (*Models of Teaching*): Emphasizes investigations learners design and conduct, including virtual labs and extended study. Predict-check-explain is often a **micro-cycle within** larger scientific inquiry, not a substitute for full investigation design.

**Discrepant event / puzzling situation**: An experience or problem that violates everyday expectation—essential for productive prediction failure.

**Do not confuse with:**

| Nearby practice | Distinction |
|-----------------|-------------|
| **Direct instruction** | Transmits a target model efficiently when learners lack baseline knowledge; predict-check-explain assumes enough context to make a meaningful prediction |
| **Socratic / diagnostic questioning** | Primarily uncovers reasoning through questions; predict-check-explain adds an explicit evidence confrontation and explanation revision |
| **Concept attainment** | Builds categories from sorted examples; predict-check-explain tests causal or explanatory models against events |
| **Undirected exploration (no prediction commitment)** | Open activity without a stated prediction or test often yields passive watching; this row is not Bruner’s “discovery learning” writ large—prediction forces engagement before the outcome |
| **Full problem-based learning units** | Anchors weeks of work on ill-structured problems; predict-check-explain is a tactical cycle inside or beside such units |
| **WebQuest** | Learner-driven resource quest with product; less dependent on a single prediction-evidence mismatch |
| **5E lesson cycle** | Engagement–exploration–explanation–elaboration–evaluation spans a lesson or unit; predict-check-explain maps most closely to engage → explore (check) → explain, but is narrower and more explicit about prior commitment |

## When The Model Is Useful

Use predict-check-explain when:

- Learners hold a **stable, tempting misconception** that explanation alone has not displaced
- A **small, fast test** can make the gap visible (demonstration, numeric case, map puzzle, thought experiment, short data set)
- **Discovery through evidence** is likely to be more durable than telling, because the learner must reconcile conflict
- The topic allows a **clear prediction** (“what will happen,” “which is larger,” “what caused X”) with observable or verifiable check
- You need **diagnostic information** about learner theories before choosing reteaching, direct instruction, or practice
- Learners are capable of **stating a prediction with some reasoning**, even if the reasoning is wrong
- Scientific, mathematical, historical, or literary puzzles fit **hypothesis generation and testing** (not only laboratory science)

Suchman’s problems illustrate range: why condensation appears on a glass, why hat makers in a historical period showed neurological symptoms, why two identical plants diverge in growth, why map projections distort land masses. POE fits demonstrations with immediate, observable outcomes—often physical science or statistics contexts.

---

## When The Model Is A Poor Fit

Avoid or postpone predict-check-explain when:

- Learners are **too lost to predict meaningfully**—they lack vocabulary, context, or a mental model to commit to; teach prerequisites or use an advance organizer first
- The activity would become a **guessing game** without clear purpose, success criteria, or debrief
- **Direct explanation is faster and sufficient** because there is no consequential misconception and no need for experiential conflict
- There is **no genuine check** available—only the teacher’s authority that “the answer is X”
- Predictions cannot be **distinguished** (everyone says the same vague thing) and the check does not discriminate theories
- Safety, ethics, or time prohibit the demonstration or investigation that would test predictions
- The “surprise” is **contrived or trivial**, so revision feels like a trick rather than insight
- You need **category boundaries** more than causal models (concept attainment may fit better)
- You need **fluency** after understanding is sound (mastery learning loop fits better)

**Readiness signal:** If most learners cannot complete “I predict ___ because ___,” they are not ready for the full cycle. Scaffold the problem statement, vocabulary, or a simpler sub-question first.

## Structure Of The Cycle

The model has five phases. **POE** is predict-first: commit before any observation. **Suchman** inquiry may enter the check phase through shared yes/no data gathering before a class hypothesis is fixed—do not treat the five-phase list as one universal protocol for every tradition. In practice, phases 2–4 may repeat when the first hypothesis fails.

### Phase 1: Set up a puzzling problem

**Purpose:** Anchor inquiry in a situation that matters, connects to prior knowledge, and can support a testable prediction.

**Teacher moves**

- Select a **genuinely puzzling** problem aligned to important content—not a riddle with arbitrary rules
- Prefer **discrepant events** or ill-structured questions where everyday intuition misleads
- Research the problem thoroughly; prepare a **data sheet** or evidence plan (facts, sources, demonstration steps, safety notes)
- Decide how much context to give initially and what scaffolds exist if questioning stalls
- State the problem clearly; for Suchman-style work, post **process rules** (e.g., yes/no questions only, when small-group talk is allowed)
- Connect to standards or disciplinary questions without reducing the problem to a single textbook paragraph

**Learner moves**

- Listen or read the problem; clarify unfamiliar terms
- Activate prior knowledge about similar situations
- Accept that the goal is a **reasonable, evidence-backed explanation**, not only a right answer on first try

**Readiness to advance**

- Learners can restate what they are trying to figure out
- The problem is specific enough that predictions could differ among learners

**Shallow setup warning:** Problems with one obvious answer, or where the teacher already signaled the conclusion, remove the need for genuine prediction.

---

### Phase 2: Predict (with reasoning)

**Purpose:** Freeze prior thinking before evidence appears; make theories visible for instruction.

**Teacher moves**

- Require **individual commitment** before the check—writing predictions reduces revision after the fact to match peers
- Ask for **both** what learners expect and **why** (mechanism, cause, rule, or analogy)
- Avoid confirming or correcting predictions at this stage
- Use predictions to **group learners** by similar theories for targeted discussion later
- For Suchman inquiry, encourage questions framed as testable propositions (“If X were true, would Y happen?”) rather than open requests for the answer

**Tradition note (Suchman vs POE):** Classic **POE** requires each learner to commit in writing **before** any observation. **Suchman** inquiry often begins with shared **yes/no data gathering**; a class hypothesis may emerge mid-lesson rather than only as an upfront written prediction. Do not force POE’s predict-first protocol onto a Suchman lesson whose first evidence step is structured questioning.

**Learner moves**

- State a concrete prediction
- Articulate reasoning, even if tentative
- Recognize that differing predictions among classmates are useful data

**Readiness to advance**

- Each learner (or group) has a recorded prediction and reason, not only “I don’t know”

**Checks for understanding (diagnostic, not evaluative)**

- “What exactly do you expect to see or conclude?”
- “What would have to be true in the world for your prediction to hold?”

**Shallow prediction warning:** Vague predictions (“something will happen”) or reasons copied from authority without personal sense-making weaken the cycle.

---

### Phase 3: Check (observe, test, or gather evidence)

**Purpose:** Provide shared evidence that learners must interpret against their predictions.

**Teacher moves**

- Run the demonstration, investigation, data collection, or structured questioning **after** predictions are locked in
- For POE-style work: ensure observations are **focused and recorded**; allow time to watch or measure carefully
- For Suchman-style work: respond to yes/no questions without rephrasing for learners; record accumulated facts publicly; allow productive frustration before releasing extra information
- When a learner offers a hypothesis, **shift questioning** to test that hypothesis exclusively until it is accepted, revised, or rejected
- Direct learners to additional sources or experiments when the classroom check is insufficient
- Do not let the check step collapse into **telling the answer** before learners engage with conflict
- In **Suchman** lessons, treat early yes/no questioning as the check phase even when no single written prediction preceded it; once a hypothesis is proposed, test it exclusively before broadening again
- In **POE** lessons, run observation only after predictions are recorded—reversing that order destroys diagnostic value

**Learner moves**

- Observe or gather data honestly; distinguish observation from interpretation
- In POE-style work, **record what was observed** (brief notes, sketches, measurements) before debating why—standard practice in predict-observe-explain protocols
- Compare results to prediction; note **match, partial match, or conflict**
- In Suchman inquiry, refine questions for clarity and test one hypothesis at a time

**Readiness to advance**

- Everyone has access to the same core evidence (or understands which evidence bears on the prediction)
- Learners can state whether their prediction was supported, refuted, or incomplete

**Shallow check warning:** Learners watch passively without recording observations; the teacher narrates the conclusion while learners still hold the old model.

---

### Phase 4: Explain (reconcile and rebuild)

**Purpose:** Turn mismatch into a better explanatory model; generalize where appropriate.

**Teacher moves**

- Prompt reconciliation: “How does what happened compare to what you predicted?”
- Require **amended explanations** that account for observations—not silent replacement with the teacher’s words
- Facilitate discussion so learners hear **alternative predictions and reasons**
- Ask learners to **state rules or principles** implied by the accepted explanation (Suchman step: rules associated with hypothesis)
- Probe **generalization**: Does the rule hold in a new case? What would falsify it?
- If explanations remain inadequate, return to evidence gathering rather than lecturing through the entire solution without learner reconstruction
- Normalize error-and-revision as part of disciplined inquiry

**Learner moves**

- Identify specific points where prediction and evidence diverged
- Revise causal or conceptual explanations in their own words
- Evaluate peers’ explanations critically but respectfully
- Articulate what they would predict in a **near transfer** case

**Readiness to advance**

- Learners can explain **why** the new account fits evidence better than the old prediction
- At least one generalizable rule or principle is stated and tested against a follow-up case

**Checks for understanding**

- “Given [new scenario], what do you now predict, and why?”
- “What would you have had to assume for your original prediction to be right—and why did that assumption fail?”

**Shallow explain warning:** Learners verbally agree with the teacher’s explanation but cannot apply the idea to a novel problem; the class jumps to vocabulary labels without mechanism.

---

### Phase 5: Consolidate process and transfer

**Purpose:** Strengthen retention, inquiry skills, and discipline-specific habits—not only the answer to one puzzle.

**Teacher moves**

- **Analyze the process** (Suchman): What questions worked? What dead ends occurred? How could inquiry be more efficient next time?
- Assign **evaluation** tasks: new puzzling situations, accountability for hypothesis quality, or comparison of competing viable hypotheses
- Connect the resolved problem to **broader disciplinary ideas** and later lessons
- Plan **transfer** problems that differ in surface features but test the same principle
- For extended inquiry units, link the micro-cycle to charts or logs that track hypotheses, sources, and solutions over time

**Learner moves**

- Reflect on personal theory change
- Practice generating questions and predictions in new contexts
- Take responsibility for continued questioning beyond the lesson

**Readiness to conclude**

- Learners demonstrate transfer on at least one new item, or can teach the principle to a peer with accurate constraints

## Teacher And Learner Roles Across The Cycle

| Phase | Teacher role | Learner role |
|-------|--------------|--------------|
| Setup | Problem designer, process steward, safety and resource manager | Problem poser in later lessons; engaged investigator now |
| Predict | Diagnostician; withholds outcome | Theory builder; commits ideas |
| Check | Evidence provider or gatekeeper of yes/no data; resists premature telling | Observer, tester, data recorder |
| Explain | Facilitator of reconciliation; challenges generalizations | Model reviser; rule articulator |
| Consolidate | Coach of inquiry metacognition | Transfer agent; questioner |

In problem-based inquiry at unit scale, the teacher acts as **tutor and coach** rather than sole knowledge source; in Suchman-style lessons, the teacher may be the **primary data source** but still must not own the hypothesis. In POE demonstrations, the teacher orchestrates the event but learners own explanation revision.

---

## Readiness Checks Between Phases

| Transition | Evidence to proceed |
|------------|---------------------|
| Setup → Predict | Problem understood; learners can propose competing outcomes |
| Predict → Check | Predictions and reasons recorded; no peeking at outcome |
| Check → Explain | Shared evidence established; conflicts identified |
| Explain → Consolidate | Revised explanation fits evidence; at least one rule stated |
| Consolidate → Close | Transfer or process reflection shows learning beyond one answer |

If check and predict align for everyone, either the problem was too easy or predictions were not specific enough—use a harder transfer item rather than skipping explanation.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

- Predictions **differ** across learners before the check, surfacing multiple models
- Learners cite **evidence** when revising explanations, not only teacher authority
- Wrong predictions are treated as **resources**, not shameful failures
- The check is **legitimate**—learners could trust the outcome if they repeated it
- Explanation phase produces **mechanisms**, not only labels
- Teachers adjust **next instruction** based on what predictions revealed
- Process debrief improves **question quality** over time in Suchman-style work
- Transfer tasks show **changed predictions** on structurally similar problems

---

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

- Predictions happen **after** the demonstration or answer is known
- “Check” is only the teacher saying the correct answer
- Learners change written predictions to match observations **without** explaining why
- Activity feels like a **magic trick** with no disciplinary payoff
- Only one learner talks; others adopt that explanation passively
- Inquiry rules are posted but the teacher **rephrases every question** or supplies hypotheses
- No follow-up—learners leave with surprise but without a **stable revised model**
- Puzzle chosen for entertainment, **misaligned** to learning goals

## Common Misuses And Category Confusion

**Using inquiry when telling would work.** If learners lack basic vocabulary or representations, a short direct explanation plus guided practice may precede or replace the cycle.

**Puzzle without discipline.** Games that surprise but do not map to a principle learners will reuse waste time and breed cynicism.

**Fake predictions.** Calling for predictions after learners have already seen the outcome destroys diagnostic value and careful observation.

**Endless questioning without hypothesis focus.** Suchman inquiry requires shifting to **test one hypothesis** once proposed; otherwise data gathering stays scattered.

**Hypothesis ownership by the teacher.** When the teacher supplies the “right” hypothesis early, learners practice verification theater, not inquiry.

**Treating check as observe-only in non-observable domains.** In history or ethics, “check” may mean examining sources, documents, or logical consequences—not a lab demo. The cycle still applies if evidence can challenge a prediction.

**Confusing predict-check-explain with full PBL or WebQuest.** Extended projects need planning charts, resource literacy, and product assessment; the micro-cycle alone does not carry a multi-week unit.

**Skipping generalization.** Accepting a one-off answer (“mercury poisoned hatters”) without extracting a transferable rule limits retention and transfer.

**Overusing the model.** Not every lesson needs discrepant events; overuse trains learners to expect tricks rather than disciplined reasoning.

## Relationships To Nearby Models

**Socratic / diagnostic questioning** uncovers assumptions through dialogue; add predict-check-explain when you are ready to **confront** those assumptions with a structured result and require explicit revision.

**Direct instruction** efficiently builds components learners need before they can predict; use it for prerequisites, then return to inquiry for misconception repair.

**Concept attainment** clarifies what belongs in a category; predict-check-explain tests **why or how** something happens in a situation.

**Worked example with fading** supports procedural start-up when learners cannot begin; use when the barrier is **performance**, not a wrong explanatory model.

**Advance organizer** helps when overload comes from missing structure, not from a wrong causal belief.

**Case-based / problem-based teaching** embeds principles in scenarios over longer arcs; predict-check-explain is a **move within** a case when a decisive prediction moment arises.

**Mastery learning loop** fits after the explanatory model is sound and learners need **reliable performance** with feedback and variation.

**Cause-and-effect and concept development models** (IMA) also use hypothesis generation; predict-check-explain is the **shortest** public version of that logic tied to a single event.

---

## Variations Worth Knowing

**Suchman seven-step inquiry** (select problem → introduce process → gather data → develop and test hypothesis → explain hypothesis and rules → analyze process → evaluate) embeds predict-check-explain inside yes/no data gathering and explicit rule extraction. The cycle is **not** POE order: learners typically **gather data before** a class hypothesis is fixed and tested. Best when the teacher can answer constrained questions from prepared research.

**POE three-step cycle** (predict → observe → explain) is the streamlined science demonstration form. **Individual prediction must precede observation**; written commitment before the demo is central. Best when immediate observation is possible and individual written commitment matters.

**Problem-based inquiry with 5E and inquiry charts** scales inquiry across days: engage with problem, explore with organized questions and sources, explain solutions, elaborate through action, evaluate process. Predict-check-explain often appears in **engage** and early **explore** before charts fill with multi-source answers.

**Thought experiments and counterexamples** substitute for lab demos when ethical, spatial, or historical reasoning is at stake—provided learners still commit before the “check” reveals consequences of their assumptions.

## Design Checklist For A Single Cycle

1. Identify the **misconception or untested assumption** you need to surface.
2. Choose a **puzzling situation** where intuition misfires but discipline tools explain the outcome.
3. Prepare **evidence** (demo, data, sources, yes/no fact sheet) and safety constraints.
4. Write a **prompt** that requires a specific prediction plus reason.
5. Plan **recording** (individual papers, public chart, hypothesis board).
6. Run the check **without** revealing the answer in advance.
7. Facilitate **reconciliation** before naming the formal principle.
8. State a **rule** and test it on a near neighbor problem.
9. Debrief **process** and assign one transfer item.
