# Analogy and Model Bridging

Analogy and model bridging use a familiar **source** situation to make a less familiar **target** idea intelligible. The instructional move is not decorative comparison. It is deliberate alignment of relations—causes, roles, constraints, and outcomes—so learners can see how the target works before they try to reason about it in target terms alone.

The approach appears in creative-problem-solving traditions (notably synectics), in cognitive research on structure mapping and analogical transfer, and in everyday classroom practice under names such as instructional analogy, bridging analogy, and mental model building. What unifies these lines of work is a shared problem: many important ideas are abstract, invisible, counterintuitive, or densely relational, while learners already possess concrete experiences that can scaffold understanding if the match is chosen and bounded carefully.

In the teaching-models library crosswalk, the textbook-named model is **Synectics** (*Instruction: A Models Approach*, Chapter 12; *Models of Teaching*, information-processing family). This reference title—**analogy / model bridging**—names the broader analogical practice those sources extend: relational mapping, explicit limits, target re-description, and target-only checks, as described both in synectics enactments and in structure-mapping research.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

Learners often stall when:

- the target concept cannot be directly perceived (submicroscopic processes, large-scale systems, abstract structures);
- verbal definitions list features without showing how parts interact;
- prior knowledge is fragmentary but not yet organized into a workable model;
- a tempting informal model (a "folk" explanation) blocks uptake of the formal one.

Model bridging reduces initial cognitive distance. A well-chosen source gives learners something to align, question, and revise. The bridge is temporary scaffolding: the instructional goal is target understanding that can stand without the source.

## Terminology and Lineage

**Source and target.** In analogical reasoning research, knowledge is often mapped from a **base** or **source** domain into a **target** domain. In classrooms, the source is usually more familiar, concrete, or imageable; the target is the new learning focus.

**Relational vs. surface match.** Strong instructional analogies align **relations** (what causes what, what enables what, what role each part plays), not mere appearance. Cooks do not look like ribosomes; both may **produce** outputs within a larger **system** governed by **control** relations.

**Model bridging.** This phrase emphasizes building or repairing a mental model of the target by importing relational structure from a source, then **releasing** the bridge once the target model is stable.

**Metaphor and analogy.** Metaphor highlights figurative language and perspective shift; analogy emphasizes systematic correspondence and inference. Instruction often combines both, but the pedagogical obligation is the same: make correspondences explicit and mark where the comparison stops.

**Synectics.** William Gordon's synectics process (developed for industry and adapted in schools) uses structured analogy work—direct comparisons, personal "becoming" the analogy, and tension-filled pairings—to deepen insight and creative problem solving. Instructional treatments in *Instruction: A Models Approach* describe three classroom versions: making the familiar strange, making the strange familiar, and extended analogy excursions for open problems. *Models of Teaching* treats synectics as an information-processing model for metaphoric and divergent thinking.

**Structure-mapping theory.** Dedre Gentner's framework explains why instructional analogies succeed when shared **systems of relations** align, and why they fail when learners fixate on object resemblance or import irrelevant source features.

These traditions overlap but are not identical. A short bridge into a new concept may need only mapping, limits, and a target-only check. A synectics lesson may spend much longer generating, voting on, and recombining analogies to shift how learners see a topic.

## When the Model Is Useful

- The target is abstract, invisible, or hard to visualize.
- Learners have relevant everyday or prior knowledge that could support relational alignment.
- The concept is organized around interactions, flows, controls, or tradeoffs rather than isolated facts.
- A representation (diagram, story, physical system) can make relationships concrete without replacing formal precision.
- Learners need intuition before notation, or meaning before procedure.
- A faulty informal model needs to be displaced by a better relational story (after the bridge is tested, not assumed).

## When It Is a Poor Fit

- The chosen source shares only surface features with the target.
- The analogy would introduce a **worse** misconception than the one being corrected.
- Learners are already **overextending** an earlier analogy and need constraint, not a new bridge.
- The learning moment demands exact definition, measurement, or proof where intuition would blur necessary distinctions.
- Prerequisites for understanding either the source or the target are missing.
- Cognitive load is already high and adding a second domain would overload working memory without strong scaffolding.
- The teacher cannot state clearly what maps, what does not, and how to check understanding without the source.

## Core Instructional Cycle: Model Bridging

For many lessons, a compact cycle is enough. Longer synectics sequences (below) elaborate the same logic.

### 1. Clarify the target need

State what learners must understand in the target—not only vocabulary, but the relational story (what interacts, what changes, what is constrained). If a misconception is known, name the faulty relation it rests on.

### 2. Select a source with relational fit

Choose a source that learners can grasp and that shares the **causal or functional relations** you want in the target. Prefer sources that are:

- familiar enough to explain fully;
- concrete enough to visualize or act out;
- sparse enough that irrelevant features can be set aside;
- honest enough that limits can be stated without undermining the main mapping.

Mapping should generally proceed from the better-understood source toward the less-understood target.

### 3. Establish correspondences explicitly

Present the source and map element-by-element or relation-by-relation to the target. Use verbal explanation, aligned diagrams, color coding, or comparison prompts so correspondences are visible—not left for learners to guess.

Ask learners to articulate **why** each correspondence holds (functional similarity), not only **what** matches.

### 4. State limits and non-correspondences

Every instructional analogy is partial. Identify features of the source that must **not** transfer. This step prevents entrenched misconceptions (for example, treating a cell as a factory with a single boss when control in cells is distributed and regulated differently).

Limit statements are not optional polish; they are part of correct analogical teaching.

### 5. Return to the target in target terms

Re-describe the phenomenon using target vocabulary and representations. Learners should be able to explain the target **without** leaning on the source language. The bridge should fade once the target model is taking hold.

### 6. Check understanding without the analogy

Use a small task that requires target reasoning: a prediction, explanation, classification, calculation, or application in a new context. Success here is the main signal to move on or reduce support.

## Readiness to Advance or Fade the Bridge

Move toward target-only work when learners can:

- explain target relations without prompting from the source;
- correct overextensions when asked "where does the analogy break?";
- apply the idea in a slightly novel target context;
- distinguish essential target features from source-only features.

Return to mapping or choose a new source when learners:

- answer only in source terms ("because of the boss in the analogy");
- treat superficial similarity as proof;
- show stable errors traceable to the analogy;
- cannot perform the target-only check even with hints.

## Teacher Moves

- Explain the source fully before demanding target inference; do not assume shared "common knowledge."
- Direct attention to **relations** (prepare/produce, enable/prevent, regulate/respond) rather than object look-alikes.
- Use aligned representations and comparative language ("this role in the source corresponds to this role in the target").
- Model limit-setting: "This part of the picture helps; this part does not carry over."
- Fade the bridge deliberately; do not leave the analogy as the permanent explanation.
- For novices under load, reduce extraneous detail in both domains and keep both representations visible when comparing.
- When comparing two target examples (not source-to-target), use the same explicit alignment discipline to highlight shared structure.

## Learner Moves

- Describe correspondences in their own words.
- Justify mappings with relational reasons.
- Propose where the analogy fails.
- Re-explain the target concept using disciplinary terms.
- Attempt a target task that does not mention the source.
- In extended analogy work, generate alternatives and compare which relations they preserve.

## Extended Enactment: Synectics-Informed Sequences

Full synectics lessons are advanced and cognitively demanding, but two variations are especially relevant to model bridging. In Estes and Mintz (*Instruction: A Models Approach*) and Joyce, Weil, and Calhoun (*Models of Teaching*), full synectics enactments are ordinarily **structured group** work—shared idea lists, voting on promising comparisons, and cooperative generation—not only individual metaphor making. A compact source-to-target bridge can run in pairs or whole-class discussion, but fidelity to synectics excursions assumes sustained collaboration.

In *Models of Teaching*, synectics appears in the information-processing family as a model for metaphoric and divergent thinking. Full procedural detail from that book's dedicated chapter is not reproduced here beyond what the local preview permits; enactment guidance below prioritizes IMA Chapter 12 summaries and independent analogy research.

### Making the strange familiar

Used when **new** content must connect to prior knowledge.

Typical flow:

1. Provide essential information about the target topic.
2. Present a teacher-chosen direct analogy to a familiar domain; list relational similarities.
3. Invite personal analogies (how it feels to "be" something in the source) to surface rich descriptors.
4. Form **compressed conflicts**—pairs of descriptors held in metaphoric tension (for example, radiant yet enclosed)—to push beyond literal description.
5. Compare the chosen tension back to the target subject.
6. **Identify differences** between source and target explicitly.
7. Re-examine the target using target language and images from the process.
8. Invite learners to create new analogies farther from the first one to test understanding.

This sequence embeds the core bridging cycle but adds generative comparison and affective perspective-taking before return to the target.

### Making the familiar strange

Used when learners already know a topic but need **new patterns, depth, or reinterpretation**.

Typical flow:

1. Elicit rich descriptions of the familiar target (words, images, features).
2. Create **direct analogies** to an unrelated category (machine, plant, animal, weather) to defamiliarize the topic.
3. Develop **personal analogies** from a chosen comparison.
4. Extract compressed conflicts from those personal descriptions.
5. Build a new direct analogy from the winning conflict.
6. **Reexamine** the original topic through the final analogy and prior images generated along the way.

Here the bridge moves away from the topic and back, loosening fixed views before reconceptualizing the familiar.

### Problem-focused analogy excursions

For open problems (design challenges, policy dilemmas, process improvement), longer sequences add expert information, purge weak obvious solutions, restate the problem from multiple angles, and use Gordon's varied analogy types—**direct**, **personal**, and **symbolic** (and sometimes fantasy in extended excursions)—before forcing analogies back onto the problem and evaluating new solutions. This is closer to creative problem solving than to a quick conceptual bridge, but it shares the same requirement: analogies must eventually be tested against the actual problem constraints.

## Evidence of Learning Within the Model

Formative evidence during bridging includes:

- quality of stated correspondences (relational, not cosmetic);
- learner-generated limits and difference statements;
- ability to use images from the process while still speaking accurately in target terms;
- participation in comparison talk that improves the mapping rather than drifting off-topic.

Summative or exit evidence should emphasize **target competence**: explanations, predictions, and applications without the source analog. Rubrics can note both mapping skill and target accuracy; target accuracy should dominate decisions about whether bridging succeeded.

## Signs of Strong Implementation

- The source is understood before transfer is demanded.
- Correspondences are explicit and relation-centered.
- Limits are taught, not discovered only after errors appear.
- Learners can repair overextensions when prompted.
- The target is revisited in disciplinary language.
- Checks require target reasoning, not analogy recall alone.
- Bridges are faded once evidence supports target understanding.

## Signs of Shallow Implementation

- Decorative comparisons ("it's like a…") with no mapping work.
- Source dominates long after the target should stand alone.
- Surface features drive the comparison (shape, color, nickname).
- No discussion of mismatch or partial fit.
- Analogy replaces definition, evidence, or practice that the topic requires.
- Learners can repeat the analogy story but cannot solve a target problem.
- Multiple competing analogies without alignment discipline.

## Common Misuses and Category Confusion

**Analogy vs. advance organizer.** An advance organizer provides a **structural overview of the target itself** before details. An analogy imports structure from a **different** domain. Organizers map the territory; bridges import a map from elsewhere.

**Analogy vs. inquiry.** Inquiry uses prediction and evidence within the target domain. Analogy imports an external model. Inquiry may follow a bridge when learners test whether the target behaves as the mapped relations predict.

**Analogy vs. concept attainment.** Concept attainment builds categories from examples and non-examples **of the concept itself**. Analogy compares across domains; category boundaries must still be taught in target terms.

**Analogy vs. direct instruction.** Direct instruction explains the target plainly. Bridging is often embedded inside explanation but should not substitute for precise target exposition where precision is required.

**Analogy vs. metaphor alone.** A metaphor can orient attention; bridging requires checkable correspondence, limits, and target-only performance.

**False confidence from familiarity.** A vivid source can feel satisfying while target understanding remains thin. Always require a target-only check.

## Cognitive Constraints Worth Designing For

Analogical alignment draws on working memory, inhibitory control (ignoring irrelevant features), and prior knowledge in both domains. Under load, stress, or weak domain knowledge, learners default to surface similarity. Instruction can mitigate load by:

- simplifying objects in examples used for comparison;
- keeping both analogs visible during comparison;
- labeling comparisons explicitly;
- teaching the source before the target mapping;
- comparing cases that share relational structure when the goal is abstraction within the target domain;
- scaffolding difference identification before independent transfer.

Research on teaching by analogy also stresses **causal relations** as the usual instructional focus: what enables, prevents, produces, or regulates outcomes in both source and target.

## Relationships to Nearby Models

- **Advance organizer:** target-structure first; analogy cross-domain second. Complementary when detail overwhelm is the issue.
- **Worked example with fading:** shows expert target performance; bridging supplies meaning for why steps make sense. Often sequenced: bridge → worked example → fading.
- **Inquiry (predict-check-explain):** can test predictions derived from a bridged model.
- **Socratic / diagnostic questioning:** can surface which part of an analogy a learner overgeneralized.
- **Concept attainment:** clarifies what counts as the target category after bridging gives relational intuition.
- **Case-based / problem-based teaching:** cases situate the target in context; analogies import structure from outside the case when the case alone is insufficiently familiar.
