# Explanatory Drawing

A teaching picture is judged by what it shows, not by how it looks. It should make one idea easier to see than the words alone, be simple enough that the learner could redraw it from memory, and leave nothing on the page that isn't doing a job. This skill covers composition: which frame to draw, where to start, how to lay it out, and what to leave out. The whiteboard skill covers board mechanics and pacing, and each rendering tool covers its own format.

## 1. Start from the question the picture answers

Before drawing, name the idea's shape. Each shape has one natural frame, and each frame has its own coordinate system and starting point (Dan Roam, *The Back of the Napkin*: "for every way of seeing there is one way of showing").

| The idea is about… | Frame | Coordinate system | Start here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who or what: parts, identity, appearance | Portrait | The object itself | The subject in the center: a simple shape with its name. Add only the features that distinguish it. |
| How much or how many | Chart | A quantity axis against items or categories | The axes and units, then the values. Sort or group so the pattern is visible. |
| Where: how things fit together, in real space or in idea space | Map | Positions relative to each other, along any two meaningful directions | The most prominent landmark, then everything else placed relative to it. Proximity and containment should mean something. |
| When: order, stages, duration | Timeline | Time running left to right | The starting event on the left. Distinguish order from duration, mark the turning points, and draw repeating stages as a cycle. |
| How: a process, mechanism, or causal chain | Flow | From action to response | The triggering action. Put verbs in the boxes, label every branch, end every path, and mark loops. |
| Why: several factors acting together | Multi-variable plot | Two or more meaningful axes | The horizontal axis, then the vertical, then place the items and name the regions. Draw one only when it has a point. |

Two combinations come up often: quantity over time (a line across a timeline) and a process over time (stages along a timeline, one lane per actor). When several questions matter, pick the one that carries the explanation as the frame and layer the others lightly onto it. Don't let two frames compete.

Examples:
- the parts of a flower is a portrait;
- population by country is a chart;
- the rooms of a heart and the valves between them is a map;
- the phases of mitosis is a timeline;
- how a bill becomes law is a flow;
- why some cities grow faster than others is a multi-variable plot.

## 2. Set the version before drawing

The same frame can be drawn many ways. Decide deliberately, based on who the learner is and what this moment needs:

- **Simple or elaborate.** For a first encounter, draw the fewest elements that carry the idea, and add detail as understanding grows. Draw the elaborate version when precision, completeness, or a reference is the point. The opposite of simple is elaborate, not complex: a simple picture can hold a deep idea.
- **Qualitative or quantitative.** Show relationships and meaning with sketches; show measured amounts with scales and numbers. Don't imply precision you don't have.
- **Individual or comparison.** To compare, draw the things side by side on the same scale, at the same size, and in the same layout, so the differences stand out and nothing else does. Comparisons work better adjacent in space than stacked in time: put both versions on one picture rather than across turns, tabs, or animation frames. For many cases or stages, a row of small, identical frames (small multiples) lets the eye compare them at a glance.
- **Change or status quo.** To show change, draw before and after in parallel or mark the movement with arrows. When the change itself is the lesson and the learner should drive it, an interactive page may teach better than a still picture.
- **Big picture or detail.** When you zoom into a detail, keep a small version of the whole in view and mark where the detail sits.

## 3. Build it the way a teacher draws at the board

- **Frame first.** Put down the title or question, the axes, zones, or landmark, and any key before the content. The learner then knows what kind of picture is coming and where to look.
- **Then add while you explain.** Add one element or group per idea, in the order you explain them, so each addition answers the question you just raised. For a structure the learner is meeting for the first time, this progressive build is usually worth more than a finished drawing.
- **Complete, but not done.** When constructing part of the picture is the learner's task, leave that part visibly open, such as an empty box, an unlabeled arrow, or a missing stage. Otherwise, finish it.
- **Grow the same picture.** When the next question extends the idea, add to the existing picture instead of starting a new one. A picture that keeps absorbing new cases, like one curve that later explanations are plotted on, becomes a tool the learner can reuse.

## 4. Keep only what explains

- **Make it redrawable.** Aim for a picture the learner could sketch on paper in under a minute. If they can reproduce it, they can reuse the idea.
- **Remove noise.** Use simple shapes, faceless stick figures, a browser drawn as a rectangle with a bar, and short labels. Detail that doesn't carry meaning competes with the part that does. Add realistic detail only when recognizing the appearance is itself the point.
- **Use the smallest effective difference.** Make every visual distinction as subtle as possible while still clear: light grids and guides, thin leader lines, muted secondary elements, and one strong emphasis for the thing that matters. Heavy pointers and borders drown the content they point at.
- **Draw the verbs.** Explanation is about change, mechanism, and cause and effect: what causes what, by what means, at what rate. Make those visible with arrows that read as verbs, before-and-after pairs, and ordered stages, rather than drawing only the nouns.
- **Start from what the learner knows.** Begin with a compact, familiar model and stretch it. An idealized case (a frictionless slope, a two-person trade, a single cell) teaches the principle before the messy real one.
- **Give every mark a job.** Use each encoding for one meaning and keep it consistent:
  - position for order or hierarchy;
  - proximity for relationship;
  - size for magnitude;
  - color for category or emphasis, with only a few colors;
  - arrows for flow, cause, or dependency, never decoration;
  - containers only for real grouping.
- **Label on the thing.** Put labels next to what they name rather than in a distant legend, and keep them short. A label that becomes a sentence belongs in the chat.
- **Title with the point.** A title like "Enzymes lower the energy barrier" teaches; "Enzyme diagram" doesn't.
- **Let words and picture divide the work.** The picture carries the structure; your prose says what to look at and why it matters. Don't restate the picture in text, and don't pack the explanation into the picture.

## 5. Check before you finish

- Does it answer one question, and does the title say what the answer is?
- Does its frame match the idea's shape, and is the starting point where the eye begins?
- Could the learner redraw it from memory?
- Does every element, color, and arrow do a job?
- Are labels next to what they name?
- If it will be extended, is there room to grow?

## Common failures

- **The finished mechanism on a first encounter.** Showing every part of an expert's diagram at once is the Curse of Knowledge on paper. Start smaller.
- **Decoration.** Icons, gradients, 3D, and textures that carry no meaning train the learner to skim.
- **Arrows that mean "related somehow."** Every arrow should read as a verb: causes, becomes, sends, depends on.
- **Two frames fighting.** A timeline and a map crammed together without a clear primary frame.
- **Starting over each turn.** A new picture for every follow-up loses the record the learner was building.
- **Polish over clarity.** A rough sketch that shows the relationship beats a beautiful one that hides it.
