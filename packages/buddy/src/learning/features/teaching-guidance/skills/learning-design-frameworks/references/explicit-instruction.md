# Explicit Instruction

Explicit Instruction is a teacher-led approach for teaching new knowledge and skills clearly, systematically, and with frequent checks for understanding. It is especially useful when learners are novices, misconceptions are likely, or accuracy and fluency matter.

Explicit Instruction does not mean nonstop teacher talk. Strong explicit instruction includes modelling, active student response, guided practice, feedback, checks for understanding, independent practice, and review.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

Novices often do not know what to attend to, which steps matter, how experts make decisions, or whether their current understanding is accurate. If instruction is too implicit, learners can overload working memory, practice errors, or form misconceptions.

Explicit Instruction reduces unnecessary guesswork. It makes the target knowledge, process, model, and success criteria visible before asking learners to perform independently.

The theory of action is grounded in novice learning and working memory. New information is hard to process when students must simultaneously infer the goal, identify relevant features, hold steps in memory, avoid misconceptions, and monitor quality. Explicit instruction reduces unnecessary load, builds accurate initial schemas, gives students successful rehearsal, and gradually frees attention for more complex thinking.

## When It Is Most Useful

Use Explicit Instruction when:

- students are new to a concept, procedure, strategy, vocabulary set, representation, or routine
- the task has steps that can be modelled
- misconceptions are predictable and need to be prevented or corrected early
- foundational accuracy or fluency matters
- learners need worked examples before independent practice
- the teacher needs to reduce cognitive load
- students are struggling because too much is being left implicit

It is common in literacy, numeracy, science procedures, vocabulary, problem solving, worked examples, classroom routines, and strategy instruction.

## Core Teaching Moves

### Review Prior Learning

Begin by activating relevant prior knowledge and checking prerequisite understanding. Review is not filler; it prepares students to connect new material to what they already know and reveals gaps before they block the lesson.

Review should include material needed for today's lesson and material that needs overlearning for later fluency. It can include vocabulary, facts, procedures, concepts, homework errors, or earlier examples.

### Present New Material In Small Steps

Break complex learning into manageable chunks. Introduce only enough new information for students to process successfully, then pause for practice or checking. This protects working memory and keeps errors visible.

### Model The Thinking

Show the process, not only the answer. A good model makes expert decisions visible:

- what to look for
- what step comes next
- why that step matters
- how to avoid a common error
- what a good answer sounds or looks like

Worked examples and think-alouds are central tools.

Examples and non-examples are especially useful when students must learn a concept, category, or rule. A clear example shows what fits; a carefully chosen non-example shows the boundary. Without non-examples, students may overgeneralize from the first model they see.

### Ask Many Questions And Elicit Responses

Students should actively process the material. Questions can check recall, probe reasoning, surface misconceptions, require explanation, or ask students to complete a step. Strong questioning reaches more than the confident volunteers.

Active participation can include choral response, partner rehearsal, written response, mini-whiteboards, hand signals, sentence completion, worked-step completion, or brief oral explanation. The point is to make thinking visible and frequent, not to keep students passively listening.

### Check For Understanding

Do not rely on "Any questions?" or a few correct answers. Use checks that collect evidence from many or all students: short written responses, mini-whiteboards, quick explanations, targeted questioning, examples/non-examples, or brief tasks.

The purpose is to decide whether to move on, reteach, give more guided practice, or adjust the explanation.

### Guide Practice

Guided practice is the bridge between modelling and independence. Students attempt the work while the teacher watches, prompts, questions, corrects, and gives feedback. This is where many errors should be caught before they become habits.

Guided practice should start close to the model and then vary gradually. If the first practice item is too different from the model, students may fail for reasons unrelated to the target learning.

### Obtain A High Success Rate

Practice should be challenging but successful enough that students are learning the intended pattern. Too many errors can mean the model was unclear, the steps were too large, prior knowledge was missing, or independence came too soon.

### Scaffold Difficult Tasks

Scaffolds can include worked examples, partially completed examples, sentence frames, checklists, prompts, visual organizers, cue cards, or step lists. Scaffolds should support learning and then fade as competence grows.

Fading matters. A scaffold that remains forever becomes a crutch; a scaffold removed too early creates unnecessary failure. The teacher should remove prompts as evidence shows students can carry the thinking independently.

### Independent Practice

Independent practice comes after students are ready. It consolidates learning, builds fluency, and reveals remaining issues. If many students fail independently, the lesson likely moved too quickly from modelling to independence.

### Weekly And Monthly Review

Review over time strengthens retention and transfer. Without spaced review, students may appear to understand during the lesson but lose access later.

## Planning And Observation Checklist

Use this checklist to design or inspect an explicit lesson:

- Is the critical content clear, and is nonessential complexity removed?
- Are prerequisites reviewed before new material depends on them?
- Is the learning objective stated in student-usable language?
- Is new material sequenced logically from easier to harder, simple to complex, or familiar to unfamiliar?
- Are explanations concise enough for students to process?
- Are there clear models, worked examples, and where useful, non-examples?
- Does the teacher think aloud about decisions, not just demonstrate steps?
- Do students respond frequently during instruction?
- Are checks for understanding broad enough to reveal the class's learning, not only a few volunteers?
- Is feedback immediate enough to prevent repeated error?
- Does guided practice come before independent practice?
- Is practice distributed over time rather than massed once?
- Are scaffolds planned, used, and faded?
- Is the pace brisk enough to sustain attention while still allowing thinking?
- Does the lesson reconnect small steps into the whole skill or concept?

This checklist should not turn explicit instruction into a rigid script. Its purpose is to preserve the instructional functions that make explicit teaching effective.

## Subject Examples

Mathematics. The teacher reviews prerequisite facts or representations, models a worked example while naming each decision, gives a similar guided problem, asks students to explain why a step is valid, then gradually varies the numbers, representation, or context.

Phonics or decoding. The teacher identifies the sound-spelling pattern, models blending or segmenting, contrasts examples and non-examples, elicits frequent responses from all students, corrects errors immediately, and builds toward reading the pattern in words and connected text.

Vocabulary. The teacher gives a student-friendly explanation, uses examples and non-examples, connects to prior knowledge, asks students to use the word in controlled and then more open contexts, and reviews the word over time.

Science procedure. The teacher explains the purpose of the procedure, models safe and accurate steps, names common errors, guides students through the first attempt, checks technique, and only then releases students to more independent investigation.

Reading comprehension strategy. The teacher models the strategy with a think-aloud, highlights decision points, guides practice on a short text, asks students to explain how the strategy helped comprehension, and later reduces prompts.

## What Good Implementation Looks Like

- The learning target is clear.
- The teacher models the process and reasoning.
- New material is chunked into manageable steps.
- Examples and non-examples clarify boundaries when concepts are being taught.
- Students respond frequently.
- Checks for understanding gather evidence from many students.
- Errors are corrected early.
- Guided practice happens before independent practice.
- Students move toward independence only when evidence supports it.
- Review is built into later lessons.

## Common Misuses

- Treating explicit instruction as lecturing.
- Asking "Does everyone understand?" and treating silence as evidence.
- Moving to independent work after one model.
- Giving worksheets before students have had enough guided practice.
- Breaking content into small steps but never reconnecting the whole.
- Over-scaffolding so students become dependent.
- Using explicit instruction for everything, even when learners are ready for inquiry, discussion, application, or production.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- Gradual Release describes the shift from teacher responsibility to student responsibility; Explicit Instruction supplies many of the teaching moves inside that shift.
- Gagne's Nine Events gives a broader lesson sequence that can include explicit instruction.
- PBL and inquiry learning often still need explicit instruction at bottlenecks.
- Formative Assessment overlaps with checks for understanding but extends to broader evidence-informed decision-making.
