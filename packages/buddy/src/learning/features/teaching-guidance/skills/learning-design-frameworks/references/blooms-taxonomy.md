# Bloom's Taxonomy

Bloom's Taxonomy is a classification system for learning objectives. It helps teachers describe the kind of thinking a learning goal requires and check whether instruction and assessment are aiming at the same level of cognitive work.

The original taxonomy was published in 1956. The widely used revised version, associated with Anderson and Krathwohl's 2001 revision, reframes the cognitive categories as verbs: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create. The revised taxonomy is often paired with a knowledge dimension: factual, conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive knowledge.

In common use, "Bloom's Taxonomy" often means the cognitive domain only. Historically, the broader taxonomy tradition also includes affective objectives and psychomotor objectives. That distinction matters: a teacher designing objectives for attitudes, values, participation, professional dispositions, lab technique, performance, or physical skill should not force everything into the cognitive taxonomy.

## What It Is Good For

Bloom is most useful when a teacher needs clearer learning objectives. It gives language for moving from vague goals such as "learn photosynthesis" toward more teachable and assessable goals such as recall the parts, explain the process, apply the model to a new plant, analyze limiting factors, evaluate an explanation, or create a model.

It is also useful for checking a course or unit's task mix. If every assessment only asks for recall while the course claims to develop analysis or creation, Bloom exposes the mismatch.

## How Teachers Use It

Use Bloom at the objective-writing and assessment-alignment stage:

1. Decide what learners should be able to do with the content.
2. Decide which domain is central: cognitive, affective, psychomotor, or a combination.
3. If the objective is cognitive, choose a cognitive process that matches the goal.
4. Clarify the knowledge type involved: factual, conceptual, procedural, or metacognitive.
5. Design tasks that actually require that kind of thinking.
6. Check whether instruction gives enough practice for the expected level.

The verb alone is not enough. A verb like "explain" can be shallow or deep depending on the task, the content, and the evidence required. Good use of Bloom requires reading the whole task, not only the first verb.

An operational workflow:

1. Start with the topic or standard.
2. Identify the intended transfer or performance: what should students be able to do later?
3. Choose the knowledge type:
   - factual: terms, details, symbols, facts;
   - conceptual: categories, relationships, principles, models;
   - procedural: methods, algorithms, techniques, criteria for procedure use;
   - metacognitive: strategy knowledge, task knowledge, self-knowledge.
4. Choose the cognitive process:
   - remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, or create.
5. Combine them into an objective: "Students will be able to [process] [knowledge] in order to [purpose/context]."
6. Design an assessment that requires the same process and knowledge type.
7. Design practice that prepares students for that assessment without reducing the demand.

Example progression for a science unit:

- Topic: ecosystems.
- Weak objective: "Students will understand ecosystems."
- Clearer factual objective: "Students will identify producers, consumers, and decomposers in a food web."
- Clearer conceptual objective: "Students will explain how energy flow and matter cycling differ in an ecosystem."
- Clearer analytical objective: "Students will analyze how removing one species changes relationships in a given food web."
- Clearer evaluative objective: "Students will evaluate two habitat restoration proposals using evidence about biodiversity, constraints, and ecological tradeoffs."

## The Cognitive Process Categories

- Remember: retrieve relevant knowledge.
- Understand: construct meaning from instruction, text, examples, or representations.
- Apply: use a procedure, concept, or principle in a situation.
- Analyze: break material into parts and examine relationships or structure.
- Evaluate: make a judgment using criteria and evidence.
- Create: put elements together into a coherent or original product, plan, model, explanation, or solution.

The categories are often drawn as a pyramid, but the pyramid can mislead. Teaching does not always start at the bottom and climb step-by-step. A strong task can involve multiple categories, and a "higher" category is not automatically better for every goal.

## Knowledge Dimension

The revised taxonomy is stronger when paired with knowledge type:

- Factual knowledge: terms, details, elements.
- Conceptual knowledge: categories, principles, theories, relationships.
- Procedural knowledge: methods, algorithms, techniques, criteria for using procedures.
- Metacognitive knowledge: knowledge about strategies, tasks, conditions, and one's own learning.

This matters because the same cognitive process can look different across knowledge types. "Analyze factual knowledge" is not the same teaching problem as "analyze procedural knowledge."

The two-dimensional matrix is the most useful teacher-facing feature of the revised taxonomy. It prevents objective writing from collapsing into a single verb. For example:

- Remember factual knowledge: recall key terms or details.
- Understand conceptual knowledge: explain a relationship among ideas.
- Apply procedural knowledge: use a method in a specified situation.
- Analyze conceptual knowledge: distinguish parts of a model and explain their relationships.
- Evaluate procedural knowledge: judge whether a method is appropriate for a case.
- Create metacognitive knowledge: design a personal strategy for approaching a class of tasks and justify when it should be used.

Teachers do not need to fill every cell of the matrix. They use it to clarify intent and detect mismatch. If the objective is "evaluate conceptual knowledge" but the assessment only asks students to define terms, the assessment is misaligned. If the objective is "apply procedural knowledge" but instruction only includes explanation, students may not get enough practice executing the procedure.

## Affective And Psychomotor Domains

Bloom-related taxonomies are not limited to cognitive objectives.

The affective domain concerns attitudes, values, motivation, participation, identity, ethical commitments, and dispositions. It is relevant when a course or unit aims for students to listen respectfully, value evidence, participate in critique, adopt professional norms, persist through difficulty, or integrate a value into practice. Affective objectives need observable evidence, but that evidence may look like participation patterns, reflective justification, choices during collaboration, ethical reasoning, or sustained behavior across tasks.

The psychomotor domain concerns physical, manual, technical, or performance skills. It is relevant in laboratory work, arts, athletics, clinical practice, trades, handwriting, instrument use, physical modelling, field methods, and tool operation. Psychomotor objectives should specify accuracy, sequence, fluency, safety, coordination, adaptation, or performance conditions.

Use the cognitive taxonomy when the central demand is thinking with knowledge. Use affective or psychomotor taxonomies when the central demand is valuing, disposition, participation, physical performance, or procedural execution in the body. Many real objectives combine domains: a lab objective may require conceptual understanding, safe motor technique, and responsible scientific practice.

## Strong Uses

- Writing learning objectives that describe observable cognitive work.
- Checking alignment among goals, activities, assessments, and rubrics.
- Ensuring a course includes more than recall when deeper thinking is intended.
- Designing question sets with a deliberate range of cognitive processes.
- Helping students understand what kind of thinking a task requires.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not use Bloom as a full curriculum design framework. Use UbD or backward design for outcome-evidence-experience alignment.
- Do not use Bloom as a rigor scale. Use DOK for task complexity.
- Do not assume "create" is always superior to "remember." Foundational recall can be essential.
- Do not assign a level mechanically from a verb list.
- Do not treat the taxonomy as proof of learning quality. The task and evidence still matter.
- Do not force every lesson to include all levels.
- Do not treat the cognitive taxonomy as the whole of learning. Attitudes, values, collaboration norms, embodied skills, and performance skills may need affective or psychomotor framing.
- Do not write objectives that contain a Bloom verb but no content, context, criteria, or evidence.

## Operational Questions

- What should learners be able to do with this knowledge?
- Is the objective cognitive, affective, psychomotor, or mixed?
- What knowledge type is involved: factual, conceptual, procedural, or metacognitive?
- What evidence would show that learners reached the intended cognitive process?
- Does the assessment require the same kind of thinking as the objective?
- Have learners practiced the kind of thinking they will be assessed on?
- Is the task genuinely asking for analysis, evaluation, or creation, or only asking learners to recall a named procedure?
- Does the objective include enough content and context that another teacher could design a matching task?

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

- Bloom vs DOK: Bloom classifies cognitive processes; DOK analyzes complexity and depth of demand.
- Bloom vs SOLO: Bloom helps describe intended cognitive work; SOLO helps describe the quality of observed learning outcomes.
- Bloom vs Hess Cognitive Rigor Matrix: Hess combines Bloom's cognitive-process dimension with Webb's DOK to analyze cognitive rigor more precisely.
- Bloom vs UbD: Bloom can help phrase objectives; UbD designs the full alignment among goals, evidence, and learning experiences.
- Bloom vs affective/psychomotor taxonomies: the cognitive taxonomy describes thinking with knowledge; affective and psychomotor taxonomies help when the intended learning is about values, dispositions, participation, physical skill, or performance.
