# Restorative Practices

Restorative practices in schools are relationship-centered approaches for building community, strengthening belonging, addressing conflict, repairing harm, and reintegrating people after harm or exclusion. They are proactive and responsive: most of the work should happen before harm occurs, through trust, shared norms, communication, and community-building.

Restorative practices are often associated with restorative justice, but school-based restorative work is broader than post-incident conferencing. It includes everyday language, circles, informal conversations, classroom agreements, conflict repair, reentry support, and whole-school culture.

## What Problem It Solves

Traditional school discipline often asks: What rule was broken, who broke it, and what punishment follows? That may establish authority, but it can leave relationships damaged, needs unnamed, responsibility shallow, and reintegration unsupported.

Restorative practices add a different set of questions:

- What happened?
- Who was affected?
- What needs were involved?
- What responsibility must be taken?
- What would repair harm or restore trust?
- What support is needed so the harm is less likely to repeat?

The goal is not to remove accountability. The goal is to make accountability relational, specific, and repair-oriented.

## Use When

Use restorative practices when:

- a class or school needs stronger relationships, belonging, and trust;
- students need practice listening, speaking, empathizing, and taking responsibility;
- minor conflicts can be addressed early before they escalate;
- harm has occurred and the affected people need voice, repair, and a plan;
- a student is returning after suspension, extended absence, or serious conflict;
- discipline data shows disconnection, exclusion, or inequitable outcomes;
- PBIS or SEL systems need stronger relational accountability.

Restorative practices are strongest when implemented as a whole-school culture, not as a last-minute meeting after a major incident.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use restorative practices as:

- no consequences;
- forced apology;
- public shaming;
- a replacement for safety planning;
- a way to pressure harmed people to forgive;
- a quick script after serious incidents without preparation;
- a teacher-only burden without administrative support;
- a response to violence or harassment that ignores policy, protection, or legal duties;
- a circle ritual with no accountability or follow-through.

Restorative participation should be prepared, voluntary where harm repair is involved, and sensitive to power, trauma, safety, and readiness.

Avoid or delay restorative conferences when:

- immediate safety is not secured;
- the harmed person does not want contact or feels pressured to participate;
- there is coercion, intimidation, retaliation risk, or a serious power imbalance;
- the person who caused harm is denying basic facts, minimizing harm, or unable to take any responsibility;
- the issue involves harassment, violence, abuse, discrimination, mandated reporting, or legal/policy requirements that must be handled first;
- participants are dysregulated, traumatized, or unprepared;
- the facilitator lacks training for the seriousness of the incident;
- the process would expose private information or create public shame.

Restorative practice can still be relevant later, or indirectly, but it should not be used to bypass protection, investigation, discipline policy, or support services.

## Core Ideas

Relationships are the foundation. People are more likely to cooperate, learn, and repair harm when they experience belonging and trust.

Community is built proactively. Circles, shared agreements, affective language, and regular check-ins help create conditions where conflict can be handled constructively.

Harm affects people and relationships, not only rules. Restorative work asks who was affected and what repair requires.

Accountability includes understanding impact. A student must do more than accept a penalty. They must understand the effects of their actions and take concrete steps toward repair where possible.

Support and accountability go together. High support without accountability becomes permissive. High accountability without support becomes punitive. Restorative practice aims for both.

Voice matters. People affected by harm should have an opportunity to name impact, needs, and repair conditions without being coerced.

Reintegration matters. After conflict, suspension, or exclusion, students often need a structured path back into community.

## Common Practices

Affective language. Adults and students name impact, feeling, need, and expectation in specific language. The point is to humanize interactions and make effects visible without humiliation.

Restorative questions. Questions invite reflection on what happened, who was affected, what responsibility is needed, and what repair should happen.

For a person who caused harm, restorative questions usually move through:

- What happened from your perspective?
- What were you thinking or feeling at the time?
- What have you thought about since?
- Who was affected, and how?
- What responsibility can you take?
- What needs to happen now to repair harm and reduce the chance of repetition?

For a person harmed or affected, the questions usually move through:

- What happened from your perspective?
- What impact has this had on you or others?
- What has been hardest or most concerning?
- What do you need in order to feel safe, respected, or able to move forward?
- What repair, agreement, boundary, or support would help?

These are not scripts to rush through. The facilitator should adapt language to age, culture, context, and readiness while preserving the core movement from event, to impact, to responsibility, to repair.

Impromptu conversations. Brief informal conversations address minor conflicts, disruptions, or misunderstandings before they grow.

Proactive circles. Structured group conversations build trust, create norms, discuss classroom agreements, practice listening, process events, or strengthen belonging.

Responsive circles. Circles can address conflict or shared harm when the group is prepared and the situation is appropriate.

Formal conferences. More serious incidents may require planned meetings with trained facilitation, preparation of participants, safety checks, family involvement, and a concrete repair agreement.

Reentry circles. After suspension, absence, hospitalization, conflict, or exclusion, reentry circles help a student return with support, clarity, and accountability.

Repair agreements. A repair plan names what will be done, by whom, by when, and how follow-through will be checked.

## How To Apply It

Start proactively. Build routines for connection before using restorative practices for serious conflict. Students and staff need practice with respectful speaking, listening, turn-taking, and shared norms.

Create shared expectations. Involve students in discussing what safety, respect, responsibility, and belonging look like in specific classroom routines.

Use informal repair early. Many minor conflicts can be addressed through short conversations that name impact and ask what needs to happen next.

Prepare before formal repair. For serious harm, meet separately with participants first. Clarify what happened, assess safety, determine readiness, explain the process, and avoid surprising people in a group setting.

Protect the harmed person. Restorative processes should not require a harmed person to sit with someone who harmed them unless it is safe, prepared, and chosen. Repair can sometimes happen indirectly.

Keep accountability concrete. Repair is not just "say sorry." It may include restitution, service, changed behavior, a plan for future interactions, checking in with affected people, or rebuilding trust over time.

Follow up. Restorative agreements need monitoring. Without follow-through, the process teaches that words replace accountability.

Align with policy. Restorative practices can coexist with disciplinary policies, safety plans, mandated reporting, and consequences. They add repair and reintegration; they do not erase institutional responsibility.

Train adults. Staff need skill in facilitation, de-escalation, bias awareness, trauma sensitivity, and group process. Poor facilitation can cause additional harm.

## Whole-School Scope

Restorative practices can begin in one classroom, but durable implementation is usually whole-school work. Schools need shared expectations, leadership support, staff training, time for circles and preparation, family communication, student voice, data review, reentry routines, and alignment with discipline policy.

Implementation takes time. It is common for meaningful schoolwide effects to require several years of sustained work. A teacher using circles alone can improve classroom relationships, but cannot by themselves transform school discipline patterns, reentry practices, or equity outcomes.

Leadership should ask whether the school has:

- trained facilitators for serious incidents;
- enough proactive community-building, not only responsive conferences;
- protected time for preparation and follow-up;
- policies that clarify how restorative work interacts with consequences and safety plans;
- data routines for monitoring discipline, access, and equity;
- family and student voice in implementation.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong restorative practice is visible when:

- most effort is proactive community-building, not only post-incident meetings;
- students and adults share norms for respectful dialogue;
- harm is named clearly and specifically;
- affected people have voice and choice;
- accountability includes concrete repair;
- consequences, safety, and repair are not treated as mutually exclusive;
- facilitators are trained and prepared;
- agreements are followed up;
- reentry after exclusion is planned;
- discipline data is monitored for equity and effectiveness.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- restorative practice means "no one gets in trouble";
- students are forced into apologies or circles;
- teachers are told to restore harm without time, training, or support;
- serious incidents are handled with a quick script;
- harmed students are pressured to forgive or move on;
- repair agreements are vague and not monitored;
- adults use restorative language but continue humiliating or inconsistent practices;
- the school uses circles only after conflict and never to build community;
- leadership claims restorative practice while preserving inequitable discipline patterns.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

PBIS and restorative practices can complement each other. PBIS supplies prevention, expectations, data, and tiered systems. Restorative practices deepen relationships, voice, repair, and reintegration.

CASEL SEL overlaps with restorative work through self-awareness, social awareness, relationship skills, responsible decision-making, and self-management. SEL can teach capacities that restorative processes require.

Trauma-informed teaching matters because restorative processes must attend to safety, regulation, choice, power, and potential re-traumatization.

Classroom management frameworks can establish predictable routines. Restorative practices add relational accountability when routines break down.

MTSS can house restorative supports across tiers: universal community-building, targeted conflict support, and individualized reentry or repair planning.

## Design Questions For Educators

- Are we using restorative practices proactively, or only after harm?
- What relationships and routines must be built before conflict happens?
- Who was affected, and how will their voice be protected?
- Is the person who caused harm ready to take responsibility?
- What concrete repair is possible and appropriate?
- What support is needed to prevent repetition?
- How will we monitor follow-through?
- What safety, legal, or policy requirements also apply?
- Are restorative opportunities and discipline outcomes equitable across groups?
- Do staff have time, training, and leadership support?
