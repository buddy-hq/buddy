# Webb's Depth of Knowledge

Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DOK) is a tool for analyzing the cognitive complexity required by a standard, objective, question, task, prompt, assessment item, or learning activity. Norman Webb originally developed it for alignment work: checking whether assessments and learning opportunities actually match the cognitive demand of academic standards.

DOK is about the kind and degree of thinking required, not how hard a student happens to find the task. A long memorization task may be difficult but low in complexity. A shorter task may be more complex if it requires reasoning, planning, evidence, abstraction, or transfer.

## What Problem It Solves

Teachers and assessment designers often use the word rigor loosely. DOK gives a more precise question: what complexity of engagement does the learning target require, and do the tasks students complete actually require that same level of engagement?

It helps prevent two common mismatches:

- a standard expects reasoning, argument, modeling, investigation, or transfer, but classroom tasks ask mainly for recall or routine procedure;
- a task is made longer, more obscure, or more effortful and is mistakenly treated as more rigorous.

DOK is most useful when planning curriculum, reviewing assessment items, designing performance tasks, checking standards alignment, and auditing whether students have access to appropriately complex work.

## Use When

Use DOK when:

- analyzing the cognitive demand of standards or objectives;
- checking whether assessments match the intended learning target;
- redesigning low-complexity questions into richer reasoning tasks;
- planning a unit that includes both foundational knowledge and complex application;
- distinguishing difficulty from rigor;
- reviewing curriculum materials for alignment;
- balancing recall, skill use, strategic reasoning, and extended thinking across a sequence.

It is especially helpful when an objective uses a broad verb such as explain, analyze, describe, compare, or evaluate. The verb alone is not enough; the required thinking depends on what students must do with content, evidence, constraints, and context.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use DOK as:

- a verb chart or "DOK wheel";
- a student rating or label;
- a grading rubric;
- a fixed lesson sequence;
- a hierarchy where higher levels are automatically better;
- a mandate that every task must be DOK 3 or DOK 4;
- a measure of topic complexity by itself, such as whether photosynthesis or constitutional law is inherently complex.

DOK levels describe the demand of the task in context. They do not judge the importance of a standard, the intelligence of a student, or the value of a teaching method.

## The Four Levels

Level 1: Recall and reproduction. Students retrieve facts, definitions, terms, simple procedures, or directly taught information. The path is known and the cognitive processing is minimal. Examples include naming, defining, identifying, computing with a familiar one-step procedure, or reproducing a taught fact or method.

Level 2: Skills and concepts. Students use information, concepts, procedures, or relationships in a relatively routine way. They may classify, compare, organize, summarize, make observations, interpret a simple representation, or explain a relationship. There is some decision-making, but the path is still mostly conventional.

Level 3: Strategic thinking. Students reason, justify, plan, use evidence, handle nonroutine problems, compare approaches, construct arguments, or explain why a solution or interpretation is defensible. The task can often be completed in a limited time, but it requires more abstract or strategic processing than routine application.

Level 4: Extended thinking. Students engage in complex reasoning over time. They may investigate, synthesize across sources or concepts, revise based on evidence, design and test a solution, develop a sustained argument, or transfer learning to a new and complex situation. The extended nature matters because iteration, reflection, and integration are part of the demand.

The levels are not a ladder for every lesson. A DOK 4 project may contain DOK 1 moments, such as learning terminology or measuring accurately. Strong instruction often moves among levels depending on the purpose.

## Discipline-Specific Nuance

DOK must be interpreted inside the discipline. Generic labels often misclassify tasks.

In mathematics, a multi-step procedure can still be low complexity if the path is well known and students are mainly executing an algorithm. Complexity rises when students must decide among approaches, represent relationships, justify a solution, construct an argument, model a situation, or reason through a nonroutine problem. A proof copied from a model and a proof constructed for an unfamiliar case are not the same DOK demand.

In reading, low-complexity tasks may ask students to locate explicitly stated information, decode, recognize text features, or recall details. Complexity rises when students infer across sentences or sections, analyze author purpose or structure, evaluate credibility or bias, compare texts, synthesize evidence, or develop an argument across sources.

In science, DOK should attend to sense-making and knowledge-in-use. Recalling a fact, naming a structure, following a routine protocol, or reading directly from a graph can be DOK 1 even if the topic is advanced. Complexity rises when students use data, models, evidence, concepts, and practices to explain phenomena, plan investigations, argue from evidence, revise models, or transfer ideas to new phenomena. In NGSS-style work, DOK 1 knowledge may be necessary inside a larger performance, but it is usually not the whole intended performance.

In social studies, low-complexity tasks may ask students to identify terms, events, people, places, or explicit information in a source. Complexity rises when students analyze cause and consequence, compare perspectives, evaluate sources, use evidence to support claims, interpret historical context, explain continuity and change, or synthesize multiple sources into an argument.

The same prompt form can shift DOK by discipline and context. "Explain" could mean restating a sentence, explaining a mathematical relationship, justifying a scientific model with data, or interpreting an author's purpose across a text set.

## How To Apply It

Start with the learning target. Identify the central performance students must demonstrate. Ask what kind of thinking the target requires for an acceptable response.

Ignore the verb at first. The same verb can sit at different DOK levels depending on context. "Identify the main character" and "identify the most defensible policy option using conflicting data" do not require the same complexity.

Look at the content demands. Consider how much students must process, connect, infer, select, justify, plan, or transfer. DOK depends on the interaction between task, content, criteria, and expected response.

Check the assessment. If the learning target requires strategic reasoning, a recall-only item is misaligned. If the target requires accurate use of a routine procedure, a complex project may be unnecessary or may assess other things.

Design opportunities before assessment. Students need instruction and practice at the intended complexity. It is unfair to assess DOK 3 argumentation if students only practiced DOK 1 recall and DOK 2 routine examples.

Use DOK in planning conversations. Ask colleagues:

- What complexity does this standard actually require?
- What evidence would show that students reached that complexity?
- Which current tasks are below, at, or above the target demand?
- Where do students need foundational work before complex work?
- Which students are being denied access to complex tasks because they struggle with lower-complexity skills?

## Assessment Item Review

When reviewing an assessment item or task, judge the whole item, not only the stem.

1. Identify the learning target or standard being assessed.
2. Name the intended DOK demand of that target.
3. Read the full item, prompt, materials, data, source text, model, or context.
4. Determine what a successful student must actually do mentally.
5. For selected-response items, inspect the distractors. Plausible distractors that reveal reasoning can increase interpretive value; trivial distractors may reduce the item to recognition.
6. Ask whether the item's complexity comes from reasoning, evidence, planning, or transfer, rather than confusing wording, hidden vocabulary, irrelevant reading load, or unnecessary calculation.
7. Check whether students had learning opportunities at the intended complexity before the assessment.
8. Revise the item if the DOK demand is below or above the intended target.

A badly worded item is not more rigorous. Ambiguity, trick phrasing, excessive reading load, or missing information usually means the item needs editing, not a higher DOK label.

## Examples Of Shifting Complexity

Recall to concept use:

- Low complexity: Define erosion.
- Higher complexity: Compare two examples and explain which process of erosion is shown in each.

Routine application to strategic reasoning:

- Low complexity: Use a taught formula with given values.
- Higher complexity: Choose a model for a messy situation, justify the choice, and explain limitations.

Text comprehension to evidence-based argument:

- Low complexity: Identify the author of a text.
- Higher complexity: Use evidence from two texts to defend which author makes the stronger claim.

Short reasoning to extended thinking:

- Short-term strategic: Evaluate which of three designs best meets stated constraints.
- Extended: Design, test, revise, and justify a solution across several rounds using data and constraints.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong DOK use is visible when:

- teachers discuss task complexity with reference to the full task, not just verbs;
- standards, instruction, and assessment are aligned in cognitive demand;
- students get access to complex tasks, not only more work;
- foundational recall and routine skill practice are used purposefully;
- DOK 3 and 4 tasks require reasoning, evidence, planning, or transfer, not just projects or presentations;
- lower DOK is not treated as bad, and higher DOK is not treated as automatically better;
- assessment items reveal whether students can meet the intended complexity.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- teachers use a verb wheel to assign levels;
- rigor means adding length, time pressure, obscure content, or more steps without increasing cognitive complexity;
- every objective is pushed upward even when the standard calls for recall or fluency;
- DOK is used to label students rather than tasks;
- projects are assumed to be DOK 4 even when students mostly decorate, copy, or assemble information;
- students who need support are kept on low-complexity work and never engage with reasoning-rich tasks;
- DOK labels are added after the fact without changing task design.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Bloom's Taxonomy classifies types of cognitive processes. DOK analyzes the complexity of engagement required by a task or expectation. The two can overlap, but they answer different questions.

SOLO Taxonomy describes the structural quality of a learner's response, from fragmented knowledge to integrated and extended understanding. DOK focuses more on the task demand before or during instruction.

Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix combines Bloom-style cognitive process categories with Webb's DOK levels to help educators examine both type of thinking and depth of engagement.

Backward Design uses the desired learning outcome to plan evidence and learning experiences. DOK can sharpen the evidence question by clarifying the cognitive demand that assessments must capture.

Formative assessment can use DOK to check whether in-lesson evidence matches the target complexity, not just whether students remember facts.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What is the central performance students must produce?
- What makes the task cognitively complex, if anything?
- Is the task difficult because of complexity, or difficult because of memory load, ambiguity, vocabulary, time, or lack of prior exposure?
- Does the task require reasoning, justification, evidence, planning, or transfer?
- Can students answer correctly by following a memorized procedure?
- Does the assessment match the complexity of the standard?
- Have students had learning opportunities at this level of complexity?
