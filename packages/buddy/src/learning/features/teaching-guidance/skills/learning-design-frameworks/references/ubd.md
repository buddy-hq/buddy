# Understanding by Design / Backward Design

Understanding by Design (UbD) is a curriculum and unit-design framework associated with Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe. Its central move is backward design: begin with the intended learning, decide what evidence would show that learning, and only then plan instruction, activities, and resources.

UbD is not mainly a lesson-delivery model. It is a design discipline for alignment, transfer, and evidence of understanding.

## The Design Problem It Solves

Many units are activity-driven: a teacher chooses readings, lectures, labs, projects, worksheets, or textbook chapters, then later writes a test. UbD reverses that order. It asks teachers to clarify the destination before choosing the route.

The framework is especially useful when a teacher wants students to transfer learning beyond the original lesson. If students should apply concepts to unfamiliar problems, interpret evidence, produce an argument, design a product, or make judgments, then the unit needs evidence that captures those performances.

## The Three Design Stages

### 1. Identify Desired Results

Clarify what students should know, understand, and be able to do by the end.

This includes:

- standards and required outcomes
- enduring understandings
- essential questions
- transfer goals
- important knowledge and skills
- likely misconceptions

The practical question is: What should students carry forward after the unit is over?

Useful filtering questions:

- What ideas have lasting value beyond this unit?
- What ideas are central to the discipline rather than isolated facts?
- What ideas are difficult, counterintuitive, or easily misunderstood?
- What knowledge and skills are prerequisites for the desired transfer?
- What content is worth being familiar with but should not drive the unit?

This filtering matters because UbD is not a way to teach everything with equal emphasis. It asks teachers to distinguish enduring understandings, important knowledge and skills, and lower-priority material that may be introduced but not treated as the unit's intellectual center.

### 2. Determine Acceptable Evidence

Decide what would count as evidence that students reached the desired results.

Evidence can include:

- performance tasks
- projects or products
- explanations and arguments
- quizzes or tests
- observations and conferences
- journals, drafts, and process artifacts
- self-assessment or reflection

For transfer goals, the strongest evidence usually requires students to use knowledge in a meaningful context, not merely repeat it.

When designing a performance task, teachers often use the GRASPS logic:

- Goal: What problem, challenge, or purpose gives the task direction?
- Role: What role or perspective will the student take?
- Audience: Who is the work for, and what does that audience need?
- Situation: What context, constraints, or conditions shape the work?
- Product or performance: What will the student create, do, explain, decide, or present?
- Standards or success criteria: What qualities will make the work effective?

GRASPS should not become decorative role-play. Its purpose is to make evidence more valid for transfer: students must use learning in a context where choices, audience, constraints, and criteria matter.

UbD also treats understanding as more than recall. A strong evidence plan may ask students to explain, interpret, apply, take perspective, empathize with a viewpoint or context, or self-assess their own work. A unit does not need to force all of these into every task, but the teacher should ask which forms of understanding the desired results actually require.

### 3. Plan Learning Experiences And Instruction

Only after goals and evidence are clear does the teacher plan the learning sequence.

Instruction should prepare students for the evidence. This may include direct teaching, modelling, worked examples, inquiry, practice, discussion, feedback, revision, or independent application. The point is not to make activities entertaining in isolation; it is to make them purposeful preparation for the desired learning.

UbD often summarizes Stage 3 planning with WHERETO design prompts. In practice, the teacher checks whether the learning plan:

- makes the destination and expectations clear to students;
- hooks interest and gives students a reason to engage the big ideas;
- equips students with needed knowledge, skills, models, language, and resources;
- gives students chances to rethink, revise, and deepen initial understanding;
- builds in evaluation, feedback, and self-assessment;
- adapts to student needs without losing the unit goal;
- organizes the sequence so the work builds toward transfer rather than drifting through activities.

WHERETO is not a required section heading or a script. It is a planning check for whether the learning experiences actually prepare students for the desired evidence.

## When To Use UbD

Use UbD when:

- planning a unit, course, module, project, or performance task
- standards, activities, and assessments need stronger alignment
- a teacher is starting from textbook order or favorite activities and needs a clearer destination
- a unit claims to teach understanding or transfer but assesses only recall
- a team needs common expectations for what counts as evidence of learning
- a curriculum has too much coverage and not enough clarity about what matters

## When UbD Is A Poor Fit

UbD is not the best first tool when:

- the teacher needs a quick routine for tomorrow's lesson
- the problem is classroom behavior or relationships
- the main issue is learner access and variability rather than alignment
- the teacher only needs to tune a single practice task
- the assessment and goal are already clear and the issue is delivery

In those cases, use another framework alongside or instead of UbD.

## How A Teacher Applies It

Start with the end of the unit and write a plain-language transfer statement:

- Students will use this learning to...
- Students will be able to explain...
- Students will be able to decide...
- Students will be able to create...

Then ask what would count as convincing evidence. Avoid accepting evidence that is easier to grade but misaligned with the goal. If the goal is argumentation, evidence should include argument. If the goal is scientific modelling, evidence should include modelling. If the goal is interpretation, evidence should require interpretation.

After that, plan the sequence backward:

1. What final performance or evidence will students produce?
2. What knowledge and skills must they have before that?
3. What misconceptions are likely?
4. What practice and feedback will prepare them?
5. What formative checks will show whether to reteach, extend, or adjust?
6. What activities can be removed because they do not support the evidence?

## Signs Of Good Implementation

- Learning goals, assessment evidence, and activities clearly support each other.
- Students know what quality looks like before the final performance.
- The unit includes formative evidence before final judgment.
- Performance tasks require meaningful use of learning when transfer is the goal.
- Activities are selected because they prepare students for the desired evidence.
- The teacher can explain why each major task belongs in the unit.

## Common Misuses

- Treating UbD as a form-filling exercise.
- Writing goals broadly but assessing narrowly.
- Designing a project because it is engaging, then trying to justify it afterward.
- Confusing coverage with understanding.
- Adding essential questions that do not actually shape instruction or assessment.
- Writing essential questions that are too broad to guide evidence, too narrow to invite inquiry, or disconnected from the final performance.
- Assuming every unit needs one large performance task even when smaller evidence would be more valid.
- Treating GRASPS as a costume for a conventional assignment rather than as a way to strengthen authentic evidence.
- Listing WHERETO elements after the fact instead of using them to improve the learning sequence.
- Treating backward design as anti-flexibility. Good backward design clarifies purpose while still allowing responsive teaching.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- Bloom's Taxonomy helps phrase cognitive objectives; UbD aligns those objectives with evidence and instruction.
- DOK helps check the complexity of tasks and assessments.
- Formative Assessment supplies evidence during the unit, not only at the end.
- UDL helps design multiple routes to the same high-quality evidence.
- PBL can serve as the vehicle for UbD when the project is the unit.
