# 5E Instructional Model

The 5E Instructional Model is a learning-cycle model developed by BSCS Science Learning. It is especially associated with science and engineering education. The five phases are Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, and Evaluate.

The model is designed to sequence learning so students encounter a phenomenon, problem, or question; explore it; build explanations; extend understanding; and evaluate learning. It is not just a lesson-plan formatting template.

The best-known version is the BSCS 5E model. BSCS materials may also discuss newer work such as Anchored Inquiry Learning, which builds on the same broad tradition of phenomenon-anchored, inquiry-oriented science learning. Teachers who encounter both terms should treat 5E as the classic instructional sequence and Anchored Inquiry Learning as related contemporary BSCS design work, not as an unrelated framework.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

In many science lessons, teachers explain first and students investigate afterward. The 5E model changes the order. It gives students a reason to need the explanation by engaging them with a phenomenon or problem and giving them structured exploration before formal explanation.

This supports conceptual change because students' prior ideas, questions, and partial explanations become visible before the teacher names and formalizes the scientific ideas.

## The Five Phases

### Engage

The teacher creates interest, surfaces prior knowledge, and introduces a problem, phenomenon, puzzling event, or question. The goal is not entertainment alone. The Engage phase should create intellectual need and reveal what students already think.

Teacher moves:

- present a phenomenon or problem
- ask students to predict, notice, wonder, or explain
- elicit prior ideas without immediately correcting everything
- establish the purpose of the investigation

Avoid explaining the full concept too early.

### Explore

Students investigate, manipulate materials, test ideas, gather observations, or work through a design problem. The teacher facilitates rather than lectures. Students should have enough structure to be productive but enough openness to make sense of the situation.

Teacher moves:

- provide materials, data, cases, or simulations
- ask probing questions
- listen for misconceptions and emerging patterns
- encourage students to compare observations and explanations

Avoid turning Explore into a step-by-step verification lab where students already know the answer.

### Explain

Students first articulate their explanations, then the teacher helps formalize concepts, vocabulary, principles, and models. This phase connects student experience to disciplinary understanding.

Teacher moves:

- ask students to use evidence from exploration
- introduce formal terms and concepts when students have a reason for them
- compare student explanations to accepted explanations
- correct misconceptions and clarify relationships

Avoid making the teacher's explanation the first meaningful contact with the idea.

### Elaborate

Students extend, apply, or deepen the concept in a new context. This phase helps transfer learning beyond the original example.

Teacher moves:

- pose a related problem
- ask students to apply the model to new evidence
- connect the concept to a real-world system
- require students to revise or extend their explanation

Avoid treating Elaborate as a decorative enrichment task unrelated to the core concept.

### Evaluate

Evaluation includes both teacher assessment and student self-assessment. It can occur throughout the sequence, not only at the end. The teacher gathers evidence of conceptual understanding, skill development, and ability to apply the idea.

Teacher moves:

- check explanations against evidence
- use performance tasks, written explanations, models, discussions, or quizzes
- ask students to reflect on how their thinking changed
- identify what needs reteaching or extension

Avoid reducing Evaluate to a final test disconnected from the earlier phases.

## How A Teacher Applies It

Start with the phenomenon or conceptual target, not with the activity. Ask:

1. What concept or practice should students understand more deeply?
2. What phenomenon, data, problem, or design challenge can create a need for that understanding?
3. What can students explore before formal explanation?
4. What explanations are students likely to generate?
5. What formal language, model, or principle must be introduced?
6. How will students apply the idea in a new context?
7. What evidence will show that understanding has changed?

The phases may take multiple lessons. They may also loop: Explore can lead to partial Explain, which leads to more Explore, then deeper Explain.

In strong use, a 5E cycle often spans a multi-lesson learning sequence rather than a single class period. A useful planning assumption for substantial science concepts is one or more lessons per phase, sometimes across two to three weeks. Short one-period 5E lessons can be useful for small targets, but compressing a rich concept into Engage-Explore-Explain-Elaborate-Evaluate in 45 minutes often weakens every phase.

Looping is normal. A sequence might move Engage -> Explore -> Explain -> Explore -> Explain -> Elaborate -> Evaluate when students need another experience before a deeper explanation. The model is ordered by learning function, not by a rigid one-time checklist.

## NGSS And Three-Dimensional Science

For NGSS-style science teaching, a 5E sequence should integrate:

- a phenomenon or design problem that gives students a reason to make sense of something;
- disciplinary core ideas that explain the phenomenon or support the solution;
- science and engineering practices such as asking questions, modelling, investigating, analyzing data, constructing explanations, designing solutions, arguing from evidence, or communicating information;
- crosscutting concepts such as patterns, cause and effect, systems, structure and function, energy and matter, scale, or stability and change.

A practical three-dimensional check:

1. Are students exploring, investigating, explaining, or designing around a phenomenon or problem, not merely completing activities?
2. Does the sequence help students demonstrate the intended learning goals?
3. Do students use science and engineering practices and crosscutting concepts to make sense of disciplinary core ideas?
4. Are access, language, representation, collaboration, and support planned so all students can participate in meaningful sense-making?

If the sequence has hands-on activity but no phenomenon, no sense-making, no disciplinary model, and no evidence-based explanation, it is not strong 5E implementation.

## Strong Uses

- Science and engineering units built around phenomena, models, data, or design problems.
- Lessons where students need to confront prior ideas before formal explanation.
- Concept sequences where inquiry and explanation need careful timing.
- Curriculum planning aligned with scientific practices, modelling, evidence, and application.
- Moving beyond "activity first, concept later" by making inquiry purposeful.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not force all five phases into a short routine lesson when the concept needs a longer cycle.
- Do not treat 5E as a one-period template for every lesson.
- Do not make Engage merely a fun hook with no conceptual purpose.
- Do not make Explore a recipe lab where students simply confirm what was already explained.
- Do not skip student explanation and jump straight to teacher vocabulary.
- Do not treat Elaborate as optional enrichment if transfer is important.
- Do not delay all evaluation until the end.

## Evidence Of Good Implementation

- Students' prior ideas become visible early.
- Exploration creates a genuine need for explanation.
- Formal vocabulary and models are connected to evidence and experience.
- Students revise explanations over time.
- Students apply the concept to new situations.
- Assessment captures conceptual understanding, not only participation in activities.
- The sequence integrates practices, crosscutting ideas, and disciplinary ideas where science standards require three-dimensional learning.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- 5E is a lesson or unit enactment model, not a full curriculum alignment framework like UbD.
- Explicit Instruction may still be needed inside the Explain phase or at skill bottlenecks.
- Formative Assessment is embedded throughout the cycle.
- PBL may use 5E-like inquiry cycles inside a larger project.
