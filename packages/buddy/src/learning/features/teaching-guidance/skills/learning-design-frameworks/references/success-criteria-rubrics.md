# Success Criteria and Rubrics

Success criteria and rubrics make quality visible. They translate a learning intention into the features of successful performance, so students and teachers can judge where the work is now, what is missing, and what improvement would look like.

Success criteria usually describe what counts as success for a lesson, task, sequence, or unit. Rubrics organize criteria across levels of performance quality. Both can support formative assessment, self-assessment, peer feedback, revision, and fairer grading. Neither is useful if it merely restates the task, lists compliance steps, or turns complex learning into point collection.

## What Problem They Solve

Students often complete assignments without knowing what good work looks like. Teachers may carry criteria in their heads but not make them explicit until grading. This creates avoidable confusion, weak revision, inconsistent feedback, and disagreement about grades.

Success criteria and rubrics solve the clarity problem. They help answer:

- What am I trying to learn or demonstrate?
- What features would make this work successful?
- How strong is this current attempt?
- What should be improved next?
- How will judgment be made more consistent?

They are strongest when students use them before, during, and after producing work, not only when teachers use them at the end.

## Use When

Use success criteria when:

- students need to understand what the learning intention looks like in action;
- a lesson or short sequence requires self-checking or peer feedback;
- feedback needs to point to a concrete next move;
- students confuse task completion with learning;
- quality depends on several visible features.

Use rubrics when:

- the work is complex enough to require multiple criteria;
- performance can vary in quality across a continuum;
- teachers need more consistent grading or feedback;
- students will revise, self-assess, or peer-assess using shared criteria;
- the same criteria can guide a family of related tasks.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use success criteria or rubrics as:

- a script that removes meaningful judgment;
- a decorative heading written on the board and ignored;
- a checklist of task directions rather than quality indicators;
- a way to make every lesson administratively identical;
- a scoring grid for work that only needs a simple correct/incorrect check;
- a substitute for exemplars, modeling, feedback, and discussion;
- a tool that rewards formulaic compliance over thoughtful work.

In open inquiry, creative work, or divergent problem solving, overly specific criteria can narrow student thinking too early. In those cases, criteria may need to focus on discipline-specific qualities such as evidence, coherence, precision, originality, ethical reasoning, or fit to constraints rather than prescribing a single path.

## Learning Intentions, Success Criteria, and Rubrics

A learning intention states what students are learning. It should focus on understanding, skill, process, or transfer, not merely the activity. "Write a paragraph" is an activity. "Use evidence to support an inference" is a clearer learning intention.

Success criteria state how students and teachers will recognize successful learning. They expand the intention into observable features. For evidence-based inference, criteria might address claim clarity, relevance of evidence, explanation of reasoning, and attention to the text.

A rubric takes criteria and describes levels of performance quality. It is useful when work can be stronger or weaker in different ways. A strong rubric describes quality, not just quantity. "Uses relevant evidence and explains how it supports the claim" is more useful than "has three pieces of evidence."

## What Makes Criteria Useful

Good criteria are aligned. They match the actual learning intention and the intended level of rigor.

Good criteria are visible in the work. Students can use them to examine a draft, solution, model, performance, explanation, or product.

Good criteria are understandable. They use language students can learn to use accurately, supported by examples and discussion.

Good criteria distinguish quality. They do not merely list required parts. A required heading, word count, or citation format may matter, but it is not the same as intellectual quality.

Good criteria are limited. Too many criteria overwhelm students and fragment feedback. Choose the few features that matter most for the current learning.

Good criteria can support action. If students cannot use a criterion to improve their work, it is probably too vague or too hidden.

## Designing Success Criteria

Start with the learning intention. Ask what successful learning would look like, sound like, or produce.

Separate task steps from success criteria. "Underline the verbs" may be an instruction. "Identify the action that each verb represents" may be evidence of learning. "Explain how verb choice affects tone" may be a deeper criterion.

Use student-facing language without reducing intellectual demand. Simplify wording, not the idea.

Include examples. Criteria become much clearer when students compare work samples and discuss why one is stronger.

Use criteria during the lesson. Return to them while students work, confer, revise, and reflect. Criteria shared only at the beginning often become wallpaper.

Let criteria evolve when appropriate. In inquiry, design, writing, discussion, or problem solving, students can help refine criteria after examining examples or early attempts.

## Co-Constructing Success Criteria

Co-construction helps students understand quality rather than merely receive a teacher list.

A practical process:

1. Start with a clear learning intention.
2. Show two or three examples, partial examples, or anonymized samples at different quality levels.
3. Ask students what makes one example more successful for the learning intention.
4. Collect student observations in everyday language.
5. Cluster observations into a small set of quality criteria.
6. Name the criteria using precise academic language.
7. Test the criteria on another sample and revise them.
8. Use the criteria during drafting, peer feedback, self-assessment, and revision.

Co-construction does not mean students invent the learning goal from scratch. The teacher protects the discipline, standards, and rigor. Students help make quality visible and usable.

This approach is especially useful for writing, explanations, discussion, presentations, inquiry, problem solving, design, and performance tasks. It is less necessary for simple procedural checks where correctness is unambiguous.

## Designing Rubrics

Decide the purpose first. A rubric for formative feedback may look different from a rubric for final grading. A formative rubric should help students improve. A grading rubric should support fair, defensible judgment.

Choose analytic or holistic structure. An analytic rubric separates criteria, such as reasoning, evidence, organization, and accuracy. A holistic rubric gives one overall judgment. Analytic rubrics are often better for feedback; holistic rubrics may be better for quick overall scoring when criteria are tightly integrated.

Analytic rubrics are useful when students need feedback on separable dimensions of quality, such as evidence, reasoning, organization, accuracy, and communication. They support targeted revision and more consistent scoring, but they can become too large or encourage fragmented thinking.

Holistic rubrics are useful when the work should be judged as an integrated whole, when quick scoring is needed, or when separating criteria would distort the performance. They are lighter to use, but they give students less diagnostic feedback.

Single-point rubrics describe the target level of performance for each criterion and leave space for notes about strengths and next steps. They can be useful for formative feedback because they keep attention on the goal without forcing teachers to write detailed descriptors for every possible level. They are weaker for high-stakes grading unless performance-level distinctions are also clear.

Write criteria that relate to learning, not surface features. For a presentation, criteria might include explanation of idea, evidence, audience adaptation, and delivery. Slide count or font choice may be requirements, but they should not dominate unless they reflect the learning target.

Describe levels of quality. Performance levels should show meaningful differences in work quality. Avoid levels that simply say excellent, good, fair, poor. Avoid counting features unless quantity genuinely indicates quality.

Use parallel language. If one level describes evidence relevance, all levels should describe evidence relevance at different qualities.

Test the rubric on real or sample work. If two teachers interpret a descriptor differently, revise it. If students cannot use it to improve work, clarify it with examples.

Prefer general rubrics when learning should transfer. A general rubric for argument writing, problem solving, investigation, or design can be reused across related tasks and help students internalize quality. Task-specific rubrics can be useful for a particular assignment but may encourage compliance instead of transfer.

## Using Them With Students

Introduce criteria through examples. Show work samples at different quality levels and ask students what makes them stronger or weaker. Then name the criteria.

Model self-assessment. Think aloud while applying a criterion to a sample. Students need to learn how to judge work, not merely receive a rubric.

Use peer feedback with limits. Ask peers to focus on one or two criteria and give evidence-based suggestions. Broad peer review without training often produces vague praise or correction.

Build revision time into the task. Criteria and rubrics support learning only when students can use them to improve.

Calibrate before grading. Teachers and students can compare judgments on sample work to make expectations more reliable.

Avoid over-scoring early drafts. If every use of criteria becomes a grade, students may focus on point protection rather than improvement.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong use is visible when:

- students can explain what quality means for the current work;
- criteria are aligned to the learning intention;
- examples and discussion make criteria concrete;
- feedback refers to criteria and leads to revision;
- rubrics describe performance quality across levels;
- students use criteria for self-assessment and peer feedback;
- students help unpack criteria from examples when appropriate;
- teachers revise criteria or rubrics after seeing how students interpret them;
- grading is more consistent without making learning formulaic.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- the learning intention is just the activity;
- success criteria repeat the learning intention or list task steps;
- rubrics are mostly point allocation with vague labels;
- students see the rubric only after the work is done;
- criteria reward superficial features while ignoring reasoning or understanding;
- there are too many criteria to act on;
- exemplars are absent, so students cannot visualize quality;
- the tool narrows rich work into compliance.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Formative assessment depends on criteria because feedback needs a target. Success criteria and rubrics help students see the gap between current and desired performance.

Backward Design begins with desired results and evidence. Success criteria and rubrics clarify what acceptable evidence looks like.

SOLO Taxonomy can help describe levels of structural quality in a response, especially when moving from fragmented ideas to integrated understanding.

DOK and the Hess Cognitive Rigor Matrix can help ensure criteria match the intended cognitive complexity rather than rewarding low-level completion.

Metacognition and self-regulated learning are strengthened when students use criteria to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own work.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What is the learning intention, not just the task?
- What features of work would show that the learning intention has been met?
- Which criteria matter most now?
- Are these criteria visible in student work?
- Do students have examples of different quality levels?
- Will students use the criteria before final submission?
- Does the rubric describe quality or merely count parts?
- Could students transfer these criteria to a related task?
