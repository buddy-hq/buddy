# Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

Universal Design for Learning is a design framework for reducing barriers and planning for learner variability from the beginning. CAST's current UDL Guidelines organize the work around three major dimensions: engagement, representation, and action/expression. The purpose is not to make learning easier in a shallow sense; it is to make ambitious goals more accessible without lowering the goal.

UDL is best understood as proactive instructional design. It asks teachers to expect variability rather than treat it as an exception.

CAST Guidelines 3.0 frames UDL around learner agency: learners should be able to set purposes, use resources meaningfully, reflect on their learning, and take strategic action. The 3.0 revision also makes equity more explicit by asking educators to notice barriers rooted in bias and systems of exclusion, not only barriers inside individual learners.

## The Design Problem It Solves

Traditional instruction often assumes one path through learning: one text, one explanation, one pace, one way to participate, one product, and one assessment format. That design creates barriers for predictable differences in background knowledge, language, motivation, attention, sensory access, executive function, and expression.

UDL helps teachers ask: Which barriers are part of the learning goal, and which are accidental barriers created by the design?

For example, if the goal is to evaluate an argument, requiring a handwritten essay under time pressure may measure handwriting speed and anxiety as much as argument evaluation. UDL pushes the teacher to preserve the intellectual demand while reconsidering unnecessary constraints.

## Three Design Dimensions

### Engagement

Engagement concerns why learners invest effort. It includes relevance, motivation, persistence, challenge, collaboration, autonomy, and emotional safety.

Operational questions:

- Why should learners care about this task?
- Where can choice increase ownership without weakening the goal?
- What threats, distractions, or frustrations might block engagement?
- How will learners sustain effort when the task gets difficult?
- How will feedback support persistence rather than only judge performance?

CAST 3.0 organizes engagement around:

- welcoming interests and identities: choice, relevance, joy, play, and reduction of bias, threat, and distraction;
- sustaining effort and persistence: meaningful goals, appropriate challenge and support, collaboration, belonging, and action-oriented feedback;
- emotional capacity: awareness of expectations and motivations, self and social awareness, reflection, empathy, and restorative practices.

### Representation

Representation concerns how information is perceived and understood. It includes language, symbols, background knowledge, examples, models, diagrams, captions, structure, and conceptual relationships.

Operational questions:

- What background knowledge must be activated or supplied?
- Which vocabulary, symbols, or conventions need support?
- Would a diagram, model, worked example, analogy, demonstration, or comparison make the structure clearer?
- Are learners expected to extract meaning from one medium when another representation would remove an irrelevant barrier?
- How will key patterns and relationships be made visible?

CAST 3.0 organizes representation around:

- perception: customizable display, multiple ways to perceive information, and authentic representation of diverse perspectives and identities;
- language and symbols: vocabulary, notation, decoding, respect across languages and dialects, bias in language or symbols, and multiple media;
- building knowledge: prior knowledge, critical features, patterns, big ideas, relationships, multiple ways of knowing, transfer, and generalization.

### Action And Expression

Action and expression concern how learners act, communicate, practice, plan, and show what they know. This includes physical access, tools, planning supports, scaffolds, drafts, self-monitoring, and assessment formats.

Operational questions:

- What forms of expression would validly show the intended learning?
- What planning or executive-function supports are needed?
- Where should learners have models, checklists, exemplars, or milestones?
- Which tools support performance without doing the thinking for the learner?
- How will students move toward independence?

CAST 3.0 organizes action and expression around:

- interaction: varied and honored methods for response, navigation, movement, and access to accessible materials and technologies;
- expression and communication: multiple media, construction tools, composition tools, creativity, fluency-building, and attention to bias in modes of expression;
- strategy development: meaningful goals, planning for challenges, organizing information and resources, monitoring progress, and challenging exclusionary practices.

## How A Teacher Applies UDL

Start by clarifying the goal. The goal tells the teacher what must remain challenging. Then identify barriers that are not essential to that goal.

A practical design process:

1. State the learning goal in a way that separates the outcome from the medium.
2. Identify predictable learner variability: knowledge, language, motivation, access, executive function, social context, confidence.
3. Identify barriers in the current materials, instructions, environment, tools, participation structures, or assessment.
4. Add options that preserve the goal while reducing unnecessary barriers.
5. Build scaffolds that can fade as learners gain independence.
6. Use formative evidence to see which barriers remain.

UDL does not require creating a different lesson for every learner. It requires designing flexible pathways toward a shared meaningful goal.

## Strong Uses

- Designing a unit, lesson, activity, or assessment before problems appear.
- Planning for predictable variation in language, motivation, background knowledge, access, or executive function.
- Creating multiple valid ways for students to engage with content or demonstrate understanding.
- Making abstract ideas more visible through representation.
- Reducing dependence on a single text, format, medium, or mode of participation.
- Improving access without weakening expectations.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not use UDL as a disability-only accommodations checklist.
- Do not confuse UDL with lowering rigor.
- Do not add random choices that are unrelated to the learning goal.
- Do not retrofit options only after learners fail when barriers were predictable.
- Do not assume "multiple means" requires three versions of everything.
- Do not make the medium optional when the medium is the goal. If the goal is oral presentation, oral expression matters.
- Do not treat UDL as culturally neutral. Materials, language, examples, participation norms, and assessment modes can carry bias or exclusion.

## Signs Of Good Implementation

- The learning goal is clear and remains ambitious.
- Options remove irrelevant barriers rather than dilute the task.
- Students understand the available pathways and can make purposeful choices.
- Barriers related to bias, identity, language, disability, culture, and exclusion are examined as design issues.
- Supports are planned before failure, not only after it.
- Scaffolds support independence over time.
- Assessment evidence remains comparable even when products or pathways differ.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- UDL and Differentiated Instruction both address learner variability, but UDL is primarily proactive design while differentiation often responds to ongoing evidence.
- UDL and SIOP can work together when language demands are central.
- UDL pairs well with UbD because options should still align to goals and evidence.
- UDL does not replace special education services, IEP accommodations, or legal accessibility requirements.
