# Differentiated Instruction

Differentiated Instruction is a responsive approach to teaching academically diverse learners. Associated most strongly with Carol Ann Tomlinson, it asks teachers to adjust content, process, product, and learning environment in response to students' readiness, interests, and learning profiles.

Differentiation is not individualized instruction for every student. It is not lowering expectations. It is flexible, evidence-informed teaching aimed at helping more students reach worthwhile learning goals.

The central discipline of DI is "teaching up": plan for rich, worthwhile learning first, then provide access, scaffolds, routes, and extensions so more students can do meaningful work. If differentiation mainly gives some students less important work, it has drifted from the framework.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

Students in the same classroom rarely begin in the same place. They differ in prior knowledge, language, pace, confidence, interests, experiences, support needs, and ways of approaching tasks. A single route through learning may be too easy for some, inaccessible for others, and poorly connected to many.

Differentiated Instruction helps teachers plan for those differences without abandoning shared goals.

## What Can Be Differentiated

### Content

Content is what students need to learn or the materials through which they access it. Teachers may vary texts, examples, resources, reading supports, entry points, or prerequisite work while keeping the essential learning goal intact.

### Process

Process is how students make sense of the content. Teachers may vary grouping, scaffolds, practice tasks, questioning, pacing, discussion structures, or learning activities.

### Product

Product is how students demonstrate what they know and can do. Teachers may vary format, audience, complexity, support, or choice while preserving criteria for the core learning.

### Learning Environment

Learning environment includes classroom routines, norms, physical arrangement, emotional climate, collaboration structures, independence expectations, and access to support.

Some Tomlinson sources name this as affect/learning environment to emphasize that classroom climate, belonging, challenge, safety, and student-teacher relationships are part of the model, not soft extras.

## What Teachers Respond To

### Readiness

Readiness is a student's current proximity to the learning goal. It is not fixed ability. Readiness can change by topic, task, day, and kind of support.

Teachers use readiness evidence to decide when to reteach, extend, scaffold, compact, group, or adjust complexity.

### Interest

Interest concerns topics, questions, problems, formats, or contexts that increase engagement and meaning. Interest-based differentiation can increase investment, but it should still serve the learning goal.

### Learning Profile

Learning profile refers to patterns in how students tend to learn or participate, including language, culture, strengths, preferences, and previous experiences. This should not be reduced to unsupported "learning styles" labels.

## Key Characteristics Of Strong DI

Strong differentiated instruction is proactive. The teacher anticipates predictable variation and plans options before students fail, while still adjusting during instruction.

It is qualitative more than quantitative. The point is not to give some students more problems and others fewer. The point is to change the kind, route, scaffold, complexity, or support so the task better fits the learning need.

It is rooted in assessment. Diagnostic and formative evidence guide decisions. A teacher may use student work, conferences, pre-assessments, exit tickets, observation, quizzes, journals, or performance samples.

It offers multiple approaches to content, process, product, and learning environment. Not every lesson varies all four. The teacher chooses the element that matters for the current learning problem.

It is student-centered without abandoning curriculum. Students do respectful work, make choices when appropriate, learn to understand themselves as learners, and gradually take more responsibility.

It blends whole-class, small-group, partner, and individual instruction. Whole-class instruction still has a place; DI does not mean every student is always doing something different.

It is organic and evolving. Differentiation improves through observation, reflection, student feedback, and revision. It is not a one-time set of strategies.

## How A Teacher Applies It

1. Start with a strong curriculum goal.
2. Gather evidence of readiness, interests, and support needs.
3. Decide what must remain common for all learners.
4. Decide what can vary: materials, scaffolds, grouping, practice, pace, product, or support.
5. Use flexible grouping rather than permanent tracks.
6. Give respectful tasks to all students. No group should consistently receive dull drill while another receives meaningful work.
7. Use ongoing assessment to regroup and adjust.
8. Preserve high expectations while varying routes and support.

## Flexible Grouping

Flexible grouping is one of the most important DI practices and one of the easiest to misuse.

Group by purpose, not by permanent ability. A teacher might group students by readiness for one skill, interest for a research question, language support for a discussion, peer explanation for practice, or choice for product development. The same student should experience different roles and group types over time.

Use readiness groups when students need different levels of support, complexity, pace, or prerequisite work for the same goal. Keep them temporary, explain that groups are based on the current task, and use evidence to regroup.

Use interest groups when motivation, topic choice, or authentic audience can increase engagement without weakening the goal.

Use mixed groups when students need peer explanation, collaboration, discussion, critique, or distributed strengths.

Avoid public status signals. Names such as "low group" and "high group," fixed seating patterns, predictable worksheet colors, or repeated pull-aside routines can stigmatize students. Good DI protects dignity while making support normal.

Regroup often enough that students do not experience DI as tracking. A student may need support in one unit, extension in another, and a leadership role in a third.

## Tiered Tasks, Compacting, And Extension

Tiered tasks aim at the same essential understanding or skill while varying complexity, abstraction, structure, or support. A tiered task is not three unrelated assignments. All versions should be respectful and connected to the same important goal.

Example pattern:

- Common goal: explain how evidence supports a claim.
- More supported tier: use a provided evidence set and sentence frames to connect evidence to claim.
- Middle tier: choose from a mixed evidence set and write the explanation independently.
- More advanced tier: evaluate competing evidence, address counterevidence, and justify why one claim is stronger.

Curriculum compacting is useful when students already show mastery of upcoming work. The teacher verifies readiness, removes or shortens unnecessary practice, and replaces it with extension, acceleration, enrichment, independent inquiry, or more complex application. Compacting should not mean free time or extra work after finishing early.

Extension for advanced learners should increase depth, complexity, transfer, independence, or creativity. It should not mean more of the same work.

## Strong Uses

- Mixed-readiness classrooms.
- Units where prior knowledge differs widely.
- Tasks where students can work toward the same goal through different supports.
- Classrooms where some students need extension while others need scaffolding.
- Learning designs that need flexible grouping.
- Assessment evidence that reveals different next steps for different learners.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not plan a separate lesson for every student.
- Do not lower the learning goal for struggling students by default.
- Do not use fixed ability groups as permanent tracks.
- Do not equate differentiation with learning-styles inventories.
- Do not give advanced students only more work.
- Do not give struggling students only low-level drill.
- Do not differentiate so much that the class loses a shared intellectual community.
- Do not make groups permanent or publicly status-laden.
- Do not use choice as decoration when all options require the same low-level thinking.
- Do not confuse DI with a bag of activities. It is a decision framework for matching route and support to important learning.

## Evidence Of Good Implementation

- Students work toward clear, worthwhile goals.
- Tasks are respectful and meaningful at different readiness levels.
- Groups change based on evidence and purpose.
- Students understand why different supports or tasks are being used.
- Assessment evidence affects instruction.
- More students can access challenge without being left alone or held back.
- Differentiation supports independence over time.
- Advanced learners encounter genuine depth or acceleration, not extra volume.
- Students who need support still work with important ideas, not only isolated fragments.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- UDL is usually more proactive: design options from the start. Differentiation is often more responsive to evidence during instruction.
- RTI/MTSS involve tiered systems of support; differentiation is a classroom-level instructional approach that can sit inside those systems.
- Formative Assessment supplies the evidence that makes differentiation responsive.
- SIOP is more specific to integrating language and content for multilingual learners.
