# Hess Cognitive Rigor Matrix

The Hess Cognitive Rigor Matrix (CRM), developed by Karin Hess, combines two different lenses: Revised Bloom's Taxonomy and Webb's Depth of Knowledge. Bloom helps identify the type of cognitive process involved, such as remembering, applying, analyzing, evaluating, or creating. DOK helps identify the complexity of engagement required, such as recall, routine skill use, strategic reasoning, or extended thinking.

The point of the matrix is not to make a bigger taxonomy. It is to help teachers and assessment designers see both dimensions at once: what kind of thinking is being requested, and how complex that thinking must be in context.

## What Problem It Solves

Teachers often talk about "higher-order thinking" as if any task using a Bloom verb is rigorous. But a task can use a high-sounding verb and still require shallow engagement. Students can also complete a creative-looking activity without much reasoning, evidence, transfer, or conceptual depth.

The CRM helps prevent that error by separating two questions:

- What cognitive process is the task asking students to use?
- How deep or complex is the task's required engagement with content?

It is useful for designing assessments, reviewing curriculum materials, planning learning tasks, checking alignment, and making sure students get opportunities for deeper understanding rather than only more work or harder recall.

## Use When

Use the Hess CRM when:

- planning tasks that should develop or assess cognitive rigor;
- auditing a unit for overreliance on recall and routine procedure;
- aligning standards, instruction, and assessment;
- comparing the rigor of questions, prompts, tasks, or performance assessments;
- designing tasks that require both disciplinary thinking and appropriate complexity;
- helping a team discuss rigor with more precision than "easy" or "hard";
- ensuring that students have practice with reasoning, evidence, transfer, and extended inquiry where standards require them.

It is especially helpful when Bloom and DOK alone are being overused or misused. Bloom without DOK can miss depth. DOK without Bloom can miss the type of thinking.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use the CRM as:

- a checklist requiring every unit to fill every cell;
- a scoring rubric for student grades;
- a replacement for subject-specific standards;
- a verb-matching tool;
- a claim that tasks in the farthest cells are always better;
- a way to make every assignment more complex regardless of purpose;
- a substitute for reading actual student work.

The CRM is an analysis and design tool. It should sharpen judgment, not replace it.

## Core Structure

The matrix crosses Revised Bloom's cognitive process dimensions with Webb's DOK levels.

Bloom's side asks what kind of cognitive process is central: retrieving knowledge, constructing meaning, using procedures, analyzing relationships, making judgments, or producing something new.

DOK's side asks how much complexity the task requires: recall or reproduction, routine use of skills and concepts, strategic reasoning, or extended thinking over time.

The useful insight is that the same Bloom category can appear at different DOK levels. For example, "explain" can mean a simple restatement, a relationship among concepts, a justified explanation with evidence, or a generalization transferred to a new context. Similarly, "create" does not automatically mean deep rigor if the task mostly asks students to assemble familiar parts without reasoning.

Hess publishes multiple CRM tools rather than one universal grid. Teachers should use the matrix closest to the discipline and task:

- close reading and listening;
- mathematics and science;
- written and oral communication;
- social studies and humanities;
- fine arts;
- health and physical education;
- world languages;
- career and technical education.

The Bloom and DOK dimensions remain the underlying structure, but the examples and cell descriptions differ by discipline. A math/science matrix should not be used as if it fully describes close reading, historical inquiry, arts performance, or language learning.

## How To Apply It

Start with the standard or learning target. Identify the intended cognitive process and the intended complexity. Do not begin with an activity idea.

Analyze the current task. Ask:

- What must students think about to produce an acceptable response?
- Is the path routine or nonroutine?
- Does the task require evidence, justification, planning, abstraction, or transfer?
- Is the work extended because learning requires iteration and synthesis, or merely because the product takes time?
- Could a student complete the task by copying, following a template, or using a memorized procedure?

Locate the task approximately in the matrix. Treat the cell as a planning diagnosis, not a precise measurement. The goal is to notice whether the task's demand matches the intended learning.

Revise for alignment. If the objective requires strategic reasoning but the task only asks for recall, add a requirement for justification, evidence, comparison, decision-making, or explanation of relationships. If the objective requires fluency or accurate recall, do not inflate the task unnecessarily.

Plan supports without lowering the target. A rigorous task may need vocabulary support, worked examples, partial models, peer discussion, graphic organizers, or staged checkpoints. Scaffolding should help students reach the intended rigor, not remove the reasoning.

Check assessment evidence. If students produce an essay, project, model, debate, investigation, or presentation, decide what evidence shows the intended cognitive process and DOK level. Product complexity is not the same as cognitive rigor.

When applying the matrix in a team, use the official Hess CRM tool as a calibration artifact. The reference file can explain how to think, but teachers need the discipline-specific grid and examples when placing real tasks.

## Classroom Examples

Vocabulary task:

- Low rigor: recall a definition.
- More rigorous: use the term to explain a relationship in a new example and justify why the term applies.

Science investigation:

- Low rigor: follow a prescribed lab procedure and record results.
- More rigorous: design a testable investigation for a question, justify variables and controls, interpret data, and revise the explanation.

Literature discussion:

- Low rigor: identify a character trait.
- More rigorous: compare how evidence from different passages supports competing interpretations of the character.

Mathematics:

- Low rigor: apply a formula to supplied values.
- More rigorous: choose among possible models, justify the choice, solve, and explain the limits of the model.

Same standard, different rigor:

- Standard intent: students use proportional reasoning to solve real problems.
- Lower-rigor task: "Use the given proportion to find the missing value." This may involve applying a familiar procedure with supplied structure.
- More rigorous task: "A recipe, map, or scale drawing gives incomplete and partly conflicting information. Choose a proportional model, justify why it fits, solve the problem, and explain what assumptions limit your answer." This still serves proportional reasoning, but it adds model selection, justification, and attention to constraints.

The stronger task is not better because it is wordier. It is stronger only if the intended standard calls for reasoning, model choice, or transfer. If the goal is initial fluency with a procedure, the lower-rigor task may be appropriate.

Social studies:

- Low rigor: list causes of an event.
- More rigorous: weigh causes using evidence, explain interactions among them, and compare the pattern to another historical context.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong CRM use is visible when:

- tasks are analyzed by both process type and cognitive complexity;
- teachers discuss what students must actually do mentally, not only what verb appears in the prompt;
- assessments match the intended rigor of standards;
- complex tasks include appropriate scaffolds without removing the reasoning;
- low-rigor tasks are used purposefully for foundations, practice, or fluency;
- teachers revise tasks to require evidence, explanation, transfer, or strategic thinking where appropriate;
- students are asked to produce more than elaborate products: they must show disciplined thinking.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- teachers copy a matrix cell as if it were a lesson plan;
- every task is pushed toward high complexity without regard for the learning target;
- verbs are used as the sole basis for placing a task;
- creative products are treated as rigorous even when thinking is superficial;
- extended time is mistaken for extended thinking;
- the CRM becomes a compliance label in lesson plans;
- students receive difficult tasks without the content knowledge and scaffolds needed to reason well.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Bloom's Taxonomy identifies cognitive process categories. The CRM keeps Bloom but adds DOK to distinguish shallow from deep versions of similar processes.

Webb's DOK identifies cognitive complexity. The CRM keeps DOK but adds Bloom to describe the kind of cognitive work involved.

SOLO Taxonomy analyzes the quality and structure of observed student responses. The CRM is more often used to design or analyze tasks and assessments before or during instruction.

Backward Design pairs well with the CRM because the matrix can clarify the level of evidence needed to show transfer, understanding, or skill.

Project-Based Learning can use the CRM to check whether a project requires sustained inquiry and strategic reasoning rather than low-level product assembly.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What cognitive process does this task actually require?
- What DOK level does the task require in context?
- Is the task rigorous because of reasoning and transfer, or just harder because it has more parts?
- Does the task's rigor match the standard?
- What evidence would show that students met the intended process and complexity?
- What scaffolds preserve rigor while making the task accessible?
- Are students getting enough practice at the same kind and depth of thinking they will be assessed on?
