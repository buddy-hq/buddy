# SOLO Taxonomy

SOLO stands for Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome. John Biggs and Kevin Collis developed the taxonomy to describe the quality and structural complexity of student responses. Where many taxonomies classify the kind of cognitive action requested, SOLO looks closely at what a learner's response shows: no relevant structure, one relevant idea, several disconnected ideas, an integrated understanding, or a generalized understanding that transfers beyond the immediate task.

SOLO is useful because learning often develops from fragments to connected structure. A student may know several facts but not yet see how they fit together. SOLO gives teachers a language for noticing that difference and designing next steps.

## What Problem It Solves

Teachers often need to judge not only whether an answer is correct, but how well the answer is organized as understanding. SOLO helps distinguish:

- a response that misses the point;
- a response with one relevant feature;
- a response with multiple relevant features but no integration;
- a response that explains relationships among features;
- a response that abstracts, transfers, or generalizes beyond the taught case.

This makes it especially useful for assessment criteria, feedback, questioning, progression planning, rubric design, and analyzing open-ended work.

## Use When

Use SOLO when:

- assessing the depth and structure of student explanations, arguments, solutions, models, designs, or interpretations;
- writing learning outcomes that progress from simple to integrated understanding;
- designing rubrics that distinguish list-like knowledge from connected reasoning;
- giving feedback on how to strengthen an answer;
- planning sequences that move students from surface knowledge to relational and transferable understanding;
- diagnosing why a student with many facts still cannot explain, justify, or transfer.

It is particularly helpful in subjects where quality depends on relationships: science explanations, historical causation, literary interpretation, mathematical reasoning, design critique, geography systems, philosophical argument, clinical reasoning, and interdisciplinary projects.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use SOLO as:

- a generic ladder where every lesson must move through all five levels;
- a replacement for subject-specific criteria;
- a verb-list exercise detached from actual response quality;
- a way to label students globally;
- a grading shortcut without looking at the evidence in student work;
- a claim that extended abstract responses are always appropriate for every age, topic, or lesson purpose.

SOLO describes the structure of an observed response in relation to a task. The same student may show different SOLO levels across subjects, concepts, and tasks.

## The Five Levels

Prestructural. The response does not address the task in a meaningful way. The student may use irrelevant information, misunderstand the question, copy words without meaning, or miss the central idea.

Unistructural. The response includes one relevant aspect. The student may name, identify, define, follow a simple procedure, or give one correct fact, but the understanding is narrow.

Multistructural. The response includes several relevant aspects, but they sit side by side rather than forming a coherent explanation. The student may list, describe, classify, calculate, or collect details without showing how the parts connect.

Relational. The response integrates relevant aspects into a structured whole. The student can compare, explain cause and effect, analyze relationships, apply a concept coherently, or show how parts work together.

Extended abstract. The response goes beyond the immediate case. The student can generalize, theorize, transfer to a new context, evaluate underlying principles, form a hypothesis, or create a new application from the integrated understanding.

The important shift from multistructural to relational is qualitative, not just quantitative. More facts do not automatically create better understanding. The facts must be connected.

## Prompt Families By Level

SOLO levels can guide questions and objectives, but the verbs are only starting points. The response quality still matters.

Unistructural prompts often ask students to name, identify, define, label, find, match, count, recall, or follow one simple procedure. Example: "Identify one factor that affects plant growth."

Multistructural prompts often ask students to list, describe, outline, classify, combine, calculate, complete, or gather several relevant pieces. Example: "List several factors that affect plant growth and describe each one."

Relational prompts often ask students to compare, relate, explain cause and effect, organize, analyze relationships, distinguish, sequence, apply a theory, or show how parts work together. Example: "Explain how light, water, carbon dioxide, and chlorophyll interact in photosynthesis."

Extended abstract prompts often ask students to generalize, predict, hypothesize, theorize, evaluate, justify, prove, design, create, argue, compose, prioritize, or transfer to a new case. Example: "Predict how a plant would respond in a different environment and justify the prediction using the photosynthesis model."

Do not assign SOLO levels from verbs alone. "Explain" can be relational if it connects parts into a whole, but it can be unistructural if the student merely repeats one memorized sentence.

## How To Apply It

Start with the target understanding. Decide what an integrated response should include in the specific subject. SOLO is empty without content. In biology, relational understanding may mean connecting structure, function, environment, and adaptation. In history, it may mean connecting cause, evidence, perspective, and consequence.

Collect or imagine sample responses. SOLO is strongest when attached to actual work. Write or gather examples that show each level for the same prompt.

Ask what the response is doing structurally:

- Is it relevant to the task?
- Does it contain one idea or several?
- Are the ideas merely listed or connected?
- Does the response explain relationships?
- Does it transfer or generalize beyond the taught case?

Design feedback as a structural next step. A unistructural response may need one or two more relevant features. A multistructural response may need a relationship, comparison, causal link, organizing principle, or claim. A relational response may need transfer, boundary testing, or abstraction.

Use prompts that invite the next level. For example:

- From prestructural to unistructural: "What is one relevant feature of this example?"
- From unistructural to multistructural: "What other features matter?"
- From multistructural to relational: "How do these features connect?"
- From relational to extended abstract: "Where else would this idea apply, and where might it break down?"

Align assessment criteria with response quality. If the desired outcome is relational understanding, the rubric should reward integration and explanation, not just the number of correct points.

## Multistructural vs. Relational

The most common SOLO error is treating "many correct points" as relational understanding.

Prompt: Explain why a city might experience flooding after heavy rain.

Multistructural response:

The student lists several relevant factors: heavy rain, blocked drains, too much concrete, rivers rising, and houses built near water. Each factor is relevant, but the response reads like a list. It does not explain how the factors interact.

Relational response:

The student explains that heavy rain increases runoff; concrete surfaces prevent water from soaking into the ground; blocked drains reduce the system's capacity; rivers rise when runoff enters faster than it can move downstream; and houses near floodplains are exposed when the water exceeds channel capacity. The response connects factors into a causal system.

The relational response is not simply longer. It is organized around relationships. Feedback to a multistructural student should ask for connection, causality, comparison, hierarchy, or organizing principle, not just more facts.

## Classroom Examples

Science explanation:

- Prestructural: gives unrelated facts about plants.
- Unistructural: states that plants need light.
- Multistructural: lists light, water, carbon dioxide, and chlorophyll.
- Relational: explains how those elements interact in photosynthesis.
- Extended abstract: applies the model to predict how changing light or carbon dioxide affects growth in a new environment.

History argument:

- Prestructural: writes about the wrong event.
- Unistructural: names one cause.
- Multistructural: lists several causes.
- Relational: explains how the causes interacted and weighs their relative influence.
- Extended abstract: compares the causal pattern with another historical case or develops a broader claim about change.

Mathematics reasoning:

- Prestructural: uses an unrelated procedure.
- Unistructural: applies one known fact.
- Multistructural: carries out several correct steps without explaining their connection.
- Relational: explains why the strategy works and how the steps relate.
- Extended abstract: generalizes the method, proves a pattern, or adapts it to a new class of problems.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong SOLO use is visible when:

- teachers examine the quality of student responses, not just task verbs;
- criteria distinguish listing from connecting;
- students learn what a stronger explanation or argument looks like;
- feedback helps students integrate ideas rather than merely add more information;
- exemplars show progression in the same task;
- teachers use SOLO flexibly across subjects while preserving subject-specific standards;
- relational and extended abstract work is built on adequate content knowledge.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- SOLO levels become decorative labels on worksheets;
- teachers assign levels by verb alone;
- students are told to "go higher" without seeing concrete examples of better structure;
- extended abstract is treated as always better, even when the task only requires accurate foundational knowledge;
- rubrics reward more points but not better connections;
- response quality is judged without reference to the actual learning goal;
- misconceptions are mistaken for creative transfer.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Bloom's Taxonomy is often used to classify kinds of cognitive processes, such as remembering, analyzing, evaluating, or creating. SOLO focuses on the structure and quality of the observed response. A student can "analyze" poorly or well; SOLO helps describe that quality.

Webb's DOK focuses on the cognitive complexity demanded by a task. SOLO focuses more on the quality of the learner's response to a task. A DOK 3 task may produce responses at different SOLO levels.

Decision rule: use Bloom when the planning problem is "What cognitive process do we intend?" Use DOK when the planning problem is "How complex is the task demand?" Use SOLO when the assessment or feedback problem is "What quality and structure does the student's response show?"

Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix is useful when a teacher wants to combine cognitive process and complexity. SOLO is more direct when the problem is response quality and progression.

Constructive alignment pairs well with SOLO: intended learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessments can be written so that students are expected to produce increasingly integrated outcomes.

Rubrics and success criteria can use SOLO-like progression, but they still need discipline-specific descriptors. SOLO supplies a structure; the subject supplies the standards of quality.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What would a relational response look like for this specific topic?
- What facts or procedures must students know before they can integrate them?
- Are students currently listing ideas, or connecting them?
- What prompt would reveal whether students understand relationships?
- What example can show the difference between multistructural and relational work?
- Is the rubric rewarding integration, explanation, and transfer where those matter?
- What next move would help this response become more structurally complex?
