# Gradual Release of Responsibility

Gradual Release of Responsibility (GRR) is an instructional framework for shifting cognitive responsibility from teacher to students. It is often summarized as "I do, we do, you do," but strong GRR is more flexible than that phrase suggests.

The model is associated with Pearson and Gallagher's work on reading comprehension and was later developed in classroom practice by Douglas Fisher and Nancy Frey. Its aim is to help learners move from supported performance to independent performance.

The reading-comprehension origin matters. GRR was not only about showing a procedure and then assigning practice; it was about making invisible expert comprehension strategies visible, supporting students while they tried those strategies, and gradually transferring control. When generalized to other subjects, the same logic applies: model the thinking, support partial performance, create structured shared practice, and release when evidence supports it.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

Many lessons fail at the handoff. Teachers may explain or model, then send students into independent work before students are ready. Or teachers may over-support for too long, preventing students from taking responsibility.

GRR gives teachers a way to plan and monitor the transfer of responsibility. The central question is: How much support do students need right now to perform the task successfully, and what evidence shows they are ready for less support?

## Core Phases

Different versions name the phases differently, but a common four-part structure is:

- "I do" usually maps to focused instruction.
- "We do" can include both guided instruction with the teacher and collaborative learning with peers.
- "You do" maps to independent practice or independent transfer.

The four-part version is often clearer because teacher-guided practice and peer collaboration are not the same thing.

### Focused Instruction

The teacher establishes the purpose, models the skill or strategy, and makes expert thinking visible. This phase often includes explanation, demonstration, think-alouds, examples, and non-examples.

The goal is not to talk for a long time. The goal is to show what successful thinking and performance look like.

### Guided Instruction

The teacher and students share responsibility. The teacher prompts, questions, checks understanding, gives feedback, and adjusts support. Students attempt parts of the task with close teacher guidance.

This phase is where misconceptions should surface early. It is also where the teacher decides whether students are ready for more independence.

### Collaborative Learning

Students work with peers to apply the learning, explain thinking, compare strategies, negotiate meaning, or solve problems. The teacher still structures the task and monitors progress, but students carry more of the cognitive load.

Collaboration is not just group seating. It requires a task that benefits from interaction and norms that make thinking visible.

### Independent Practice

Students complete the task or apply the skill with minimal support. Independent work should come after sufficient modelling, guided practice, and evidence of readiness.

Independent practice provides consolidation and evidence. If many students fail here, responsibility was probably released too early.

## How A Teacher Applies It

Plan the task by asking:

1. What does independent success look like?
2. What expert thinking must be modelled?
3. What parts of the task can students attempt with guidance?
4. What evidence will show readiness to move toward collaboration or independence?
5. What scaffolds can be faded?
6. What will trigger moving backward to modelling or guided practice?

During instruction, release responsibility based on evidence, not the clock. A teacher may move from modelling to guided practice, back to modelling, then to collaboration. New content, new contexts, or visible misconceptions may require support to increase again.

GRR is cyclical across a unit. A teacher may release responsibility for one subskill, return to modelling for a new strategy, use collaboration to deepen reasoning, then return to guided instruction when a misconception appears. The direction is toward independence over time, but the path can loop.

## Content-Area Examples

Reading. The teacher models how to infer an author's purpose from text evidence, guides students through one paragraph, has partners compare evidence from a second paragraph, and then asks students to analyze a new passage independently.

Writing. The teacher models revising a weak claim, guides the class through revising one shared sentence, has pairs revise a paragraph using criteria, and then asks students to revise their own drafts.

Mathematics. The teacher models deciding which representation fits a problem, guides students through a similar case, has small groups compare solution paths, and then gives students a nonroutine problem where they must choose and justify an approach.

Science. The teacher models how to connect data to a claim, guides students through interpreting a data table, has groups critique competing explanations, and then asks students to write an evidence-based explanation for a related phenomenon.

History or social studies. The teacher models sourcing a primary document, guides students through contextualizing a second source, has groups corroborate evidence across documents, and then asks students to develop an independent claim using sources.

## Strong Uses

- Teaching a new skill, strategy, routine, procedure, genre, or problem type.
- Moving learners from worked examples to independent application.
- Planning lessons where modelling and practice need clear sequencing.
- Supporting learners who can perform with help but not yet independently.
- Diagnosing whether students need more modelling, more guided practice, more peer processing, or more independent practice.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not treat GRR as a fixed daily script.
- Do not move to independence because the lesson plan says it is time.
- Do not treat the framework as a one-way slide that can never return to modelling or guidance.
- Do not skip guided practice after modelling.
- Do not assume collaboration automatically improves learning.
- Do not use "I do" as an excuse for long, passive lectures.
- Do not confuse task completion with readiness. Students may finish guided work without being ready to work independently.

## Evidence Of Good Implementation

- Students can describe the task and success criteria.
- Teacher modelling reveals decision-making, not only answers.
- Checks for understanding influence movement between phases.
- Guided practice catches errors before independent work.
- Scaffolds are reduced as competence grows.
- Independent practice shows that students can perform without the teacher carrying the task.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- Explicit Instruction supplies many of the modelling, checking, and guided-practice moves used in GRR.
- Formative Assessment determines when to release or reintroduce support.
- Differentiated Instruction may place different students at different levels of support.
- Reciprocal Teaching and collaborative learning routines can sit inside the shared/collaborative phases.
