# TPACK

TPACK stands for Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge. It is a framework for thinking about the knowledge teachers need to integrate technology well. The framework was developed by Punya Mishra and Matthew Koehler by extending Lee Shulman's idea of pedagogical content knowledge.

TPACK is not a ranking of digital tools. It is a way to reason about the relationship among content, pedagogy, and technology in a specific teaching situation.

Context is part of the framework. The same content-pedagogy-technology combination may work differently depending on students, access, time, curriculum constraints, infrastructure, assessment demands, school policy, privacy rules, teacher experience, and classroom culture.

## The Problem It Solves

Technology integration often fails because planning starts with the tool: a device, platform, app, simulation, video, or AI system is chosen first, and the lesson is built around it afterward. TPACK pushes the teacher to ask whether the tool serves the content and the pedagogy.

The key question is not "Should I use technology?" but "What combination of content understanding, teaching method, and technology choice will help students learn this particular thing?"

## The Knowledge Domains

### Content Knowledge

Content knowledge is knowledge of the subject matter: concepts, facts, methods, structures, standards, representations, misconceptions, and disciplinary ways of thinking.

The teacher asks:

- What is being taught?
- What makes this content difficult?
- What misconceptions are common?
- What counts as quality work in this discipline?

### Pedagogical Knowledge

Pedagogical knowledge is knowledge of teaching and learning: explanation, practice, feedback, assessment, classroom organization, motivation, scaffolding, discussion, inquiry, and differentiation.

The teacher asks:

- What teaching approach fits this learning goal?
- What prior knowledge do students need?
- What practice and feedback will build competence?
- How will learning be checked?

### Technological Knowledge

Technological knowledge is knowledge of tools and their affordances, constraints, reliability, accessibility, and learning curve. It includes digital and non-digital technologies.

The teacher asks:

- What can this tool make possible?
- What does it make easier, harder, visible, hidden, faster, or slower?
- What setup, access, privacy, or reliability issues matter?
- What will students need to learn before the tool supports learning?

## The Intersections

### Pedagogical Content Knowledge

How to teach this specific content well. This includes examples, representations, common misconceptions, analogies, explanations, and practice structures.

### Technological Content Knowledge

How technology changes access to or representation of the content. A graphing tool, simulation, corpus search, microscope, dynamic geometry app, spreadsheet, or programming environment can change what aspects of the content students can inspect or manipulate.

### Technological Pedagogical Knowledge

How technology changes teaching methods. A tool may support collaboration, feedback, modelling, practice, polling, discussion, retrieval, visualization, or creation.

### TPACK

The center is the integrated judgment: the teacher selects and uses technology in a way that fits both the subject matter and the pedagogy.

This knowledge is emergent: it is more than knowing a subject, knowing general teaching methods, and knowing tools separately. A teacher may be technically fluent and still make weak instructional choices; another teacher may know the content and pedagogy well but miss a tool that would make an otherwise hidden relationship visible.

## How A Teacher Applies It

Use TPACK before choosing a tool or redesigning a technology-mediated lesson:

1. Define the content goal.
2. Identify the learning difficulty or opportunity.
3. Choose the pedagogy that fits the goal.
4. Consider whether technology offers an affordance that improves the learning experience.
5. Check tool constraints: access, cost, reliability, cognitive load, privacy, accessibility, and teacher/student familiarity.
6. Design the task so the technology serves learning rather than distracting from it.
7. Decide how learning evidence will be collected.

Good TPACK planning may lead to using a sophisticated digital tool, a simple tool, or no digital tool at all.

## Learning Activity Types Workflow

Learning Activity Types (LAT) work translates TPACK from a diagram into planning decisions. The basic move is to choose the learning activity type first, then select technologies that fit the activity, content, and pedagogy.

Practical workflow:

1. Identify the content goal and discipline-specific thinking.
2. Choose the learning activity type students need: read, discuss, investigate, model, practice, compose, debate, analyze data, solve, design, critique, present, collaborate, reflect, or create.
3. Decide the pedagogy that makes that activity productive: explicit modelling, inquiry, guided practice, peer critique, simulation, discussion, retrieval, formative feedback, or project work.
4. Identify tool affordances that support the activity type: visualization, collaboration, feedback, simulation, data collection, annotation, publishing, accessibility, revision history, or representation.
5. Choose the simplest tool that provides the needed affordance with acceptable constraints.
6. Plan student supports for both the learning task and the tool.
7. Evaluate whether the tool improved learning evidence or only changed the medium.

Example: if the activity type is "compare historical sources," the teacher might choose a shared annotation tool because it supports evidence marking, comment threads, and comparison. If the activity type is "practice retrieval of vocabulary," a simpler flashcard or low-tech approach may be sufficient. TPACK does not reward tool complexity.

## Context Checks

Before finalizing a technology-rich lesson, check:

- Do all students have access to the device, network, account, assistive technology, and time required?
- Does the tool comply with privacy, safety, accessibility, and school policy requirements?
- Will tool setup consume attention needed for the content?
- Are students familiar enough with the tool, or is tool instruction needed?
- Does the technology create inequities in home access, language, disability support, or prior experience?
- What is the backup plan if the tool fails?
- Is the technology still worth using after these costs are considered?

Context is not an afterthought. It can change the correct TPACK decision.

## Strong Uses

- Choosing technology for a specific subject and learning goal.
- Evaluating whether a digital tool actually supports the intended pedagogy.
- Planning professional development around technology integration.
- Diagnosing why a technology-rich lesson did not improve learning.
- Comparing tools by their affordances and constraints for a particular task.
- Designing online, blended, or technology-enhanced instruction.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not start with the app and retrofit the learning goal.
- Do not treat TPACK as a student task-rigor scale.
- Do not use it as proof that technology improves learning by itself.
- Do not ignore classroom context, access, infrastructure, accessibility, privacy, or student familiarity.
- Do not treat technical fluency as enough. A teacher can know a tool well and still use it poorly for a specific content goal.
- Do not treat the framework as a checklist where every circle is separately satisfied. The value is in the interaction.

## Signs Of Good Implementation

- The learning goal is clear before the technology is selected.
- The tool's affordances directly support the content and pedagogy.
- Students spend more cognitive effort on the intended learning than on managing the tool.
- The teacher can explain why this tool is better than a lower-tech alternative for this task.
- The design includes assessment of learning, not just evidence of tool use.
- Tool constraints are anticipated rather than discovered mid-lesson.

## Relationship To SAMR

TPACK is better for planning. It asks whether the technology fits the content and pedagogy.

SAMR is better for describing how technology changes a task: substitution, augmentation, modification, or redefinition.

A common sequence is: use TPACK first to choose and design wisely; use SAMR later to analyze how much the task changed. SAMR should not replace TPACK because transformation is not automatically better than fit.
