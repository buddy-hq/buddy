# Visible Thinking Routines

Visible Thinking is a Project Zero approach for integrating the development of students' thinking with content learning. Its best-known tools are thinking routines: short, repeatable question patterns or step sequences that help students observe, reason, connect, question, take perspectives, synthesize, and reflect.

The phrase "visible thinking" matters. The goal is not only that students think, but that their thinking becomes available: to themselves, to peers, and to the teacher. Visible thinking can appear in speech, writing, sketches, annotations, concept maps, sticky notes, discussion records, or revised explanations.

## What Problem It Solves

Classroom thinking is often invisible. A teacher may see final answers but not the observations, assumptions, evidence, questions, confusions, or connections behind them. Students may also treat thinking as a private talent rather than a set of learnable moves.

Thinking routines solve this by making thinking moves explicit and repeatable. They help students learn how to:

- observe before interpreting;
- support claims with evidence;
- generate questions;
- connect new ideas to prior knowledge;
- notice shifts in understanding;
- examine multiple perspectives;
- organize and synthesize ideas;
- slow down judgment;
- participate in a culture where thinking is discussed, documented, and improved.

## Use When

Use visible thinking routines when:

- students need a structured way into complex content;
- discussion is too answer-focused or dominated by quick responses;
- students need to explain reasoning, not just state conclusions;
- a lesson uses images, artifacts, primary sources, phenomena, data, texts, designs, or dilemmas;
- the teacher wants to surface prior knowledge, misconceptions, questions, or interpretations;
- students need repeated practice with a thinking move;
- the class is building habits of evidence, curiosity, perspective-taking, or reflection.

They are useful across subjects because the routines are not content replacements. They are thinking structures applied to content.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use thinking routines as:

- decorative activities detached from a content purpose;
- a random menu where every lesson uses a different routine;
- a substitute for direct instruction, modeling, subject knowledge, or feedback;
- a way to make simple recall tasks look deeper;
- an unstructured discussion prompt with no documentation or follow-up;
- a compliance move where students fill boxes without developing thinking.

The common failure mode is activity without thinking. A routine should be chosen because its thinking move fits the learning purpose.

## Core Practices

Project Zero describes Visible Thinking as involving thinking routines, documentation of student thinking, and reflective professional practice.

Thinking routines are short structures used repeatedly. Their power comes from repetition and fit. Students become better at a thinking move because they use the same routine across multiple contexts.

Documentation makes thinking available for inspection and return. It can be as simple as recording student questions, saving initial interpretations, creating a class chart, photographing a board, collecting written responses, or revisiting a previous idea.

Reflective practice asks teachers to examine what student thinking reveals. The routine is not complete when students answer prompts. It is complete when the teacher and students use the visible thinking to deepen learning.

## Choosing A Routine

Choose by thinking purpose, not by novelty.

For introducing or exploring ideas, choose routines that invite observation, prior knowledge, curiosity, and initial questions. Examples in the Project Zero toolbox include See, Think, Wonder; Think, Puzzle, Explore; 3-2-1 Bridge; and Word-Phrase-Sentence.

For digging deeper, choose routines that press students to reason, connect, support claims, analyze complexity, or examine evidence. Examples include Claim, Support, Question; What Makes You Say That?; Connect, Extend, Challenge; and Generate-Sort-Connect-Elaborate.

For synthesizing, choose routines that help students consolidate, revise, or show changed understanding. Examples include I Used to Think... Now I Think...; Headlines; Reflect, Connect, Project; and Color, Symbol, Image.

For perspectives and dilemmas, choose routines that slow judgment and make viewpoints explicit. Examples include Circle of Viewpoints, Step Inside, Compass Points, Tug of War, and True for Who?

For objects, systems, art, and design, choose routines that encourage close looking, parts-whole analysis, function, interaction, and interpretation. Examples include Parts, Purposes, Complexities; Looking: Ten Times Two; and The Explanation Game.

Project Zero's routine library groups routines by broad thinking purposes. Use those groups as a selection guide:

- Introducing and exploring ideas: use when students need entry points, prior knowledge, observations, questions, or initial hypotheses.
- Digging deeper into ideas: use when students need evidence, reasons, connections, complexity, or analysis.
- Synthesizing and exploring ideas: use when students need to consolidate, revise, bridge old and new thinking, or make learning portable.
- Investigating objects and systems: use when students need to examine parts, purposes, interactions, systems, functions, or complexity.
- Perspective-taking: use when students need to understand viewpoints, roles, identities, or lived experience.
- Considering controversies, dilemmas, and perspectives: use when students need to weigh claims, values, tensions, truth, or competing positions.
- Generating possibilities and analogies: use when students need imagination, comparison, alternative designs, or creative transfer.
- Exploring art, images, and objects: use when close looking and interpretation matter.
- Global thinking: use when students need to connect local ideas to broader systems, identities, action, or responsibility.

This grouping is more useful than choosing a routine by popularity.

## How To Apply It

Start with the learning goal. Decide what kind of thinking students need to practice: observing, questioning, connecting, explaining, taking perspectives, evaluating evidence, or synthesizing.

Select one routine that matches that thinking move. Avoid using a routine because it is popular or visually neat.

Prepare the content object. A routine needs something worth thinking about: an image, text, source, artifact, problem, model, data set, student work sample, phenomenon, question, or design.

Give quiet individual think time first. This improves participation and reduces conformity. Students can write, sketch, annotate, or prepare before sharing.

Document thinking publicly. Capture enough responses to revisit. Documentation is not just display; it is a record for later comparison, refinement, or synthesis.

Ask for evidence. Many routines become stronger when the teacher follows up with "What makes you say that?" or "Where do you see evidence for that?"

Return to earlier thinking. At the end of a lesson or unit, revisit initial observations, questions, or claims. Ask what changed, what became more complex, and what remains unresolved.

Repeat the routine across contexts. A routine becomes a thinking habit only when students use it more than once.

## Facilitation And Documentation

Introduce the routine by naming the thinking purpose, not only the steps. Students should know whether they are practicing close observation, evidence-based interpretation, perspective-taking, synthesis, or complexity.

Keep the first use simple. Use a short text, image, object, data display, or question so students can learn the thinking move without being overwhelmed by content.

Use visible recording. Depending on the routine, record thinking on chart paper, sticky notes, a shared document, a whiteboard, a digital board, annotation layers, notebooks, or concept maps. The medium matters less than whether the thinking can be revisited.

Preserve individual thought before public sharing. Quiet writing or sketching before discussion improves participation and reduces early conformity.

Ask follow-up questions. Visible thinking routines often need teacher prompts such as "What makes you say that?", "Where is the evidence?", "What changed?", "What is another perspective?", or "What remains puzzling?"

Return to the documentation. Revisit initial thoughts after reading, investigation, instruction, critique, or discussion. Ask students to revise, connect, explain change, or identify unanswered questions.

Use official Project Zero routine pages for the exact routine language, downloadable guides, and updated examples. This handbook should help teachers choose and use routines; it should not reproduce the full routine sheets.

## Practical Examples

Image or artifact analysis. Use a routine that separates observation from interpretation so students slow down and ground ideas in evidence.

Reading a complex text. Use a routine that asks students to select important words or phrases, raise questions, and connect the text to a larger idea.

Science phenomenon. Use a routine that begins with noticing and wondering, then returns after investigation to revise explanations.

Historical perspective. Use a routine that asks students to inhabit or compare viewpoints before making judgments.

Mathematical problem solving. Use a routine that has students explain what they notice, what structure they see, what strategy they might use, and why.

Project critique. Use a routine that separates strengths, concerns, questions, and next steps.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong visible thinking practice is visible when:

- routines are chosen for a clear thinking purpose;
- students use evidence and explanation, not only opinions;
- student thinking is documented and revisited;
- routines repeat often enough to become classroom habits;
- content learning deepens through the routine;
- students become more precise about their own thinking moves;
- the teacher uses visible thinking to adjust instruction;
- routines support participation without flattening intellectual demand.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- routines are used as fillers or icebreakers;
- students complete prompts but do not discuss, revise, or apply the thinking;
- documentation is displayed but never used;
- the same routine is used for every purpose regardless of fit;
- the teacher praises all responses equally without pressing for evidence or precision;
- routines replace needed instruction;
- students learn the worksheet format but not the thinking move.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Metacognition overlaps with visible thinking because students learn to name and monitor their own thinking moves.

Formative assessment overlaps because visible thinking gives teachers evidence of understanding, misconceptions, and reasoning.

Inquiry-Based Learning and 5E can use routines during exploration, explanation, and elaboration phases to surface questions and deepen explanation.

Discussion frameworks, Socratic Seminar, and reciprocal teaching can all benefit from routines that structure evidence, questioning, and perspective-taking.

Concept mapping is a related tool for making knowledge organization visible, especially when students need to show relationships among ideas.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What thinking move do students need to practice here?
- What object, text, problem, or artifact is worth thinking about?
- Which routine best fits that purpose?
- How will students first think independently?
- How will their thinking be documented?
- What follow-up question will press for evidence or complexity?
- When will we return to this thinking?
- How will repeated use build a habit rather than a one-off activity?
