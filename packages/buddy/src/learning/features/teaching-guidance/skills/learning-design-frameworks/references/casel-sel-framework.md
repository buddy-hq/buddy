# CASEL SEL Framework

The CASEL SEL Framework is CASEL's organizing model for social and emotional learning (SEL). It describes SEL as development that happens through instruction, relationships, school culture, family partnership, and community context, not as a standalone character lesson.

In this reference, SEL means the educational work of helping young people and adults build capacities for identity development, emotion and goal management, empathy, relationship-building, and responsible choices. This is a paraphrased synthesis of CASEL's public framework language; use CASEL's official materials for the authoritative wording and implementation tools.

The framework is often shown by CASEL as a wheel. The center names five broad and interrelated social and emotional competencies. The outer settings show where SEL is developed and practiced: classrooms, schools, families, and communities.

## What Problem It Solves

Academic learning happens inside social, emotional, and relational conditions. Students need to manage attention, frustration, collaboration, disagreement, identity, belonging, empathy, goal pursuit, and decision-making. Adults also need these capacities to build healthy classrooms and schools.

The CASEL framework helps educators avoid treating these capacities as accidental byproducts. It gives schools a shared language for:

- naming social and emotional competencies;
- designing developmentally appropriate instruction and practice;
- integrating SEL into academic learning and school culture;
- coordinating efforts across classrooms, schoolwide systems, families, and communities;
- choosing evidence-based programs;
- connecting SEL to equity, discipline, belonging, mental health, and academic engagement.

## Use When

Use the CASEL framework when:

- designing or evaluating SEL instruction;
- integrating social and emotional competencies into academic work;
- building a schoolwide approach to belonging, relationships, and supportive discipline;
- selecting or reviewing SEL programs;
- aligning classroom practices, family partnerships, and community supports;
- developing adult SEL and educator capacity;
- creating standards or developmental outcomes for SEL;
- connecting SEL with PBIS, restorative practices, mental health supports, or civic learning.

It is most useful as a systemic framework, not as a one-off lesson bank.

## Do Not Use When

Do not use CASEL SEL as:

- generic inspirational activities with no skill practice;
- a replacement for academic rigor;
- a behavior compliance program;
- a way to impose one cultural style of emotion, communication, or identity;
- a standalone curriculum disconnected from school climate and adult behavior;
- a therapy substitute for students needing mental-health treatment;
- a checklist that treats competencies as isolated boxes.

SEL is not simply being nice, calm, or obedient. It includes agency, relationships, decision-making, identity, empathy, and participation in a community.

## The CASEL Five

Self-awareness. Students and adults learn to notice and interpret their emotions, values, identities, interests, strengths, limits, and sense of purpose. In practice, this includes recognizing feelings, noticing stress, understanding personal and cultural identity, and judging strengths and needs with realism.

Self-management. Students and adults learn to direct attention, effort, emotion, behavior, and planning across situations. This includes goal setting, organization, impulse control, stress management, persistence, and strategy use during challenge.

Social awareness. Students and adults learn to understand others' perspectives and contexts, including differences in culture, background, experience, and social position. This includes empathy, noticing others' strengths, understanding norms, and recognizing resources in families, schools, and communities.

Relationship skills. Students and adults learn to form, maintain, and repair relationships. This includes communication, listening, cooperation, negotiation, help-seeking, resisting harmful pressure, leadership, and conflict repair.

Responsible decision-making. Students and adults learn to make choices that account for evidence, ethics, safety, consequences, wellbeing, norms, and effects on oneself and others.

These competencies interact. A conflict discussion, for example, may involve self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making at the same time.

## Key Settings

Classrooms. SEL can be taught explicitly, practiced through active learning, embedded in academic instruction, and reinforced through routines, discussion norms, cooperative learning, project work, feedback, and reflection.

Schools. Schoolwide SEL includes climate, policies, discipline, adult relationships, student voice, leadership, professional learning, and aligned practices across settings.

Families. Families shape social and emotional development. Effective SEL treats families as partners, respects cultural knowledge, and builds two-way communication rather than only sending school-defined expectations home.

Communities. Community organizations, mentors, afterschool programs, health providers, cultural institutions, and local leaders can support and extend SEL beyond school.

The systemic point is coordination. Students should not experience SEL as a disconnected lesson in one room and contradictory adult behavior or school policy elsewhere.

## Systemic Implementation Map

For schools and districts, CASEL is not only a competency wheel. It is also an implementation framework. A practical implementation map includes four kinds of work:

Foundational support and planning. Establish a shared vision, communicate why SEL matters, create an implementation structure, allocate time and resources, and connect SEL with academic priorities, equity, student support, and school improvement. Typical artifacts include a vision statement, implementation team roles, communication plan, resource map, and phased action plan.

Adult SEL and capacity. Build adult understanding, adult social and emotional competencies, cultural competence, and staff trust. This includes professional learning, coaching, leadership modelling, collaborative routines, and attention to adult working conditions. SEL implementation is weak when adults are asked to teach competencies they are not supported to practice.

Student SEL. Promote SEL through explicit instruction, evidence-based programs, classroom practices, academic integration, student voice, family and community partnerships, and aligned student supports. This is where the CASEL five become teachable and observable in daily school life.

Continuous improvement. Use implementation and outcome evidence to refine the work over time. Evidence may include climate data, attendance, discipline patterns, student and staff perception data, family feedback, classroom observation, implementation fidelity, academic engagement, and student work. Continuous improvement should ask what conditions and practices need adjustment, not merely whether students have deficits.

This map is most relevant for school leaders, instructional coaches, counselors, and district teams. A classroom teacher can still use it as a check: if classroom SEL is constantly contradicted by school policy, adult behavior, or discipline systems, the issue is systemic rather than only instructional.

## How To Apply It

Start with local purpose. Identify what social, emotional, relational, and academic outcomes matter in the setting: belonging, engagement, peer relationships, conflict repair, attendance, agency, collaboration, or classroom climate.

Map current practices. Schools often already have SEL-related work: advisory, PBIS, restorative circles, counseling, project-based learning, mentoring, family engagement, or classroom routines. CASEL can help align these instead of adding another initiative.

Choose competencies intentionally. Do not try to teach everything at once. Select competencies that connect to current student needs, developmental stage, academic tasks, and school priorities.

Use developmental judgment. The same competency looks different by age, culture, context, and responsibility level. Self-management in kindergarten may involve naming feelings, waiting briefly, and using a practiced calming routine. Self-management in high school may involve planning long-term work, handling conflicting priorities, seeking help, and regulating emotion during disagreement. Do not use the same rubric or behavioral expectation across grade bands without adjustment.

Teach skills explicitly. Students may need direct instruction, modeling, vocabulary, examples, and practice. For example, "collaboration" requires listening, role clarity, disagreement norms, feedback, and repair strategies.

Embed practice in academic work. SEL is more durable when students use it in meaningful tasks: discussing a text, solving a group problem, revising after feedback, presenting an argument, doing a science investigation, or reflecting on learning.

Use active practice. High-quality SEL is sequenced, active, focused, and explicit. Students need repeated opportunities to practice skills, not just hear about them.

Attend to adult SEL. Adults shape climate through their own regulation, relationships, decisions, expectations, and responses to conflict. Adult practice is part of implementation.

Build supportive environments. SEL skills develop best in classrooms and schools characterized by belonging, safety, respect, meaningful challenge, student voice, and trusting relationships.

Evaluate and adjust. Use student, staff, family, climate, discipline, attendance, academic, and implementation data carefully. Avoid reducing SEL to a single score.

When selecting a program, examine more than the label "evidence-based." A team should ask:

- Which grade bands and settings was the program designed for?
- Which competencies does it emphasize, and which does it not address?
- What evidence supports the program, and for whom?
- Does the program include sequenced, active, focused, explicit instruction?
- What training, coaching, materials, and time are required?
- How will the program be adapted to local culture without removing its core design?
- How will families and students have voice in selection and implementation?
- How will the program connect with discipline, academics, counseling, PBIS, restorative practices, and student supports?

Program choice is only one part of implementation. A strong program can fail if there is no adult learning, leadership support, time, cultural responsiveness, or continuous improvement.

## Classroom Examples

Academic discussion. Teach students how to listen, build on a peer's idea, disagree with evidence, ask clarifying questions, and reflect on group process.

Writing revision. Students identify frustration, use a revision strategy, seek feedback, and decide which feedback to apply.

Mathematical problem solving. Students monitor persistence, explain reasoning, collaborate, and evaluate whether a solution is fair and reasonable.

Project-based learning. Students set group norms, assign roles, manage conflict, make decisions, and reflect on how collaboration affected the product.

Literature or history. Students examine perspectives, identity, ethical decisions, empathy, and consequences while grounding claims in evidence.

Restorative conversation. Students name impact, listen to others, regulate emotion, take responsibility, and decide how to repair harm.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong SEL implementation is visible when:

- competencies are taught, modeled, practiced, and reinforced;
- SEL is integrated with academic learning and school culture;
- adult behavior matches the SEL expectations for students;
- students have voice and agency;
- families and communities are engaged authentically;
- practices are developmentally and culturally responsive;
- competencies are interpreted in age-appropriate ways rather than as identical expectations for all students;
- school discipline supports learning, accountability, and belonging;
- evidence-based programs are implemented with support and adaptation to context;
- data is used to improve conditions, not to label students.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- SEL is a weekly lesson with no connection to classroom life;
- the focus is compliance rather than competence and belonging;
- teachers are expected to implement SEL without professional learning or time;
- programs are purchased but not embedded in systems;
- "evidence-based" is treated as a purchasing label without checking fit, implementation demands, or local conditions;
- student culture, identity, and community knowledge are ignored;
- adults ask students to regulate emotions in environments that are unsafe or humiliating;
- SEL data is used to rank or pathologize students;
- competencies are named but not practiced.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

PBIS provides tiered systems, data, and practices for positive school climate and behavior support. CASEL SEL can define competencies that students and adults learn within those systems.

Restorative Practices support relationships, accountability, repair, and belonging. SEL competencies help students participate in restorative work.

Metacognition and self-regulated learning overlap with self-awareness and self-management, especially planning, monitoring, emotion regulation, and strategy use.

UDL can support SEL by reducing barriers, increasing agency, and offering multiple ways to engage, express, and participate.

Collaborative Learning, PBL, Socratic Seminar, and discussion routines can all become SEL practice contexts when relationship skills, perspective-taking, and decision-making are explicitly taught and reflected on.

Mental-health supports are related but not identical. SEL promotes skills and environments for all students; some students also need targeted or clinical supports.

## Design Questions For Educators

- Which SEL competencies are most relevant to the current learning and school priorities?
- How will students practice these skills in real academic and social contexts?
- What adult behaviors and routines must model the competencies?
- How will families and communities shape the approach?
- Is the SEL work culturally responsive and asset-based?
- How does SEL connect to discipline, belonging, and student voice?
- What evidence will show improved conditions and skills?
- Are we building a system or adding isolated lessons?
