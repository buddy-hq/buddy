# Formative Assessment / Assessment for Learning

Formative assessment is the ongoing process of eliciting evidence of student thinking during learning and using that evidence to adjust teaching, learning, feedback, practice, grouping, pacing, or next tasks. It is not a category of small quizzes by itself. An activity becomes formative only when the evidence is interpreted and used while there is still time to improve learning.

Assessment for Learning is the closely related classroom tradition associated with Paul Black, Dylan Wiliam, Royce Sadler, and others. Its practical center is simple: make the learning goal and quality criteria visible, find out where students are now, help them understand the gap, and create a next action that moves learning forward.

## What Problem It Solves

Teaching often proceeds from what was explained rather than from what students actually understood. Formative assessment reduces that blind spot. It gives teachers a disciplined way to notice partial understanding, misconceptions, overconfidence, missing prerequisite knowledge, weak transfer, and uneven participation before a unit-ending assessment makes the problem expensive.

It also shifts assessment from judgment-only to learning support. Students learn what quality looks like, how to interpret evidence about their own work, how to use feedback, and how to take more responsibility for improvement.

## Use When

Use formative assessment when:

- deciding whether to reteach, extend, regroup, or move on;
- checking whether an explanation, model, reading, demonstration, or practice task actually landed;
- uncovering misconceptions before independent work;
- making feedback more actionable than grades or praise;
- helping students understand criteria for quality work;
- building peer review, self-assessment, revision, or metacognition;
- turning exit tickets, hinge questions, drafts, whiteboards, conferences, quizzes, observations, or discussions into instructional evidence.

It is especially useful in complex learning where correctness is not enough: writing, mathematical reasoning, scientific explanation, source analysis, design work, language learning, problem solving, and project work.

## Do Not Use When

Do not treat formative assessment as:

- another name for frequent grading;
- a compliance routine where every lesson must have an exit ticket whether or not the teacher will use it;
- a set of generic activities detached from the current learning goal;
- a way to rank students publicly;
- a replacement for summative assessment when certification, reporting, or final judgment is required;
- a data-collection burden that slows instruction without changing instruction.

The common failure mode is collecting evidence but not acting on it. A quiz, poll, or exit ticket that is scored and filed away is not doing formative work.

## Core Ideas

Formative assessment has three recurring questions:

- Where is the learning going?
- Where are students now?
- What should happen next?

Dylan Wiliam’s classroom formulation crosses those questions with three agents: teacher, peers, and students themselves. This yields five practical strategies:

- clarify, share, and develop understanding of learning intentions and success criteria;
- design discussions, questions, tasks, and activities that reveal evidence of learning;
- provide feedback that helps students improve their next attempt;
- activate students as resources for one another;
- activate students as owners of their own learning.

The U.S. Department of Education TEAL guidance frames the process around identifying the learning gap, giving usable feedback, using learning progressions, and involving students in self-assessment and decision-making. The important through-line is that evidence and action are connected.

As a simple design checklist, a formative assessment plan should include:

- Gap identification: What is the difference between current evidence and the desired learning?
- Feedback: What information will help the teacher and student decide the next move?
- Learning progression: What smaller steps or levels of quality sit between current work and the goal?
- Student involvement: How will students understand the goal, judge evidence, and act on feedback?

## How To Apply It

Start with the learning target. Decide what students are supposed to understand or be able to do, and what evidence would show meaningful progress. A vague target produces vague assessment.

Name success in observable terms. Students need more than the topic or activity. They need criteria that distinguish stronger from weaker work. For a written explanation, this may include claim accuracy, evidence selection, reasoning, and attention to counterexamples. For a skill, it may include accuracy, fluency, strategy choice, and ability to explain decisions.

Choose a method that exposes thinking, not just answers. Examples include:

- hinge questions with plausible misconceptions in the options;
- mini-whiteboard responses from every student;
- short written explanations;
- annotated examples;
- worked-example completion;
- error analysis;
- quick conferences;
- peer review against shared criteria;
- draft checkpoints;
- exit tickets focused on one decision the teacher must make.

Interpret the evidence quickly. Sort responses by the instructional decision they imply: ready to extend, needs more guided practice, has a specific misconception, lacks vocabulary, lacks prerequisite knowledge, or needs a different representation.

Respond visibly. The response can be a brief reteach, a second example, a changed grouping, a targeted practice set, a student explanation, an extension problem, a revised prompt, a model answer, a conference, or a delayed plan for the next lesson. Students should be able to see that evidence changed the path.

Make feedback a next-action message. Effective formative feedback tells students what quality gap matters most and what to do next. It should not overwhelm them with every possible correction. Grades, scores, and general praise often reduce attention to improvement unless paired with a clear next move.

Teach students to use criteria. Peer and self-assessment work only when students have models, criteria, and practice making judgments. Early peer review should focus on specific features, not broad opinions. Students may need sentence stems, exemplars, calibration, and teacher moderation before peer feedback becomes useful.

Close the loop. Give students time to use the feedback. Without revision, reattempt, discussion, or adjusted practice, feedback remains information rather than learning.

## Routines For The Five Strategies

Clarify learning intentions and success criteria.

- Compare strong and weak examples before students begin work.
- Co-construct success criteria after examining exemplars.
- Translate a standard into "what this means in today's task."
- Ask students to identify where a sample meets or misses the criteria.
- Use a short rubric or checklist during drafting, not only after submission.

Elicit evidence of learning.

- Use hinge questions at points where the next teaching move depends on student understanding.
- Ask all students to respond simultaneously through whiteboards, polling, cards, short writing, or shared documents.
- Use diagnostic distractors that represent likely misconceptions.
- Ask students to explain why an answer is correct or why an error is tempting.
- Inspect a sample of student work during practice and sort it by need.

Provide feedback that moves learning forward.

- Give one or two high-leverage next steps rather than marking every error.
- Use feedback codes only if students know what they mean and must act on them.
- Hold short conferences that end with a concrete revision decision.
- Delay grades during drafting when grades would distract from improvement.
- Build time for students to revise, retry, or apply feedback to a new problem.

Activate students as resources for one another.

- Use peer review on one criterion at a time.
- Give students models of useful feedback before asking them to critique peers.
- Ask partners to find evidence for a criterion rather than give personal preference.
- Use "two drafts, one criterion" comparison: which draft better meets the criterion and why?
- Teach students how to ask clarifying questions and request evidence.

Activate students as owners of their learning.

- Ask students to choose which criterion they most need to improve and justify the choice.
- Use self-assessment before teacher feedback so students practice judging quality.
- Ask students to mark confidence and uncertainty with evidence.
- Use reflection prompts that lead to action: what will you change next, and why?
- Have students track a learning progression over several attempts.

These routines are not automatically formative. They become formative when evidence is interpreted and changes teaching, practice, feedback, or student action.

## Practical Classroom Moves

Hinge question: Ask one carefully designed multiple-choice or short-response question at a decision point. Each wrong option should represent a likely misconception. Use the distribution of answers to decide whether to move on, reteach, or split the class.

All-student response: Use mini-whiteboards, cards, polling, or simultaneous written answers so evidence comes from the whole class rather than the fastest volunteers.

Exit ticket with a decision rule: Ask one or two questions tied to tomorrow’s plan. Before using it, decide what patterns will trigger reteaching, small-group support, extension, or a changed sequence.

No-grade draft feedback: On drafts, give focused comments or codes aligned to success criteria, then require revision. Use grades later when the purpose is judgment.

Error analysis: Give students a flawed solution or response and ask them to identify the error, explain why it matters, and repair it. This reveals whether they understand the structure of quality.

Exemplar comparison: Show two or three pieces of work at different quality levels. Ask students to identify which is stronger and why, using criteria. Then apply the same criteria to their own work.

Student self-check: Ask students to mark where they are confident, uncertain, or stuck, and to name the evidence for that judgment. Treat this as a skill to teach, not a trait students already possess.

Learning progression check: Show a short progression from early attempt to stronger work. Ask students to locate their current work on the progression and identify the next feature to improve.

Action-response feedback: After receiving feedback, students write the specific change they will make, make the change, and then explain how the revision improved the work.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong formative assessment is visible when:

- checks are aligned to the learning target and reveal thinking;
- most students, not only volunteers, provide evidence;
- the teacher changes instruction based on the evidence;
- feedback points to a next action;
- students understand what quality looks like;
- students have time to revise, retry, explain, or practice differently;
- peer and self-assessment are structured around criteria;
- assessment information improves learning rather than merely increasing records.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- every lesson has a ritual check but the results do not affect teaching;
- feedback is mostly grades, praise, or vague comments;
- teachers collect more data than they can interpret or use;
- students cannot explain the criteria for success;
- peer review becomes compliment trading or untrained correction;
- tasks check recall when the learning goal requires reasoning or transfer;
- formative evidence becomes another summative mark.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

Diagnostic assessment happens before or at the start of instruction to identify prior knowledge, prerequisites, misconceptions, or placement needs. Formative assessment happens during learning and is used to adjust the path.

Success criteria and rubrics are tools that can support formative assessment by making quality visible. They are not formative by themselves unless students and teachers use them to improve work.

Feedback is one part of formative assessment. Feedback has formative value when it changes what the learner or teacher does next.

Metacognition and self-regulated learning overlap strongly with student ownership in formative assessment. Students learn to monitor quality, choose strategies, and respond to evidence.

Summative assessment judges learning at a point in time. A summative task can be used formatively if its evidence changes future instruction or student learning, but its reporting function is different.

## Design Questions For Teachers

- What exact understanding, skill, or transfer am I checking?
- What would a misconception look like here?
- How will I get evidence from every student or from the students whose thinking I most need to see?
- What decision will I make from the evidence?
- What will students do with the feedback?
- How will students learn the criteria for quality?
- Will this check make instruction lighter and sharper, or just add another record?
