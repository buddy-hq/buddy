# Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports

Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS) is an evidence-based, tiered framework for organizing behavioral, social, emotional, academic, and mental-health supports. It is not a packaged curriculum, a token economy, or a one-day training. It is a schoolwide implementation framework that integrates systems, data, practices, outcomes, and equity.

PBIS aims to create positive, predictable, equitable, and safe learning environments. Its central assumption is that behavior support should be taught, reinforced, monitored, and adjusted with the same seriousness as academic instruction.

## What Problem It Solves

Schools often respond to behavior after it escalates, rely on inconsistent adult responses, or use exclusionary discipline without teaching replacement skills. Students may experience different expectations across settings and adults. Teachers may also lack a shared system for prevention, intervention, and progress monitoring.

PBIS addresses this by creating:

- common expectations across settings;
- proactive teaching of social, emotional, and behavioral skills;
- a continuum of supports matched to student need;
- data-based decision-making;
- consistent adult practices;
- systems for training, coaching, and fidelity;
- attention to equitable outcomes across student groups.

## Use When

Use PBIS when:

- a school needs a coherent framework for behavior support;
- expectations and responses vary widely across staff;
- discipline is mostly reactive;
- office referrals, suspensions, classroom disruptions, or exclusionary practices are high;
- students need explicit teaching of schoolwide routines and behavioral expectations;
- teams need data to identify patterns and choose supports;
- Tier 1 classroom practices are not strong enough to prevent avoidable problems;
- Tier 2 and Tier 3 supports need clearer entry, progress-monitoring, and exit criteria.

PBIS is most appropriate as a schoolwide or programwide system, not as an isolated technique used by one teacher.

## Do Not Use When

Do not treat PBIS as:

- a reward store;
- bribery for basic behavior;
- a poster campaign;
- a behavior chart system only;
- a way to avoid consequences;
- a replacement for relationships, culturally responsive practice, restorative work, or individualized support;
- a generic classroom management package;
- a reason to ignore serious harm or safety needs.

PBIS fails when schools adopt surface artifacts without the underlying systems: shared expectations, teaching routines, data use, adult consistency, leadership teams, coaching, family engagement, and progress monitoring.

## Core Elements

Systems. PBIS requires leadership teams, administrative support, professional development, coaching, routines for decision-making, staff communication, and structures that make implementation sustainable.

Data. Schools use screening, discipline, attendance, academic, fidelity, climate, and progress-monitoring data to identify strengths, needs, inequities, and response to intervention. Data should guide decisions, not merely document problems.

Practices. Schools select evidence-based practices that fit local goals and culture. Practices include teaching expectations, acknowledging expected behavior, preventing predictable problems, responding instructionally to unwanted behavior, and providing targeted or individualized interventions.

Outcomes. PBIS is judged by results: improved school climate, social-emotional competence, academic engagement, reduced exclusionary discipline, improved staff wellbeing, and more equitable student experiences.

Equity. PBIS implementation should examine whether expectations, responses, discipline, access to supports, and outcomes are fair across student groups. Equity is not an add-on; it changes how teams interpret data and design supports.

Foundational systems across tiers include:

- a shared vision for positive social culture;
- a representative leadership team that meets regularly;
- active family and community engagement;
- visible administrative support;
- professional development and coaching for staff;
- screening, progress-monitoring, outcome, and fidelity data;
- regular data-based decision routines;
- disaggregation of data to examine equity across student groups.

Without these systems, PBIS practices often become isolated classroom techniques rather than a durable framework.

## The Three Tiers

Tier 1: Universal support. Tier 1 is for all students and staff across all settings. It establishes the social culture of the school. Schools define a small number of positively stated expectations, teach what they look like in specific routines and settings, acknowledge expected behavior, and respond to unwanted behavior in a respectful and instructional way.

Core Tier 1 practices include:

- defining positive expectations with students, families, and educators;
- aligning classroom expectations with schoolwide expectations;
- explicitly teaching expectations and social, emotional, and behavioral skills;
- encouraging and acknowledging expected behavior with specific feedback;
- preventing predictable problems through routines, supervision, and engagement;
- responding to unwanted behavior in respectful, instructional ways;
- building school-family partnerships around expectations and supports.

Tier 2: Targeted support. Tier 2 is for students who need more support than Tier 1 provides. Supports are efficient, accessible, matched to need, and progress-monitored. Examples may include check-in/check-out, small-group skill instruction, self-management support, mentoring, or targeted academic-behavior supports. Tier 2 should not replace a weak Tier 1 system.

Tier 3: Intensive individualized support. Tier 3 is for students with more significant or persistent needs. Supports are individualized, assessment-informed, and often function-based. Tier 3 may involve wraparound planning, functional behavior assessment, behavior intervention planning, mental-health collaboration, family partnership, and frequent progress monitoring.

The tiers are not labels for students. They describe levels of support.

## How To Apply It

Build a representative team. Include administrators, teachers, student support staff, family/community voice where appropriate, and people with expertise in instruction, behavior, equity, data, and mental health.

Define valued outcomes. Use local data and community priorities. Outcomes should be observable and measurable, such as reduced suspensions, improved attendance, fewer hallway incidents, higher belonging, or improved classroom engagement.

Define expectations. Establish a small set of positive expectations and describe what they look like in classrooms, hallways, cafeterias, buses, online spaces, and other routines.

Teach expectations directly. Do not assume students know the routines. Model, practice, give examples and non-examples, and reteach after breaks or when data shows a problem.

Acknowledge expected behavior. Recognition should be specific and connected to expectations. The purpose is feedback and culture-building, not prize distribution.

Respond instructionally to unwanted behavior. Responses should preserve safety, repair harm where needed, reteach expectations, and use consequences proportionally. PBIS does not mean "no consequences."

Use data cycles. Teams should look for patterns by setting, time, behavior, student group, grade, staff need, and intervention response. Data should lead to action.

Check fidelity. If outcomes are poor, first ask whether the system is being implemented as designed. A weak implementation is not evidence that the framework itself failed.

Match supports to function and need. A student seeking peer attention needs a different intervention from a student avoiding work because of skill gaps or anxiety.

Engage families and students. Expectations and supports should be understandable, culturally responsive, and co-owned rather than imposed without context.

## Classroom PBIS

Classroom PBIS applies the same logic inside classrooms. It emphasizes predictable routines, active supervision, high rates of academic engagement, clear expectations, specific feedback, opportunities to respond, instructional correction, and differentiated support.

Classroom PBIS is strongest when aligned with schoolwide expectations. A teacher can still use positive and proactive classroom practices independently, but sustained PBIS depends on schoolwide systems.

Use classroom PBIS resources when the teacher's immediate sphere of control is the classroom: routines, transitions, opportunities to respond, active supervision, feedback, correction, task engagement, and classroom-specific supports. Use schoolwide PBIS work when the problem crosses settings, requires shared expectations, needs leadership-team decisions, depends on data systems, or involves Tier 2/Tier 3 entry and monitoring. A single teacher can improve classroom practice, but cannot fully implement PBIS alone.

## Signs Of Strong Implementation

Strong PBIS is visible when:

- adults share and teach common expectations;
- expectations are defined for real settings and routines;
- students receive specific feedback on expected behavior;
- behavior data is reviewed regularly and acted upon;
- Tier 2 supports are easy to access and progress-monitored;
- Tier 3 supports are individualized and function-informed;
- staff receive coaching and implementation support;
- fidelity and outcomes are both measured;
- discipline data is disaggregated to examine equity;
- families and students understand the system.

## Signs Of Shallow Implementation

Implementation is shallow when:

- PBIS means tickets, points, prizes, or assemblies only;
- expectations are posted but not taught;
- staff responses remain inconsistent;
- data is collected but not used for decisions;
- Tier 2 and Tier 3 supports are missing or delayed;
- teachers are told to be positive without receiving systems support;
- serious behavior is ignored in the name of positivity;
- implementation depends on one enthusiastic coordinator;
- equity is asserted but not examined through data.

## Relationship To Nearby Frameworks

MTSS is the broader multi-tiered system of supports that can include academic, behavioral, social-emotional, and mental-health supports. PBIS is often the behavioral/social-emotional side of MTSS, though PBIS also attends to academic outcomes.

RTI focuses on response to intervention, often in academic domains. PBIS uses similar tiered logic, data, and progress monitoring for behavior and social-emotional support.

Restorative Practices can complement PBIS by strengthening relationships, repairing harm, and building community. PBIS provides prevention and systems infrastructure; restorative work can deepen relational accountability.

CASEL SEL can complement PBIS by identifying social-emotional competencies to teach and practice. PBIS supplies systems for implementation and support.

Trauma-informed teaching can inform how PBIS expectations, responses, and supports are designed so that safety, predictability, agency, and regulation are taken seriously.

## Design Questions For Educators

- What outcomes are we trying to improve?
- What expectations need to be taught, where, and by whom?
- How will adults respond consistently and instructionally?
- What data will show whether the system is working?
- Are discipline and support outcomes equitable across student groups?
- What Tier 2 supports are available within days, not months?
- How do we determine when a student enters, changes, or exits an intervention?
- How will we measure implementation fidelity?
- What support do staff need to implement well?
