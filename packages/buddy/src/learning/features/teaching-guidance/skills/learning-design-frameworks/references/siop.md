# SIOP

SIOP stands for Sheltered Instruction Observation Protocol. It is a lesson planning, teaching, and observation model for integrating content learning with academic language development, especially for multilingual learners and English learners.

The model was developed by Jana Echevarria, Mary Ellen Vogt, and Deborah J. Short through work connected to the Center for Applied Linguistics and CREDE. It organizes sheltered instruction into eight components and thirty features.

The "8:30" shorthand is important: eight interrelated components, with thirty lesson features used for planning, teaching, coaching, and observation. The exact observation protocol should be obtained from authorized SIOP/CAL materials; this reference summarizes the architecture so teachers understand what the model is asking them to attend to.

## The Teaching Problem It Solves

Students learning academic content through a developing language face two simultaneous demands: understanding the subject and using the language of school. A lesson can be conceptually strong but inaccessible if language demands are ignored. A lesson can also support language but fail to teach rigorous content.

SIOP addresses both. It asks teachers to plan content objectives and language objectives, make input comprehensible, build background, provide interaction, teach strategies, and assess both content and language progress.

## The Eight Components

### Lesson Preparation

Effective SIOP lessons make both content objectives and language objectives explicit. Teachers plan materials, tasks, adaptations, and meaningful activities before instruction begins.

The key question: What content will students learn, and what language will they need to use to learn or show it?

### Building Background

Teachers connect new content to students' prior knowledge, cultural and linguistic experiences, and previous learning. They also develop key vocabulary and concepts.

Building background is not a superficial warm-up. It is preparation for comprehension.

### Comprehensible Input

Teachers make content understandable without watering it down. This can involve clear speech, visuals, modelling, gestures, demonstrations, adapted text, examples, repeated exposure, and careful pacing.

The goal is access to grade-level ideas, not simplified thinking.

### Strategies

Students learn cognitive, metacognitive, and language-learning strategies. Teachers provide scaffolds and gradually move students toward independence.

Examples include summarizing, questioning, predicting, clarifying, note-taking, using sentence frames, organizing information, and monitoring comprehension.

### Interaction

Students need structured opportunities to use academic language. Interaction includes teacher-student and student-student talk, discussion, clarification, negotiation of meaning, and extended responses.

The teacher plans grouping and wait time so multilingual learners can participate meaningfully.

### Practice And Application

Students practice content and language through speaking, listening, reading, and writing. Practice should involve hands-on materials, application tasks, and integration of language modalities where appropriate.

### Lesson Delivery

The teacher implements the lesson so content and language objectives remain central. Students should be engaged, pacing should support learning, and activities should connect to the objectives.

### Review And Assessment

Teachers review key vocabulary and content, provide feedback, and assess progress toward both content and language objectives. Assessment should inform next steps.

## Thirty-Feature Architecture

The 30 SIOP features can be understood in teacher-planning language:

Lesson Preparation focuses on whether the lesson has clear content and language objectives, appropriate content concepts, supplementary materials, adaptation for language proficiency, and meaningful activities.

Building Background focuses on connecting concepts to students' background experience, connecting past and new learning, and developing key vocabulary.

Comprehensible Input focuses on speech, explanations, tasks, and techniques that make content accessible without reducing intellectual demand.

Strategies focuses on explicit strategy instruction, scaffolding, and questions or tasks that promote higher-order thinking.

Interaction focuses on frequent opportunities for discussion, purposeful grouping, sufficient wait time, and chances for students to clarify concepts in their developing language.

Practice and Application focuses on hands-on or applied work, use of language and content knowledge, and integration of reading, writing, listening, and speaking.

Lesson Delivery focuses on whether content objectives and language objectives are supported during instruction, whether students are engaged, and whether pacing fits the learners and lesson goals.

Review and Assessment focuses on review of vocabulary and concepts, feedback, and assessment of student progress toward both content and language objectives.

This architecture is more than an observation checklist. It is a way to design instruction so multilingual learners can engage with rigorous content while developing the language needed to participate.

## How A Teacher Applies It

For a content lesson, plan both dimensions:

1. Content objective: What disciplinary idea, skill, or practice will students learn?
2. Language objective: What language function, vocabulary, structure, or discourse move will students need?
3. Background: What experiences, concepts, or vocabulary must be activated or built?
4. Input: How will the content be made comprehensible?
5. Interaction: When will students use academic language, and with whom?
6. Practice: How will students apply content and language together?
7. Assessment: How will the teacher know whether content and language objectives were met?

Language objectives should be specific. "Students will understand vocabulary" is weak. Stronger objectives name the language function and context: explain cause and effect, justify a claim with evidence, compare two processes, ask clarifying questions, summarize a procedure, or use target vocabulary in an oral explanation.

Planning features come first. Before the lesson, clarify content and language objectives, choose concepts and materials, adapt texts or supports, connect to background, identify vocabulary, plan interaction structures, and decide how content and language will be assessed.

Delivery features come during instruction. The teacher makes input comprehensible, explicitly supports strategies, structures interaction, provides practice in language modalities, keeps objectives visible, monitors engagement and pacing, reviews vocabulary and content, gives feedback, and checks both language and content progress.

The two halves depend on each other. A teacher cannot deliver language-aware instruction if the language objective, vocabulary, interaction, and assessment evidence were never planned. A written SIOP plan is also not enough if classroom talk, pacing, scaffolding, and assessment do not support multilingual learners in real time.

## Strong Uses

- Content-area teaching with multilingual learners.
- Lessons where academic language is a barrier to content learning.
- Co-planning between ESL specialists and content teachers.
- Observing or coaching sheltered instruction.
- Designing lessons that require both disciplinary understanding and language production.
- Supporting students in using reading, writing, speaking, and listening for academic purposes.

## Poor Fits And Misuses

- Do not treat SIOP as generic good teaching while ignoring language objectives.
- Do not use language support as a reason to reduce content rigor.
- Do not write vague language objectives that are disconnected from the content task.
- Do not make vocabulary lists the whole language plan.
- Do not assume comprehensible input means oversimplified input.
- Do not treat the observation protocol as paperwork rather than a planning and teaching guide.
- Do not plan content first and add a vague language objective afterward.
- Do not treat interaction as "turn and talk" unless students have the language, purpose, and structure needed to use the talk for learning.

## Evidence Of Good Implementation

- Content and language objectives are clear and connected.
- Students access rigorous content through appropriate supports.
- Academic vocabulary and language functions are taught in context.
- Students have meaningful opportunities to speak, listen, read, and write.
- Interaction is structured, not incidental.
- Assessment includes evidence of both content learning and language use.
- Supports help students participate more independently over time.

## Relationship To Other Frameworks

- SIOP overlaps with UDL and Differentiated Instruction, but it is specifically focused on language-content integration for multilingual learners.
- Explicit Instruction can be used inside SIOP lessons to teach vocabulary, strategies, or procedures.
- Formative Assessment helps monitor both content and language objectives.
- SIOP does not replace subject-specific pedagogy; it makes content pedagogy accessible through language-aware design.
