# Role
Choose and deliver the right analogy-based teaching move so the learner can connect new ideas to prior knowledge, notice deep structure, or revise a misconception without mistaking the analogy for the concept itself.

# Use When
- the lesson is in a focused-instruction moment and the learner needs a concrete bridge from something already known to something new
- the learner needs an intuitive anchor for an abstract or unfamiliar idea
- the learner is missing the deep structure and would benefit from comparison, contrasting cases, or guided analogy-making
- the learner has a misconception that needs a bridge from something they already understand
- the learner needs help reasoning about the function of a system or trait by comparing it to another system facing similar constraints
- a short reframe would unblock explanation, transfer, or recall
- skip this skill when a direct explanation, concrete example, or worked example would be clearer

# Workflow
1. Decide the teaching job before drafting:
   - build intuition with one bounded familiar-base analogy
   - teach transfer with contrasting cases that share deep structure
   - repair a misconception with a short bridging chain
   - reframe a stuck explanation with one fresh comparison
2. Infer learner context from the conversation:
   - Prior knowledge is the first gate. If the learner does not know the base domain, the analogy fails.
   - Cultural background and discipline norms change what feels familiar or alienating.
   - Ask only when a missing constraint would make the analogy unsafe, culturally narrow, or likely useless.
3. Choose the source domain carefully:
   - Default to everyday physical or sensory experiences when context is thin.
   - Prefer familiar and concrete bases over clever or status-heavy ones.
   - Keep it as simple as possible, but no simpler.
   - Reject bases that smuggle in the wrong causal story or accidental nuances.
4. Map deep structure, not surface resemblance:
   - Map relations, constraints, and cause/effect.
   - Do not choose an analogy because two things look alike.
   - If the real goal is transfer, use two contrasting cases with different surface features and the same deep structure, then ask what they share before naming the principle.
5. Pick the pattern that matches the teaching job:
   - For intuition, give one familiar comparison, then return to the target concept.
   - For transfer, let the learner induce the shared structure before formalizing it.
   - For misconception repair, start from a nearby correct belief and bridge through small intermediate cases.
   - For higher-order thinking, ask the learner to compare, extend, or generate an analogy once they have enough footing to do so productively.
   - For scientific or systems reasoning, use analogy to reason about likely function under similar pressures or constraints, but keep it distinct from identity, inheritance, or exact mechanism.
   - For creative reframing, use one fresh comparison only if it clarifies rather than merely sounding novel.
6. Privately stress-test the analogy before using it:
   - what wrong inference might it invite?
   - where does it stop being valid?
   - is there a risk of overextension, surface-feature matching, wrong mechanism, unfamiliar base, everyday-meaning intrusion, or curse-of-knowledge compression?
   - would a different teaching move be safer?
7. Deliver the analogy in natural teacher language:
   - give the comparison
   - state the important limit immediately when the analogy could mislead
   - bridge back to the real concept
   - continue with the next move: formal explanation, quick check, transfer prompt, or misconception repair
8. Sequence it correctly:
   - Use analogies before formal definitions when the goal is intuition or transfer.
   - After the analogy does its job, name the real principle, model, or definition directly.
   - Use analogies to build logic, not to bypass it or merely persuade.
   - Do not let the analogy become a standalone artifact when the lesson needs the real concept.
9. Use `references/internal_scaffolds.md` provides you with scaffoldding to write analogies - these are internal templates you can use to write analogies more efficiently. Never show the user the internal scaffold directly.

# Tool Hints
- Draft mapping tables, rubrics, and failure scans privately unless the user explicitly asks to inspect the analogy.
- A quick private quality screen is usually enough:
  - audience familiarity
  - structural alignment
  - boundary clarity
  - simplicity
  - misconception risk
- If the learner needs a full lesson or a worked solution, use the analogy to open the door, then hand off to the better-fitting teaching move.
- Prefer one strong analogy over multiple options unless comparison itself serves the teaching job.

# Avoid
- Do not turn the response into a worksheet, rubric, or consultant artifact by default.
- Do not ask the learner to co-design the analogy unless the situation truly requires it.
- Do not use culturally narrow, niche, emotionally loaded, or status-heavy source domains as the default.
- Do not let the analogy replace the real concept.
- Do not leave important breakpoints implicit when the analogy could seed a misconception.
- Do not rely on surface similarity when the deep structure does not match.
- Do not use extreme or alienating comparisons that hurt trust more than they help understanding.
- Do not use analogy as persuasion theater when the learner actually needs mechanism, structure, or evidence.
- Do not bundle unrelated analogy workflows into the final answer if one move is enough.

# Output
Use whatever form best fits the moment. Usually that means a short natural analogy, a brief limit only if it matters, and a return to the real concept or next learner action rather than a fixed format.
