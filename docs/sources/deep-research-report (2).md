# When Interaction With a Visualization or Simulation Improves Learning

## Executive finding

The research supports a narrower claim than “interactivity improves learning.” **Interaction is most useful when the learner’s action performs a learning-relevant cognitive job**: controlling a difficult dynamic presentation, intervening on a causal variable, committing to a prediction and testing it, choosing diagnostic experiments, coordinating representations, or constructing and testing a model. Reviews of science simulations generally find beneficial learning effects, but also substantial dependence on the instructional task, representation, guidance, and integration with the surrounding lesson. citeturn1search23turn1search14turn15search20

The cleanest evidence that **interaction itself**, rather than additional content, can help comes from learner control over a dynamic visualization. Mayer and Chandler held the basic multimedia explanation constant while giving learners simple control over when successive segments appeared; learner control improved **transfer/deeper understanding**, although not simple retention. citeturn8search0turn22view0 By contrast, many apparently strong demonstrations of an “interactivity effect” change several things at once. Evans and Gibbons, for example, found better problem solving with an interactive multimedia lesson, but its interactive version simultaneously added **pace control, self-assessment questions, and an interactive simulation**; the experiment therefore cannot identify which component caused the benefit. citeturn22view1

Across the literature, the additional value of interaction is best understood as **making the learner generate evidence rather than merely receive an outcome**. Observation can show *what happened*. Well-designed interaction can additionally let a learner ask *what would happen if I changed this?*, expose the learner's current model before feedback arrives, generate contrasting cases, and connect an action to a visible consequence. Simulation reviews also warn that these opportunities do not automatically become productive inquiry: learners often need help formulating hypotheses, designing informative experiments, and integrating what they see. citeturn15search0turn15search20turn22view7

## How the evidence was separated

For this review, I distinguish three levels of attribution.

**Interaction-isolated evidence** changes primarily whether or how the learner can act while keeping the underlying instructional information approximately constant. This is the strongest basis for saying the *interaction itself* caused a learning difference. Mayer and Chandler's segmented learner-control experiments are the clearest example in the sources reviewed here. citeturn22view0

**Interaction-plus-representation evidence** compares an interactive simulation with a physical laboratory, a less interactive display, or a different simulation design. Such studies can demonstrate that the *interactive environment as designed* is educationally valuable, but interaction is entangled with factors such as making invisible processes visible, eliminating irrelevant manipulation difficulty, changing the form of feedback, or allowing faster repetition. Finkelstein and colleagues' virtual-circuit study and Zacharia's real/virtual experimentation study fall largely into this category. citeturn22view6turn21search11

**Guided-interaction evidence** studies interaction embedded within prediction prompts, explanations, reflection, inquiry scripts, self-assessment, or teacher guidance. These studies are highly relevant to instructional design, but positive outcomes should not be attributed to clicking, dragging, or exploration alone. The broader inquiry literature is especially clear about this: Lazonder and Harmsen's meta-analysis of 72 studies found positive effects of guidance on learning activities, performance success, and learning outcomes, with an overall effect on learning outcomes of **d = 0.50**. citeturn22view7

That distinction matters because a recurring result in the simulation literature is not “maximum freedom works best,” but rather **meaningful agency plus interpretable feedback plus enough structure to make the agency productive**. De Jong and van Joolingen's review of discovery learning with simulations documents difficulties learners encounter in hypothesis generation, experimentation, and interpretation, while later work on interactive representations similarly finds that unstructured interaction can impose demands rather than relieve them. citeturn15search0turn18search8turn18search15

## Six positive learning activities

| Learning activity | What the learner manipulates | What the learner observes | Learning outcome studied | Conditions needed / what the evidence actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Step through a dynamic process at a self-chosen pace** | Timing: the learner clicks to advance through meaningful segments of an animation or process explanation. | The same causal/process sequence, but chunked according to the learner's processing needs. | **Transfer and deeper conceptual understanding**, rather than merely recalling presented facts. | Mayer & Chandler provide relatively clean evidence for the interaction itself: learner pacing improved transfer but did **not** reliably improve retention. The useful action is therefore not “clicking”; it is controlling transience so the learner can finish integrating one stage before the next arrives. citeturn8search0turn22view0 |
| **Change a causal variable and inspect the immediate visual consequence** | A parameter, initial condition, force, velocity, circuit component, connection, or similar model variable. | Motion, a graph or trace, current flow, system state, or another representation that changes contingently with the manipulation. | Conceptual understanding of functional/causal relations and, in some studies, later problem solving or physical-task performance. | The feedback must make the targeted relationship perceptually and conceptually legible. Positive studies often change both **interaction and representation**, so evidence for the whole interactive environment is stronger than evidence for manipulation alone. Rieber et al., for example, explicitly studied a Newtonian simulation in which learners controlled an animated ball while feedback representation and explanations were varied; Finkelstein's circuit simulation explicitly represented electron flow. citeturn20search21turn22view6 |
| **Predict first, then run the simulation and explain the discrepancy** | The learner first externalizes a prediction or hypothesis and then selects or changes simulation conditions to test it. | Whether the resulting trajectory, state, graph, or mechanism agrees with the committed prediction. | Conceptual-model revision, scientific inquiry, and metacognitive awareness of why a prediction succeeded or failed. | The strongest evidence is for **prediction-testing inside guided inquiry**, not for prediction as an isolated interface feature. Inquiry/modeling curricula such as ThinkerTools combine hypothesis testing with modeling and metacognitive reflection, while simulation reviews and the guidance meta-analysis indicate that structured support is important. A prompt to explain *why* the observed outcome differs from the prediction is therefore better supported than a bare “guess, then click Run” interaction. citeturn15search15turn15search24turn15search20turn22view7 |

Reviews report that learners can have difficulty designing productive experiments; guidance that helps formulate hypotheses, choose comparisons, or structure the inquiry generally improves outcomes. Thus the positive activity is **purposeful contrasting**, not unrestricted exploration. citeturn15search0turn15search20turn22view7 |

These results support **construction-plus-feedback environments**, but they do not isolate the motor act of construction from clearer representations, faster experimentation, or reduced apparatus-management demands. citeturn21search3turn22view6turn21search11 |

### Why these activities add more than observation

The six activities provide different forms of information that passive observation generally does not.

**Pacing generates time for cognitive integration.** A continuously presented animation determines the processing schedule for the learner; learner-controlled segmentation lets the learner decide when enough of the current causal step has been processed to advance. The selective result in Mayer and Chandler—better transfer without corresponding retention gains—is important because it points toward construction of a usable mental model rather than simply greater exposure or attention. citeturn22view0

**Manipulation creates intervention evidence.** Watching an outcome reveals a state of the system. Changing a variable and seeing the system respond creates a contrast between states and can therefore make a functional relationship much easier to infer. In virtual circuits, for example, a learner can alter the system while simultaneously seeing a representation of processes that are not directly visible in physical wires. That is an advantage of the simulation's action–representation coupling, not merely of animation. citeturn22view6

**Prediction creates an error signal tied to the learner's own model.** An unexpected animation seen passively can simply be accepted. A result that contradicts an explicit prior prediction identifies a particular belief that needs reconciliation. However, the literature reviewed here does not justify treating prediction alone as a proven causal ingredient; successful implementations typically combine prediction/hypothesis generation with experimentation, explanation, reflection, or teacher/scaffold support. citeturn15search20turn15search24turn22view7

**Controlled experimentation lets learners choose the evidence that distinguishes explanations.** A single observed outcome can be compatible with multiple causal stories. Systematically manipulating one factor while holding others constant can discriminate among them. The important qualification is that novices often do not spontaneously select maximally informative experiments, which is why experiment-design prompts and other forms of inquiry guidance matter. citeturn15search0turn22view7

**Representation manipulation turns integration into an overt task.** Simply placing an equation beside a graph or animation leaves the learner responsible for discovering which elements correspond. Bodemer and colleagues' work indicates that requiring learners to establish those relations can improve learning, and that structured interaction with dynamic representations is more useful than assuming learners will explore systematically on their own. citeturn18search8turn18search15

**Construction externalizes a model.** When learners assemble a circuit or other system, their choices instantiate what they believe about the system. Running that construction then subjects the model to consequences. This can provide a particularly tight loop among *idea → action → visible consequence → revision*. The circuit studies provide unusually compelling evidence that such a virtual loop can transfer beyond the screen, although they still cannot tell us whether the active construction, the explanatory visualization, or their combination deserves most of the credit. citeturn22view6turn21search11

## Where the evidence is mixed or insufficient

### “More interactivity” is not a supported general principle

Evans and Gibbons reported a positive “interactivity effect”: undergraduates using an interactive bicycle-pump lesson performed better on novel problem solving and completed subsequent tests faster. But the interactive treatment simultaneously added three components—**learner pacing, self-assessment questions, and an interactive simulation**—so it is evidence for a productive instructional package, not evidence that generic interface activity is intrinsically beneficial. citeturn22view1

This distinction is consistent with the broader reviews: simulations can be effective educational tools, but effectiveness varies according to instructional role, guidance, task structure, and how the simulation is integrated with other instruction. citeturn1search23turn1search14

### Learner control has selective, not universal, benefits

The strongest interaction-isolated study here does **not** show that pace control improves every form of learning. Mayer and Chandler found advantages principally on transfer/deeper-understanding measures, not retention. It would therefore be an overreach to infer that pause/continue buttons automatically increase memory, or that every animation should be maximally learner-controlled. citeturn8search0turn22view0

A reasonable interpretation is that control is especially useful where the presentation is transient enough that learners need time to integrate successive stages; the evidence does not establish a universal “learner control effect” independent of task characteristics. citeturn22view0

### Unguided manipulation is not reliably productive inquiry

The simulation literature has long documented a basic problem with discovery learning: having the ability to manipulate variables does not guarantee that learners formulate useful hypotheses, conduct diagnostic experiments, interpret data correctly, or connect the results to an underlying model. De Jong and van Joolingen's review specifically treats instructional support as a way to address these difficulties. citeturn15search0turn15search20

The later meta-analysis by Lazonder and Harmsen strengthens that conclusion at the level of inquiry learning more broadly. Across 72 studies, guidance had positive effects on learning activities (**d = 0.66**), performance success (**d = 0.71**), and learning outcomes (**d = 0.50**); there was also meaningful heterogeneity, so the exact optimal guidance cannot be reduced to one universal script. citeturn22view7

Accordingly, **“let students play with the simulation until they discover the rule” has weaker support than “give students agency to investigate a question, plus enough structure to make their experiments informative.”** citeturn15search20turn22view7

### Visual feedback is promising, but its independent effect is rarely isolated

Finkelstein's virtual-circuit environment produced striking outcomes: students using the simulation outperformed peers using real equipment on the conceptual survey and on coordinated tasks requiring them to assemble and explain a real circuit. But the simulation also explicitly represented electron flow, whereas the physical circuit did not make that mechanism directly visible. Thus the study demonstrates the value of the **interactive, representational environment**, not a separable effect of immediate visual feedback alone. citeturn21search3turn22view6

Zacharia's randomized comparison similarly favored incorporating virtual experimentation: 88 undergraduates used the same inquiry-oriented curriculum, while the experimental group substituted virtual experimentation for real experimentation during part of it; conceptual understanding favored the virtual condition in that portion of the curriculum. Yet again, virtual experimentation simultaneously changes manipulation, visibility, repeatability, and feedback. citeturn21search11

The defensible design conclusion is therefore **not** “any immediate animation after a click promotes learning.” It is that visual feedback is valuable when it visibly expresses the conceptual consequence of the learner's action and makes the target mechanism or relationship easier to reason about. citeturn22view6turn18search8

### Multiple representations can help or hinder

Dynamic and interactive multiple representations create an opportunity to see the same concept in complementary forms, but also impose the burden of deciding where to look, how representations correspond, and how an action in one relates to changes in another. Bodemer and colleagues explicitly note that learners may fail to meet these demands; their interventions improved outcomes by making integration itself an active, structured task. citeturn18search8turn18search15

Thus, evidence favors **manipulating correspondences for a purpose** over merely adding more simultaneously moving graphs, equations, diagrams, and animations. citeturn18search8

### Prediction is theoretically compelling but not well isolated experimentally

Prediction-before-feedback appears repeatedly within successful inquiry and modeling approaches because it forces learners to expose an expectation that can be tested. But among the studies reviewed here, the strongest positive evidence comes from broader inquiry/modeling systems, not experiments that differ only in whether learners made a prediction. White and Frederiksen's ThinkerTools work, for example, combines inquiry, modeling, and metacognitive processes rather than isolating prediction; simulation reviews likewise treat hypothesis generation and experimentation as parts of a larger discovery process. citeturn15search15turn15search24turn15search0

The evidence therefore supports **predict → test → compare → explain** as a learning activity more strongly than it supports a claim that the prediction step, by itself, causes the gain. Guidance and reflection remain plausible co-causes. citeturn22view7turn15search20

## Practical interpretation of the evidence

Taken together, the literature suggests a useful criterion for deciding whether a proposed interaction deserves to exist:

> **The learner's action should change what evidence they can generate, what relationship they can perceive, or how deliberately they can process the representation.**

A click whose only function is to reveal an outcome that could just as well have appeared automatically adds little educationally. By contrast, the six activities above have identifiable cognitive functions supported by the research: controlling transience, probing causality, exposing predictions to disconfirmation, generating controlled evidence, coordinating representations, and externalizing/testing a constructed model. citeturn22view0turn15search20turn18search8turn22view6

For instructional design, the evidence favors a loop such as **question or prediction → purposeful manipulation → immediate, conceptually mapped visual consequence → comparison across cases → explanation/reflection**. The empirical basis for the individual pieces is uneven: pacing has relatively direct evidence; active representation integration has experimental evidence but includes structured support; simulation manipulation and construction have strong environment-level evidence; and prediction/controlled experimentation derive much of their support from guided inquiry packages and reviews rather than component-isolation experiments. citeturn22view0turn18search8turn22view6turn22view7

This also explains why interaction can outperform mere observation without implying that physical action itself is educational. The valuable part is usually the **contingency between an intentional learner action and interpretable evidence about the target concept**. When that contingency is weak, when learners do not know what to test, or when the display overwhelms them with uncoordinated information, interactivity can become additional cognitive work rather than additional learning. citeturn15search20turn18search8turn18search15
