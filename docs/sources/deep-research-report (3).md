# When Purpose-Built Static Diagrams and Illustrations Improve Understanding

## Evidence frame

The most defensible conclusion from the research is not that “pictures help learning,” but that **a static visual helps when it makes task-relevant structure perceptually available that learners would otherwise have to construct, search for, or hold in working memory**. The strongest recurring benefits come from showing spatial or causal relations, keeping labels next to what they name, aligning analogous structures so correspondences can be seen, and suppressing surface detail when that detail competes with the relation to be learned. A 2018 meta-analysis of spatial-contiguity and split-attention studies found a substantial overall advantage for integrated over spatially separated presentations, with an estimated effect of *g* = 0.63. citeturn14search19turn14search26

That conclusion is importantly different from an appeal to visual attractiveness. In experiments on the cardiovascular system, simplified and more detailed diagrams both helped learners construct mental models, but the simplified versions were better for factual learning and most strongly supported integration of information during comprehension. citeturn17search11turn17search20 Likewise, experiments with scientific devices found that illustrations helped most when they represented **both the system's parts and the causal steps by which it operates**; merely adding a picture of the parts did not produce the same conceptual and transfer benefits. citeturn18search7turn18search8

The evidence for **realistic detail is much less consistently positive**. In controlled anatomy experiments, realistic rendering was neutral in one study and reduced retention in another, whereas placing labels next to the structures they described consistently helped. citeturn15view1 Other experiments indicate that realistic representations can be useful when their distinctive features are themselves relevant, especially when signaling such as color helps learners locate the important information, but realism does not appear to be a general-purpose route to deeper conceptual understanding. citeturn16search17turn16search20

The six situations below therefore separate **schematic evidence**, where the visual deliberately abstracts or reorganizes the subject matter, from **realistic-imagery evidence**, where naturalistic appearance is retained because perceptual resemblance may itself matter.

## Circulatory biology: simplify the heart to expose the flow structure

**Teaching situation.** When novices are first learning how blood moves through the atria, ventricles, lungs, and body, use a deliberately simplified heart-and-circulation diagram rather than beginning with an anatomically rich rendering. Show the chambers as clearly separated functional regions, use a small number of directional connections for blood flow, place names immediately adjacent to the corresponding structures, and omit texture, irregular anatomical contours, vessels not needed for the explanation, and other detail that does not change the route being reasoned about.

**What the visual is for.** Its job is to let the learner notice a **relational model**: which chamber connects to which, what comes before and after what, and how a complete circulation cycle is organized. The diagram functions as an external model against which the learner can integrate statements in the accompanying explanation. It is not primarily an identification picture of what a dissected heart looks like.

This is unusually direct evidence. Butcher's two experiments compared text alone with text plus simplified diagrams highlighting important structural relationships and text plus more detailed, more anatomically faithful diagrams. Both diagram conditions improved mental-model development relative to text alone. The simplified diagrams best supported factual learning; think-aloud/protocol evidence in the second experiment showed that diagrams promoted inference generation and reduced comprehension errors, while the simplified diagrams most strongly promoted integration of information. citeturn17search11turn17search20 The original publication is Kirsten R. Butcher, “Learning From Text With Diagrams: Promoting Mental Model Development and Inference Generation,” *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 98(1), 182–197, [DOI: 10.1037/0022-0663.98.1.182](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.98.1.182).

**Evidence status: direct, schematic.** The experiment actually manipulated simplified versus more detailed diagrams for the heart/circulatory content, so the recommendation closely matches the evidence. citeturn17search20

**Important limitation.** “Simpler” should not be read as “draw as little as possible.” The successful simplification preserved the structural relations required to construct the correct mental model. Eliminating a necessary connection or spatial relation would turn useful abstraction into misinformation. The study also does not establish that simplified diagrams are superior when the later task is anatomical identification of realistic tissue rather than explanation of circulation. citeturn17search20

## Mechanical physics and engineering: show parts together with causal steps

**Teaching situation.** When explaining how a hydraulic brake, pump, or comparable mechanical system works, provide a compact static schematic that identifies the main components **and** depicts the sequence of functional changes—pressure applied here, fluid displaced there, another component moves, and the output follows. For a system with distinct states, juxtapose two static states such as “before/after” or “off/on,” with consistent component positions and short action labels or arrows.

**What the visual is for.** The learner should be able to trace a causal chain through a physical system and then use that model to answer a new question—for example, “What happens if this valve cannot close?” or “Why does pressing here produce motion there?” A labeled inventory of components is insufficient because conceptual understanding depends on relations among components over the operating sequence.

Mayer and Gallini tested this distinction in three experiments with explanations of scientific devices. Learners encountered no illustration, illustrations emphasizing component **parts**, illustrations emphasizing **steps**, or explanatory illustrations containing both parts and steps. The combined parts-and-steps representations consistently improved recall of conceptual information and creative problem solving, whereas the weaker illustration types did not show the same pattern; the benefit was concentrated mainly among learners with low prior knowledge. The gains were not simply better verbatim retention. citeturn18search7turn18search8 The original publication is Richard E. Mayer and Joan K. Gallini, “When Is an Illustration Worth Ten Thousand Words?”, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 82(4), 715–726, [DOI: 10.1037/0022-0663.82.4.715](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.82.4.715).

Although the paper describes some of the representations as depicting the system's changing states, these were instructional illustrations rather than a claim that animation itself was necessary. What mattered for the reported advantage was that the representation supplied both the system's **structural organization and its causal operating sequence**. citeturn18search7turn18search8

**Evidence status: direct, schematic.** This is strong evidence for a purpose-built explanatory diagram in introductory mechanics or engineering: the useful visual is the one that makes the system's causal organization inspectable. citeturn18search8

**Important limitation.** The effect was not uniform across learners; high-prior-knowledge students gained less. A knowledgeable learner may already be able to generate the causal model from text. The result also argues against adding a technically impressive cutaway merely because it looks informative: a picture that identifies hardware but does not reveal the functional sequence may fail to support the reasoning task. citeturn18search7

## Mathematics: juxtapose analogous solutions so relational correspondences can be mapped

**Teaching situation.** When a learner has solved one kind of problem and needs to recognize the same mathematical structure in a superficially different problem, put the source and target examples **side by side**, preserve a parallel visual layout, and visibly align corresponding quantities, operations, or subgoals. A small number of connecting cues—matching positions, brackets, arrows, or restrained correspondence marks—can direct attention from “these two problems look different” toward “these relations play the same roles.”

For example, a teacher introducing an unfamiliar proportion problem could place it next to a previously mastered proportional situation, arrange the two solution structures identically, and align the terms that occupy equivalent relational roles. The purpose is not to illustrate the story contexts; it is to make the **mapping between relational structures** easy to inspect.

Richland, Zur, and Holyoak's research on analogy in mathematics education emphasizes that analogical learning requires learners to map systematic correspondences between a familiar source and a new target, and identifies supports such as simultaneous availability, spatial alignment, and explicit guidance to correspondences as important aids to that process. citeturn21search0turn21search16 The original publication is Lindsey E. Richland, Osnat Zur, and Keith J. Holyoak, “Cognitive Supports for Analogies in the Mathematics Classroom,” *Science*, 316, 1128–1129, [DOI: 10.1126/science.1142103](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1142103).

**Evidence status: indirect for a stand-alone static diagram.** This is an important qualification. Richland and colleagues studied supports for analogy in mathematics teaching rather than running a clean experiment in which an isolated, purpose-built static diagram was the sole manipulated variable. Their evidence supports the cognitive operation the proposed visual is designed to enable—**mapping corresponding relations**—but not the precise claim that a particular side-by-side worksheet graphic will, by itself, produce the observed benefit. citeturn21search16

There is a second reason to keep the visual relational rather than richly contextual. Kaminski, Sloutsky, and Heckler reported better transfer of a newly learned mathematical structure after instruction with abstract, generic examples than after concrete examples, suggesting that surface features can sometimes bind learning too closely to the training context. citeturn21search11turn21search35 Their original paper is “The Advantage of Abstract Examples in Learning Math,” *Science*, 320, 454–455, [DOI: 10.1126/science.1154659](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154659).

That finding should **not** be generalized into “abstract is always better.” A critical replication and extension by Trninic, Kapur, and Sinha found that relatively modest improvements to the concrete examples eliminated the supposed general advantage: participants learning from improved concrete examples performed as well as or better than those receiving abstract examples. The authors argue that the original effect depended substantially on the quality of the concrete instantiations. citeturn13search6turn13search26 See Dragan Trninic, Manu Kapur, and Tanmay Sinha, “The Disappearing ‘Advantage of Abstract Examples in Learning Math’,” *Cognitive Science*, 44, e12851, [DOI: 10.1111/cogs.12851](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12851).

**Important limitation.** The useful principle is therefore **relational alignment, not maximal abstraction**. Concrete information is a problem when it draws attention away from the invariant mathematical relationship; well-designed concrete representations can perform very well. citeturn13search6

## Chemistry: use a particle-level before/after schematic to make conservation inspectable

**Teaching situation.** When students are asked to reason about a chemical change that they can observe macroscopically but cannot see microscopically, provide a static before/after particulate diagram. Keep the same atom types visually invariant across the two panels, spatially group atoms into molecules, and arrange the panels so learners can count or match what is conserved while observing which bonds or groupings change. Place short labels adjacent to any representational convention students may not already understand.

A useful example is a reaction for which students must explain why matter is conserved even though the visible substances have changed. A purpose-built particle diagram can make it possible to notice simultaneously that the **number and identity of atoms remain constant while their combinations change**. That is a reasoning affordance that neither a photograph of the reaction vessel nor the symbolic equation alone necessarily makes perceptually immediate.

Chemistry education has explicitly used particulate diagrams to connect the visible world of substances with an otherwise invisible particle model; Pentecost, Weber, and Herrington's formal chemistry-education publication describes this use of particulate representations as a means of deepening understanding. citeturn7search7 The broader experimental literature on instructional graphics also supports two elements of the proposed design: simplified diagrams can make structural relations easier to integrate, and labels placed close to their referents outperform designs that require learners to search between separated text and graphics. citeturn17search20turn14search26

**Evidence status: indirect for this exact design.** The retained evidence is substantially stronger for the **general visual mechanisms**—externalizing relations and integrating labels—than for the exact “before/after particle schematic” specified here. The chemistry publication is discipline-specific, but it should not be treated as equivalent to the tightly controlled diagram-versus-no-diagram experiments in the cardiovascular and mechanical-system cases. citeturn7search7turn14search26

**Important limitation.** Particle drawings introduce their own representational conventions. A learner can mistakenly infer that particle size, spacing, color, or two-dimensional position is literally realistic. The diagram therefore needs to be explicitly presented as a model whose geometry is chosen to make conservation and rearrangement visible. The case for **realistic molecular imagery** is much weaker when the teaching objective is conservation or stoichiometric reasoning, because realistic appearance would not add the relation the learner needs to inspect. This conclusion is an inference from the diagram and abstraction literature rather than a direct realistic-versus-schematic chemistry trial in the evidence reviewed here. citeturn17search20turn13search6

## Medical anatomy: integrate labels directly into a restrained anatomical schematic

**Teaching situation.** When novices must learn the names and spatial locations of structures in a knee joint, stomach, or similar anatomical region, use one or a small number of clear views with each label placed close to the structure it names. Leader lines should terminate unambiguously; labels should not be moved into a remote key merely to make the page look cleaner. Unless realistic shading is itself necessary for the later task, begin with a schematic rendering that keeps boundaries and shapes distinct.

**What the visual is for.** The benefit here is largely attentional and spatial: the learner can bind a verbal name to the right structure without repeatedly searching between an image and a distant legend. That leaves more capacity for learning the anatomical relations themselves rather than performing visual lookup.

Skulmowski directly manipulated this design in two anatomy experiments. In the first experiment, participants learned knee anatomy from either schematic or realistic renderings, with labels either integrated next to the structures or spatially separated. Separated labels significantly increased extraneous cognitive load and reduced retention; realism did not significantly improve retention. citeturn15view1 In the second experiment, using stomach anatomy, split attention again reduced retention and near transfer. Realistic rendering additionally produced a negative effect on retention, while its effect on near transfer was not significant. citeturn15view1 The original publication is Alexander Skulmowski, “Realistic Details Impact Learners Independently of Split-Attention Effects,” *Cognitive Processing*, 24, 187–198, [DOI: 10.1007/s10339-022-01123-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10339-022-01123-z).

These experiments agree with the broader meta-analysis: across many instructional contexts, putting mutually dependent verbal and pictorial information together is preferable to forcing visual search between separated sources; Schroeder and Cenkci estimated an overall advantage of *g* = 0.63 for integrated designs. citeturn14search19turn14search26 See Noah L. Schroeder and Ada T. Cenkci, “Spatial Contiguity and Spatial Split-Attention Effects in Multimedia Learning Environments: a Meta-Analysis,” *Educational Psychology Review*, 30, 679–701, [DOI: 10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9).

**Evidence status: direct for label placement; direct and cautionary for realism.** Few of the recommendations here require extrapolation: the knee and stomach experiments directly compared integrated versus separated labels and schematic versus realistic renderings. citeturn15view1

**Important limitation.** Integrated labeling can itself clutter a very dense figure. The research supports reducing unnecessary search, not covering a diagram with text. For a structure with dozens of labels, several purpose-built regional diagrams may therefore be more defensible than one encyclopedic plate; that specific “split into multiple diagrams” recommendation is an inference from the split-attention evidence rather than a directly tested result in these experiments. citeturn14search26turn15view1

## Realistic imagery: retain detail when realistic recognition is itself part of the learning target

**Teaching situation.** In health-professions or biological identification instruction where students will eventually have to recognize a structure in a naturally variable, textured, or shaded appearance, a realistic image can have a legitimate instructional role—but it should be **purpose-built around the diagnostic or identification features that must transfer to the real task**. A good static visual would preserve the relevant realistic texture or shape while using restrained signaling to direct attention to the feature that distinguishes one structure from another.

The crucial distinction is between two goals. If the learner needs to understand **relations or mechanisms**, realism often contributes little and can compete with the intended structure. If the learner needs to recognize **what the real thing looks like**, removing all realistic cues risks removing part of the eventual task itself. Research by Skulmowski and Rey has reported concreteness-specific learning/testing effects consistent with realistic features serving as retrieval cues, while earlier work found that realistic visualizations benefited from color signaling when important detail otherwise had to be located among realistic features. citeturn16search17turn16search20 The relevant original publications are Alexander Skulmowski and Günter Daniel Rey, “Realistic Details in Visualizations Require Color Cues to Foster Retention,” *Computers & Education*, 122, 23–31, [DOI: 10.1016/j.compedu.2018.03.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2018.03.012), and Skulmowski and Rey, “Realism as a Retrieval Cue: Evidence for Concreteness-Specific Effects of Realistic, Schematic, and Verbal Components of Visualizations on Learning and Testing,” *Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies*, 3, 283–295, [DOI: 10.1002/hbe2.209](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbe2.209). citeturn16search17turn16search20

**Evidence status: indirect for realistic clinical or specimen recognition.** The positive teaching situation above goes beyond what these studies directly establish. They show that realism can interact with signaling and retrieval conditions; they do **not** establish a broad causal rule that realistic instructional pictures improve later clinical diagnosis, specimen identification, or other authentic perceptual expertise. That application is a reasoned extrapolation from concreteness-specific and retrieval-cue findings. citeturn16search20

The caution is substantial. In Skulmowski's later controlled anatomy experiments, realistic detail failed to improve retention in the knee experiment and actually reduced retention in the stomach experiment. The author concluded that the learning effects of realism remain difficult to predict; importantly, the very robust problem in those experiments was split attention, not lack of realism. citeturn15view1 Thus realistic imagery should be selected because **realistic perceptual information is part of what the learner must eventually notice**, not because photographic or three-dimensional appearance seems inherently more educational.

## Synthesis and source set

Across these situations, the evidence supports a functional test for deciding whether a static visual is worth making: **What relation, correspondence, location, or perceptual distinction becomes easier to see in the image than in prose alone?** When there is a precise answer, a purpose-built illustration often has a strong rationale. When the answer is merely that the material looks more vivid, concrete, polished, or lifelike, the evidence is much weaker. Butcher's cardiovascular experiments and Mayer and Gallini's mechanical-system experiments are especially strong demonstrations: the successful images selected and organized information needed to construct a mental or causal model, rather than maximizing pictorial detail. citeturn17search20turn18search8

| Teaching situation | Critical visual property | What it enables the learner to notice/reason about | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Circulatory biology | Simplified structure, clear flow relations | Route and organization of circulation; integration of facts into a mental model | **Direct schematic evidence** citeturn17search20 |
| Mechanical physics/engineering | Parts + causal steps; aligned states | How component actions produce subsequent events; transfer to novel problems | **Direct schematic evidence** citeturn18search8 |
| Mathematics analogy | Side-by-side structural alignment | Correspondences between relationally equivalent problems | **Indirect for a stand-alone static diagram** citeturn21search16 |
| Chemistry particles | Before/after abstraction; invariant atom identities | Conservation plus rearrangement at an invisible level | **Indirect for the exact proposed layout** citeturn7search7turn14search26 |
| Medical anatomy | Labels adjacent to structures; restrained detail | Correct name-location binding without visual search | **Direct schematic/layout evidence** citeturn15view1turn14search26 |
| Realistic identification | Task-relevant realistic cues + signaling | Perceptual resemblance and retrieval of naturalistic features | **Mixed; application to authentic recognition is indirect** citeturn16search17turn16search20turn15view1 |

The **schematic evidence is therefore considerably firmer than a general case for realism**. Schematics have direct experimental support when they expose causal structure, suppress irrelevant detail, organize multiple states, and integrate labels with their referents. citeturn17search20turn18search8turn14search26 Realism has a narrower justification: it may supply useful discriminative or retrieval cues when those cues correspond to the eventual task, but controlled anatomy studies show that additional realism can also be neutral or harmful for retention. citeturn15view1turn16search20

The abstraction literature gives one final qualification. It would be a mistake to convert “schematic often works” into “make every visual maximally abstract.” Kaminski and colleagues found a transfer advantage for generic mathematical examples, but Trninic and colleagues' critical replication showed that improved concrete examples could match or outperform the abstract condition. citeturn21search35turn13search6 The better design rule is: **preserve the information needed for the intended inference and remove, reorganize, or signal information that competes with that inference**.
