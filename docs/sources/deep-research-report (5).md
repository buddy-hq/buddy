# When Graphs Improve Quantitative Understanding: Evidence for Teaching with Function Graphs and Empirical Data Plots

## Executive summary

The research does **not** support a blanket rule that quantitative explanations become better when graphed. Reviews of graph comprehension instead converge on a task-dependent account: graphs are most useful when the learner must perceive or reason about **relations among values**—shape, rate of change, covariation, distribution, or uncertainty—whereas success also depends on the display's visual structure, the learner's graph knowledge, and domain knowledge. Graph format and the instructional question therefore have to be matched; simply adding a graph is not an instructional treatment in itself. citeturn8search17turn8search6turn8search1

I found **five positive teaching situations with sufficiently direct evidence** to recommend conditionally. One concerns **function graphs generated from symbolic functions**; the other four concern **empirical-data plots**. The clearest classroom evidence for function graphs comes from an intermediate-algebra comparison in which a graphing-calculator curriculum substantially improved function modeling, interpretation, translation among representations, and “reification” of functions, while producing **no detectable advantage on a conventional algebra final or mathematics attitude**. citeturn4view0turn5view0turn5view1 For empirical data, especially compelling mechanisms are immediate sensor-to-graph correspondence in motion experiments, restructuring data into distributions rather than isolated cases, carefully scaffolded views of bivariate covariation, and sampling-based displays of uncertainty. citeturn18view2turn19view0turn19view4turn23view0

The most defensible generalization is therefore **“plot when the learning target is relational or distributional structure, and design the plot around that target,” not “plot whenever numbers appear.”** The strongest feature-level findings in this literature concern linked representations, temporal alignment, aggregation/restructuring, and the representation and pacing of uncertainty. In contrast, **axis scaling, annotation, and labeling are clearly important to graph comprehension in reviews, but were rarely manipulated independently in the classroom intervention studies examined here**; for those features, the evidence does not justify pretending that there is one empirically established optimal setting. citeturn8search17turn8search6

## Function-graph teaching situation: make a function visible across representations

**Learner task.** A particularly defensible use of a function graph is when the student is not merely being asked to compute \(f(x)\), but to answer questions such as: *What kind of object is this function? What does its equation imply about its global behavior? How would the same relationship appear as a table or graph? How do I model a contextual relationship with it?* This is precisely the class of tasks studied by Hollar and Norwood in intermediate algebra. Their graphing-approach students used TI-82 calculators and a curriculum organized around exploration and movement among equations, tables, and graphs, while the comparison curriculum emphasized conventional paper-and-pencil procedures. citeturn4view0

**Why the plot helps.** The plausible mechanism is not “pictures are easier.” It is **representational coordination**. A computed list of \(x,f(x)\) pairs exposes local values one at a time; the graph spatially externalizes how those values cohere into a single relationship. Rapid recomputation and plotting also let learners vary a formula and immediately inspect invariant and changing properties. The authors themselves interpreted the gains in translating and interpreting as related to students' routine movement among multiple representations, and the reification gain as possibly related to prolonged access to those representations. citeturn4view0turn5view1

**Measured learning.** The study enrolled 90 low-placement college intermediate-algebra students, 46 in the graphing treatment and 44 in control sections. Students in the graphing condition scored higher on every component of O'Callaghan's Function Test: modeling, interpreting, translating, and reifying; the multivariate treatment effect was significant, \(F(4,69)=4.68,\ p<.01\). The reported total means were 21.02 versus 15.62 out of 37. Using the reported means and SDs gives an approximate standardized difference of about **one pooled standard deviation** on the total test; because the paper did not report that standardized effect itself and test completion may differ from enrollment, it should be treated as a descriptive reconstruction, not an author-reported causal effect size. citeturn4view0turn5view0

The specificity of the result matters. There was **no significant treatment difference on the department's conventional algebra final and no significant difference in mathematics attitude**. In other words, graph-rich instruction improved the aspects of function understanding it was designed to make visible, not every mathematically adjacent outcome. citeturn5view1 A separate upper-secondary comparison by Ruthven likewise found markedly stronger graph-to-symbol “symbolization” performance among students for whom a graphing calculator was a normal tool, providing convergent evidence, although that study was not a randomized trial. citeturn2view0

**Features that matter.** The evidence directly favors **fast linked representations and learner-controlled graph generation**. It does *not* isolate graph scale, axis range, labels, annotations, or point-sampling density; those details should be recorded as **unspecified causal contributors**, not retroactively credited for the learning gain. The major limitation is treatment confounding: the graphing group received a different textbook/curriculum, different representational routines, and graphing technology, so the study supports an instructional package in which graphs play a central role rather than an isolated effect of drawing a curve. citeturn4view0turn5view1

## Empirical-data teaching situations

**Real-time motion data: connect change in the world to change on the axes.**  
The learner is trying to answer *“When I speed up, slow down, reverse, or stand still, what changes on a distance–time or velocity–time graph?”* This is a special case in which the time series is empirical—generated by a sensor—even though it resembles a mathematical function graph. Research therefore supports treating it as an **empirical-data plot**, not as evidence about graphing arbitrary symbolic functions. Brasell found that even a single class period using a motion microcomputer-based laboratory improved high-school physics students' comprehension of distance and velocity graphs relative to a pencil-and-paper graph-construction treatment. A follow-up description of the experiment reports that delaying the graph by only about 20 seconds diminished the advantage. citeturn21search8turn21search25

The mechanism is unusually concrete: the learner experiences the event and its graphical trace at essentially the same time, reducing the inferential gap between “what happened” and “what this mark means.” Mokros and Tinker identified four mutually reinforcing characteristics of microcomputer-based laboratories: multiple modalities, real-time pairing of events with graphical representations, authentic investigation, and removal of the mechanical burden of constructing the graph. In their three-month longitudinal study, middle-school students improved significantly across 16 graphing items even though lessons targeted science content rather than graph-reading instruction. citeturn18view2

This is a case where **interactivity and timing are more clearly implicated than decorative graph design**. The evidence does not establish an optimal y-axis scale or annotation scheme; the important manipulated contrast was temporal coupling. The work is also several decades old, effect sizes are not reported in the indexed abstracts, and the technology changes several things simultaneously—physical engagement, automatic plotting, feedback speed, and opportunity for repeated trials. The result is best interpreted as support for **immediate sensor-to-plot feedback when the conceptual target is change over time**, not as proof that automatically generated graphs always beat hand construction. citeturn18view2turn21search25

**Distribution: make the aggregate visible when individual cases conceal it.**  
For univariate empirical data, a plot becomes especially useful when the learner's question changes from *“What is each value?”* to *“What does the collection look like?”* That includes location, spread, clusters, gaps, outliers, overall shape, and expectations about what might remain stable in a repeated sample. A substantial design-research tradition found that learners often need instructional support to make this shift from cases to aggregate distribution. citeturn11search6turn14search1

The productive displays did more than put dots on axes. In Cobb and colleagues' instructional tools, students could partition observations into equal-width intervals or equal-sized groups and **hide the individual observations**, so that proportions and aggregate structure became the object of attention. Interviews after the preceding seventh-grade sequence found that 19 of 29 students could use equal-interval and four-equal-group displays with the underlying points hidden to build effective data-based arguments. Early in the next year's work, all but a few students routinely chose to hide individual points during analyses—a qualitative behavioral marker of a shift away from case-by-case inspection. citeturn19view4

Related work by Bakker and Gravemeijer followed seventh-grade students through a progression in reasoning about distribution using specially designed software, student-produced graphs, and prediction tasks; students began coordinating overall “bump” or shape with outliers and sample size. Petrosino, Lehrer, and Schauble's work with **22 fourth graders** similarly investigated how measurement error and experimental variation could be structured as distribution rather than dismissed as bad individual measurements, while Lehrer and Schauble documented late-elementary students using distributional form to reason about mechanisms underlying plant-growth data. citeturn11search6turn13search3turn14search1

Here **sampling density matters conceptually rather than through a simple numerical rule**. In the Cobb sequence, students analyzed stacks of 50 reaction-time measurements and discussed whether shape would persist with repeated samples of 50 or 100, connecting a visible empirical distribution with the idea of stability under resampling. But none of these studies establishes “50 points” as optimal; the number is part of the instructional setting, not an experimentally optimized density. citeturn19view3turn19view4 Likewise, the studies support reordering, grouping, hiding/revealing cases, and prediction as mechanisms, while exact effects of axis truncation, labeling, or annotation remain unspecified.

The evidential limitation is substantial: these are rich design studies rather than tightly controlled graph-versus-table trials. They are strong evidence that certain representations can **support a documented learning trajectory** and illuminate mechanisms of conceptual change, but weak evidence for a population-average treatment effect attributable solely to the plotted marks. citeturn16search10turn14search1

**Bivariate relationships: scaffold the scatterplot so noise is part of the relationship.**  
The learner question is *“When X increases, what happens to Y—not for every individual case, but to the distribution of possible Y values?”* A conventional scatterplot can in principle answer that question, yet Cobb, McClain, and Gravemeijer's 14-week eighth-grade experiment is particularly useful because it shows why a scatterplot alone may **fail**. Their students initially tended to “eyeball” the points and reduce the cloud to a deterministic line: variability around the relationship was treated as something to be ignored rather than as part of the statistical relationship. citeturn19view4turn19view5

The productive intervention was to make **conditional distributions perceptually explicit**. Their software partitioned the bivariate space into slices and offered grid, two-equal-group, and four-equal-group views. Each vertical slice could be treated as a univariate distribution. The researchers deliberately postponed formal line fitting because they wanted students first to understand a bivariate distribution as a distribution of conditional distributions. citeturn19view0

This is an unusually informative negative-to-positive transition. The authors write that asking students to analyze ordinary scatterplots when the tool was first introduced was counterproductive; their instructional agenda began to succeed when they returned to stacked data and oriented discussion toward the **shape and density of the stacks**. Students then began using ideas such as hills, humps, clusters, and areas of relative density to describe structure. citeturn19view0turn19view4

The outcome evidence is qualitative rather than a standardized test gain. The experiment involved 41 approximately 40-minute sessions over 14 weeks in an urban middle school. The researchers had hoped to continue with the 29 students from the preceding seventh-grade study; after transfers and scheduling losses, 16 volunteered and **11 participated through the experiment**. That small, self-selected group and the continuously adapted design rule out a conventional causal-effect interpretation. Its value is mechanistic: it demonstrates that **the right graph for learning covariation may initially be a structured distributional view, not the canonical scatterplot itself**. citeturn18view4turn19view2

**Uncertainty: show plausible repeated outcomes when the task is probabilistic judgment.**  
The learner or novice viewer is asking a different kind of question: *“Given noisy data, which underlying trend is more plausible, and how much variation could sampling alone produce?”* Static point estimates with error bars require the viewer to map an abstract graphical interval onto a statistical concept. Hypothetical outcome plots instead show a sequence of possible samples from the underlying distribution. In effect, probability is represented through **experienced frequency** rather than only through a summary interval. citeturn23view0

Kale, Nguyen, Kay, and Hullman tested this idea in two controlled crowdsourced experiments. In Experiment 1 they recruited 62 U.S. MTurk Masters workers and retained **50 after preregistered exclusions and sampling procedures**. Each participant made repeated judgments about whether a noisy monthly jobs series was more consistent with growth or no growth, using either error bars or animated HOPs. The outcome was a psychometric “just-noticeable difference”: how much statistical evidence was needed to reach roughly 75% correct discrimination. HOPs lowered this threshold by an estimated **0.68 log-likelihood units**, 95% CI −1.19 to −0.14, \(t=-2.49,\ p=.02\). citeturn24view1turn23view2

The graph-feature result is unusually specific. Experiment 1 displayed each hypothetical sample for 1,500 ms. Experiment 2 compared static ensembles of **50 lines** against line HOPs presented at 400 ms or 100 ms per hypothetical sample. At 400 ms, HOPs again lowered the discrimination threshold relative to the static line ensemble, estimate −1.22, 95% CI −2.18 to −0.26, \(p=.01\). At 100 ms, the estimated advantage was smaller and unreliable, −0.74, 95% CI −1.69 to 0.22, \(p=.13\). Thus “animation” is not the operative recommendation by itself; **sampling representation plus a usable temporal rate** appears to matter. citeturn24view4turn23view3

There were also meaningful nulls. Psychometric-function spread and confidence-fitness measures did not reliably differ across the Experiment 2 visualization conditions, and faster HOPs reduced reported confidence relative to line ensembles. The authors also observed practice/order effects in Experiment 1 and explicitly warn that an experiment designed to measure *learning* should ideally be longitudinal and separate practice from visualization effects. These experiments therefore constitute relatively strong evidence about **immediate uncertainty judgment by novice adults**, not direct evidence of durable classroom learning. citeturn23view2turn24view4

## What graph features actually matter

Across these studies, the graph features with the best direct support are those that change the **mapping between the learner's question and the structure made perceptually available**. Reviews reinforce this task-centered view: graph comprehension depends jointly on visual characteristics, the graph-reading task, graph knowledge, and subject-matter knowledge. citeturn8search17turn8search6

| Feature | What the evidence supports | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale and axes** | Axes and scale are part of the visual characteristics known to condition graph comprehension, so they must make the relevant comparison perceptible. citeturn8search17turn8search6 | The intervention studies above generally did **not independently randomize axis range, aspect ratio, or truncation**. No universal “best scale” follows from them. |
| **Linked representations** | Strongest in the function-graph study: quickly coordinating equation, table, and graph coincided with large gains on interpretation and translation. citeturn4view0turn5view1 | It is not known how much of the benefit came from the graph itself versus curriculum, calculator access, exploration, or teacher practice. |
| **Annotation and labels** | Reviews imply that visual decoding demands and graph knowledge matter, so labels must support—not compete with—the intended interpretation. citeturn8search17 | Annotation was not an isolated treatment variable in the principal classroom studies here; claims about an optimal amount or style are **unspecified by this evidence**. |
| **Interactivity** | Useful when interaction exposes a consequential relationship: change an equation and observe the graph; move physically and see the trace; regroup or hide observations and inspect the resulting distribution. citeturn4view0turn18view2turn19view4 | The research does not show that interactive graphs are generically superior to well-designed static ones. Interactivity is usually bundled with tasks and feedback. |
| **Sampling density / aggregation** | Enough observations can make distributional structure and variability available; restructuring or suppressing individual points can help learners attend to aggregates. Static over-aggregation can also be inferior to well-paced sampling-based uncertainty displays. citeturn19view4turn24view4 | There is no general optimal number of points. “More data on screen” is not a demonstrated learning principle. |
| **Uncertainty representation** | Repeated hypothetical samples can outperform error bars and static ensembles on some probabilistic trend judgments; representational form and temporal rate matter. citeturn23view2turn24view4 | HOPs did not improve every measure, very fast animation lost the reliable advantage, and immediate judgment is not equivalent to durable statistical learning. |
| **Temporal alignment** | When graph meaning is grounded in a changing physical event, immediate sensor-to-graph correspondence is better supported than delayed display. citeturn21search25turn18view2 | This does not imply that every animation should be instantaneous; HOP research shows that excessively fast presentation can itself hurt. citeturn24view4 |

A useful distinction emerges. **Function graphs** benefit when plotting exposes the global behavior implicit in computed or symbolic values and supports translation between representations. **Empirical-data plots** benefit when plotting makes collective properties of observations—temporal change, aggregate shape, noisy covariation, or uncertainty—available to perception. Those are related but not interchangeable instructional mechanisms. citeturn4view0turn18view2turn19view0

## Limits, null findings, and when a graph is not automatically better

First, graphs should not be added merely because quantities are present. The major reviews characterize graph comprehension as an interaction among **task, display, reader, and domain**, which implies that a graph whose spatial structure does not answer the learner's actual question can add decoding work rather than remove it. citeturn8search6turn8search17 In particular, the evidence assembled here is strongest for relational questions—*how does it vary, what is the shape, how are variables related, how uncertain is the pattern*—rather than for simple retrieval or arithmetic on isolated exact values.

Second, a familiar canonical graph can be pedagogically premature. Cobb and colleagues provide an unusually direct example: early scatterplot use led students to collapse noisy data into deterministic trend lines, and the researchers judged this phase counterproductive. Structured stacks and conditional distributions were needed before the ordinary scatterplot could function as intended. citeturn19view0turn19view4 Thus “use a scatterplot to teach relationship” is too crude a recommendation; the learner must already possess, or be helped to construct, an aggregate interpretation.

Third, benefits are often **outcome-specific**. Hollar and Norwood found substantial function-concept gains but no significant advantage on a conventional algebra final or attitude measure. citeturn5view1 Kale and colleagues found better discrimination of uncertain trends with appropriately paced HOPs but no reliable benefit for several other psychophysical/confidence measures, and a 100-ms presentation rate failed to outperform static ensembles reliably. citeturn24view4 These are important checks against interpreting any statistically significant graph advantage as general “deeper understanding.”

Finally, much of the strongest classroom mechanism evidence comes from **design experiments** rather than randomized trials. Such studies are particularly good at showing *how* learners' reasoning changes and which representational obstacles arise, but their small, context-specific samples and adaptive instruction make causal effect sizes difficult to estimate. Conversely, the uncertainty experiments have better experimental control but measure short-term judgments by online adults rather than school learning. The overall evidence is therefore strongest for **specific, task-matched uses of graphs**, not for a general graph-first pedagogy. citeturn18view4turn24view1
