# When Geometrically Accurate Figures Improve Learning of Spatial and Mathematical Relationships

## Scope and bottom line

The research supports a more precise claim than “visuals help.” **Geometrically accurate figures are most useful when their accuracy preserves the mathematical relationships the learner is supposed to reason about**—for example, incidence of a point on a line, equality of lengths or angles, parallelism or perpendicularity, correspondence under a transformation, or the mapping between a three-dimensional object and its two-dimensional views. What matters is usually **relational or constraint accuracy**, not pictorial realism or a diagram being drawn globally “to scale.” This distinction is consistent with Fischbein’s account of geometrical figures as simultaneously visual and conceptual: the image can stimulate reasoning, but the conceptual constraints of the geometric object have to govern the reasoning. citeturn21view3

The clearest empirical literature comes from **dynamic geometry environments** such as Cabri, where a construction can maintain specified geometric dependencies while a learner drags points. That makes these environments unusually informative for the present question: changing the appearance of a figure while preserving its defining relations lets students distinguish **invariants** from accidental visual features. Jones’s longitudinal study, for example, found that 12-year-olds’ explanations of constructed quadrilaterals could develop from everyday, imprecise descriptions through software-mediated reasoning toward mathematical explanations that went beyond the software. citeturn20view1

There is an important evidential qualification. Most studies do **not** randomly compare an accurately constructed version of a figure against an otherwise identical inaccurate or schematic version. They compare dynamic-geometry instruction with worksheets or traditional instruction, or they analyze students working with exact constructions. Thus, the literature gives reasonably good evidence for the educational value of **constraint-preserving figures plus interaction**, but much weaker causal evidence that geometric exactness *alone* produced the gains. A systematic review and meta-analysis of nine quasi-experimental dynamic-geometry studies, involving 587 participants, found a large overall advantage over traditional instruction (standardized mean difference 1.02, 95% CI 0.56–1.48), but that treatment bundles accuracy with dragging, immediate feedback, measurement, task design, and often different classroom interaction. citeturn21view0turn21view1

That distinction drives the recommendations below. I treat a property as **accuracy-critical** when the learner’s intended inference would cease to be valid if that property were represented incorrectly. A property can remain **schematic** when it is irrelevant to the inference and can legitimately vary without changing the mathematical object under study. This is a synthesis of the empirical work rather than a claim that each individual property has been separately manipulated in experiments. citeturn21view3turn20view1

## What “accurate” needs to mean in a teaching figure

For instructional geometry, four kinds of fidelity should be separated.

**Incidence and dependency accuracy** concerns which points lie on which lines or circles, which lines meet at which intersections, whether a point is genuinely a midpoint, and whether one constructed object depends on another. In a dynamic construction these dependencies are especially consequential: when a free point moves, dependent points must continue to satisfy their defining relations. Jones’s work on students constructing and discussing quadrilaterals illustrates why this matters: the mathematical meaning can emerge from what remains true as the constructed object changes, rather than from the appearance of a single frozen drawing. citeturn20view1

**Metric accuracy** concerns equal lengths, equal angles, perpendicularity, distances from a point or line, and numerical measurements. It is critical when students are explicitly reasoning from those quantities. González and Herbst found that students who dragged and measured dynamic diagrams developed what the authors characterize as a “measure-preserving” conception of congruency and shifted attention from whole-shape appearances toward properties. citeturn20view3

**Transformation accuracy** concerns the correspondence between a preimage and image and the invariant relations induced by a reflection, rotation, translation, or other transformation. Güven’s quasi-experiment is especially relevant here because eighth graders learning transformation geometry in a dynamic geometry environment outperformed a group receiving the same topic through dotted and isometric worksheets on both an achievement test and a test of learning levels. The study demonstrates an advantage for the dynamic treatment, although it does not isolate exact transformation geometry from interactivity. citeturn19view1

**Projection or view accuracy** concerns the mapping between spatial objects and their two-dimensional representations. In Baki, Kösa, and Güven’s quasi-experiment with preservice mathematics teachers, Cabri 3D instruction and physical manipulatives both produced greater spatial-visualization gains than traditional instruction, while the dynamic-geometry group performed better than the physical-manipulative group specifically on the “views” portion of the Purdue Spatial Visualization Test. This is unusually pertinent evidence for learning relationships among spatial objects and projections/views. citeturn19view0

By contrast, **screen position, color, line thickness, labels’ typography, and usually the object’s global orientation** need not be geometrically faithful unless the task specifically makes one of them meaningful. Absolute size can also be schematic when only ratios, incidence, angle relations, or shape class matter. Indeed, allowing such irrelevant properties to change can be pedagogically useful because it makes invariant properties easier to separate from contingent appearance. This interpretation accords with the dynamic-geometry studies, in which learners reason across changing instances rather than treating the first visible configuration as the definition. citeturn20view1turn20view3

## Six positive teaching situations supported by the research

**Construct a quadrilateral from defining relationships, then drag its vertices.**

**Learner’s task.** The learner constructs a quadrilateral so that its mathematical properties arise from construction dependencies—for example, through constrained points, lines, or relationships—then drags allowable points and explains which properties remain invariant. The instructional goal is to move from “it looks like a rectangle/parallelogram” toward reasoning about the relationships that make it one. Jones studied precisely this broader transition in a longitudinal investigation of 12-year-olds working with dynamic geometry and explaining properties of quadrilaterals they constructed. citeturn20view1

**What the figure makes possible.** An accurately constrained construction creates a distinction that a single hand sketch cannot reliably provide: **free variation in irrelevant appearance while mathematical dependencies remain fixed**. A point can move substantially on the screen while a required relationship continues to hold. That gives the learner repeated instances of the same mathematical structure without the teacher having to draw many separate examples. Jones reported that students’ explanations developed from imprecise everyday descriptions, through explanations explicitly mediated by the software, toward mathematical accounts that could transcend the particular tool. citeturn20view1

**What must be accurate.** Any dependency being used to define or justify the quadrilateral must be real in the construction: point-on-line incidence, shared vertices, parallel or perpendicular lines, midpoint dependencies, equal sides or angles when those are defining conditions, and the intersections generated from them. If a student is meant to infer a property because it survives dragging, the software must preserve exactly the prerequisite constraints whose consequences are being investigated. This is relational accuracy, not merely making the quadrilateral look plausible. citeturn20view1turn21view3

**What can be schematic.** Orientation, location on the page or screen, overall scale across different examples, elongation within whatever degrees of freedom the definition permits, color, and label placement can vary. In fact, that variation helps prevent the *particular picture* from standing in for the geometrical concept. Fischbein’s theoretical analysis explicitly locates a tension between the compelling image and the conceptual constraints that should govern geometric reasoning. citeturn21view3

**Research finding and limit.** This is strong evidence for the *process* by which an exact, constraint-preserving construction can support mathematical explanation, but not a randomized test of accurate versus inaccurate quadrilaterals. Jones’s study was longitudinal and interpretive; students’ tasks, teacher interaction, peer discourse, and the software itself all contributed. It therefore supports “use mathematically constrained figures to expose invariants” more securely than it supports “more precise-looking drawings cause higher achievement.” citeturn20view1

**Investigate congruence by dragging and measuring corresponding lengths and angles.**

**Learner’s task.** Students manipulate paired figures, measure corresponding parts, drag the configuration, and formulate criteria for when the figures remain congruent. Rather than deciding congruence principally from “same-looking shape,” they track corresponding distances and angles across changes. González and Herbst analyzed this type of work in an American geometry class using Cabri’s dragging and measuring tools. citeturn20view3

**What the figure makes possible.** An accurate dynamic figure can separate two ideas that are easily conflated in a static page: **appearance changes** because position and orientation can change, while **metric invariants remain constant**. The study’s analysis suggests that technology enabled a measure-preserving conception of congruency and helped students shift attention from shapes as visual wholes toward their properties. The authors also identified a “functional mode” in which students related changes produced by dragging inputs to resulting outputs. citeturn20view3

**What must be accurate.** Correspondence between vertices must be unambiguous; corresponding lengths and angles that are supposed to be preserved must actually be preserved; measurements must update consistently as the figure moves; and any construction dependencies used to establish correspondence must remain valid. A slightly distorted “congruent” copy is not merely cosmetically inaccurate here—it corrupts the very relationship the learner is investigating. citeturn20view3

**What can be schematic.** Translation on the screen, rotation, and—where reflections are allowed in the task—orientation reversal can vary. Colors and drawing conventions can also be schematic. Absolute size may differ **between separate examples**, but within a claimed congruent pair corresponding distances cannot be rescaled: uniform scaling would change the relation from congruence to similarity. citeturn20view3

**Research finding and limit.** The paper provides detailed evidence of changes in students’ conceptions and discourse, not a controlled estimate of the effect of measurement fidelity. Its authors explicitly caution that the conception they describe is a model for explaining some student behavior, not a claim that it captures all practices involving congruence. Classroom discourse and interaction with the tools are part of the mechanism. citeturn20view3 [Stable DOI: 10.1007/s10758-009-9152-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10758-009-9152-z)

**Explore reflections, rotations, and translations with a correctly mapped preimage and image.**

**Learner’s task.** Students manipulate a geometric object and its transformed image, varying the object or transformation and observing how corresponding points, lengths, angles, and spatial relationships behave. A reflection task, for example, needs the image to remain tied to the correct axis; a rotation task needs correspondence to the actual center and angle of rotation. Güven tested an instructional unit of this general kind with eighth graders. citeturn19view1

**What the figure makes possible.** A dynamically accurate transformation lets students perceive a transformation as a **mapping governed by a rule**, rather than as two static pictures they must compare by eye. When a preimage point moves, its image responds according to the same transformation. This makes the invariant and covariant relationships perceptually accessible while students interact with them. In Güven’s pre/post quasi-experiment, 36 students learned transformation geometry in a dynamic geometry environment and 32 comparison students received the instruction using dotted and isometric worksheets; covariance analysis favored the dynamic group on both academic achievement and measured learning level. citeturn19view1

**What must be accurate.** The point correspondence and transformation parameters are indispensable: the reflection axis, rotation center and angle, or translation vector must genuinely determine the image. For rigid motions, corresponding distances and angles must be preserved exactly; for a reflection, the geometric relation between corresponding points and the mirror line cannot merely be approximated. Otherwise the student is studying an incorrect transformation. citeturn19view1

**What can be schematic.** The initial position, orientation, screen location, colors, and choice of a particular polygon can vary unless the task asks about them. Indeed, varying the preimage is valuable because the transformation rule should remain operative across multiple configurations. citeturn19view1

**Research finding and limit.** This is one of the stronger comparative studies in the set because it used pre/post measures and a comparison group. But it still compares **instructional environments**, not exact versus inexact transformations while holding everything else constant. The dynamic group received dragging, continuous update, and computer interaction unavailable in the worksheet condition, so the outcome cannot be attributed solely to geometric fidelity. citeturn19view1 [Stable DOI: 10.14742/ajet.878](https://doi.org/10.14742/ajet.878)

**Relate a three-dimensional object to accurate two-dimensional views or projections.**

**Learner’s task.** Learners inspect and manipulate three-dimensional solids and interpret corresponding two-dimensional views, mentally coordinating how the same spatial structure appears from different directions. This is the most directly supported positive case in the reviewed literature for projection/view reasoning. citeturn19view0

**What the figure makes possible.** A manipulable, geometrically coherent 3D model makes it possible to link visible changes in a solid to changes in its projected views rather than mentally reconstructing the relation from one fixed sketch. Baki, Kösa, and Güven assigned first-year preservice mathematics teachers to Cabri 3D instruction (n=34), physical-manipulative instruction (n=32), or traditional instruction (n=30). Both 3D approaches produced better spatial-visualization outcomes than traditional instruction, and the Cabri 3D group did better than the physical-manipulative group on the **views** portion of the Purdue Spatial Visualization Test. citeturn19view0

**What must be accurate.** The correspondence among vertices, edges, faces, and views must be consistent; the orientation of the spatial object relative to the viewing direction must drive the two-dimensional representation; and features that coincide, disappear, or change apparent arrangement under a view must follow the actual projection model used in the task. A two-dimensional sketch in which the view has been “beautified” by independently moving features can destroy the spatial inference the learner is meant to make. citeturn19view0

**What can be schematic.** Surface color, texture, lighting, and pictorial realism are not implicated by this evidence. Uniform display size and location can also be schematic when the learner’s task is to identify spatial correspondence rather than calculate physical dimensions. The crucial fidelity is the **mapping between spatial structure and view**, not photorealism. citeturn19view0

**Research finding and limit.** The advantage on the views subtest is particularly relevant, but the experiment studied **spatial visualization training**, not a direct manipulation of projection accuracy. Cabri 3D differs from both physical models and traditional instruction in interaction and representation. The sample was also preservice mathematics teachers, so transfer to younger students should not be assumed without additional evidence. citeturn19view0 [Stable DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-8535.2009.01012.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2009.01012.x)

**Use accurate, dynamically updated lengths and angles to generate and test a conjecture.**

**Learner’s task.** Students work on an open geometry problem, construct the relevant points, lines, and intersections, measure selected lengths or angles, drag the configuration through multiple cases, formulate a general relationship, and then seek an explanation or proof. Olivero and Robutti analyzed the proving processes of 15–16-year-olds specifically to understand different ways measurement in a dynamic geometry environment can function during conjecturing and proving. citeturn19view2

**What the figure makes possible.** A static accurate diagram supplies one instance. A dynamic accurate construction supplies a **family of related instances** while continuously recomputing the relevant quantities. Olivero and Robutti note that measurements update in real time as a figure is dragged and distinguish different “modalities” of measuring that can either support or hinder movement between the spatial-graphical and theoretical sides of students’ reasoning. citeturn19view2

This is especially relevant to relationships involving **points, intersections, lengths, and angles**. When a conjecture concerns an intersection-generated point or an equality of lengths or angles, exact dependency and measurement ensure that observed regularity is evidence about the intended construction rather than an artifact of a roughly drawn picture. The repeated values can provide a basis for conjecture formation while dragging exposes the conjecture to many configurations. citeturn19view2

**What must be accurate.** The points and intersections defining the problem, the constraints used to create them, and every measurement on which the conjecture depends must be internally correct and update with the construction. If the hypothesized relationship is angular, angular measurement matters; if it is a distance relationship, corresponding distances matter. Properties outside the conjecture can remain schematic. citeturn19view2

**What can be schematic.** The drawing need not retain one preferred shape or orientation. Indeed, dragging should alter irrelevant lengths, angles, orientation, or proportions wherever the problem permits them to vary. This variability is what distinguishes a conjecture about a mathematical invariant from an observation about one conveniently drawn case. citeturn19view2turn21view3

**Research finding and limit.** This paper is unusually useful for the present question because it directly examines *measurement* as a reasoning instrument, but the result is not simply “more accurate measurement improves proof.” The authors explicitly found that different uses and interpretations of measurement may **support or hinder** proving. Exact numerical evidence can therefore help generate or organize a conjecture without constituting its deductive justification. citeturn19view2 [Stable DOI: 10.1007/s10758-007-9115-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10758-007-9115-1)

**Use an exact construction to produce a genuine counterexample, then require proof.**

**Learner’s task.** Students make a plausible conjecture, test it by manipulating a correctly constrained construction, encounter a configuration that contradicts the conjecture or their initial interpretation, and then have to revise the claim or explain deductively why the corrected claim must hold. Hadas, Hershkowitz, and Schwarz deliberately designed dynamic-geometry activities around such contradiction and uncertainty. citeturn20view2

**What the figure makes possible.** A geometrically reliable environment gives a counterexample special force. When a relationship fails under dragging while the stipulated construction constraints are still being maintained, students cannot plausibly dismiss the failure as “the teacher drew it badly.” Hadas and colleagues designed activities intended to produce surprise and uncertainty through contradictions between students’ conjectures and the findings generated in the environment, specifically to challenge confidence based only on empirical experience and create a need for deductive explanation. citeturn20view2

This is an important case where accuracy serves an apparently paradoxical educational goal: **not to make the theorem visually obvious, but to make refutation trustworthy**. Exact incidence relations, intersections, constraints, and relevant measurements have to survive the manipulation so that a counterexample is mathematically legitimate. Irrelevant aspects of the picture should remain free to vary. citeturn20view2

**What must be accurate.** Every hypothesis of the proposed theorem must remain satisfied in a purported counterexample. If the claim presupposes a midpoint, parallel lines, a particular intersection, equal lengths, or a fixed angle, those conditions have to be genuinely maintained. Otherwise the “counterexample” teaches the wrong logical lesson. citeturn20view2

**What can be schematic.** Everything not included among the theorem’s hypotheses or the relation being tested can vary. This is precisely the situation in which making an incidental feature look canonical can be counterproductive: the learner should see that the theorem or counterexample is controlled by stated assumptions rather than by one prototypical picture. citeturn20view2turn21view3

**Research finding and limit.** Hadas et al. analyzed students’ conjectures, methods, and explanations in two designed activities; this was not an experiment estimating an accuracy effect. The intervention intentionally combined an exact dynamic environment with specially engineered uncertainty. Complementary proof studies likewise report productive uses of dynamic geometry within longer teaching sequences, including Mariotti’s ninth- and tenth-grade teaching experiment and Marrades and Gutiérrez’s two secondary-school case studies, but these also embed the software in substantial teaching and discourse rather than isolating figure accuracy. citeturn20view2turn22view0turn22view1 [Stable DOI for Hadas et al.: 10.1023/A:1012781005718](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1012781005718)

## Evidence about accurate construction versus evidence that “visuals help”

The distinction is consequential because the broad outcome literature is stronger than the accuracy-specific literature.

| Evidence type | What it establishes | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamic-geometry meta-analysis | Across nine quasi-experimental studies and 587 participants, DGS instruction had higher mathematical-achievement outcomes than traditional instruction, with SMD 1.02 (95% CI 0.56–1.48). citeturn21view0 | It does not isolate exact construction from dragging, measurement, feedback, instructional time, task design, or teacher practice. |
| Transformation quasi-experiment | Eighth graders using a dynamic geometry environment outperformed worksheet instruction on transformation achievement and learning-level measures. citeturn19view1 | It does not show that an exact reflection/rotation drawn statically would outperform a schematic but properly labeled one. |
| 3D spatial-visualization quasi-experiment | Cabri 3D and physical manipulatives outperformed traditional instruction; Cabri 3D did particularly well on spatial “views.” citeturn19view0 | It does not isolate projection fidelity from manipulation and dynamic viewpoint change. |
| Construction/congruence longitudinal or classroom studies | Students can use preserved dependencies, dragging, and measurement to shift attention toward properties, invariants, and mathematical explanation. citeturn20view1turn20view3 | They do not give a clean causal effect size for “accurate versus approximate figure.” |
| Conjecture/proof studies | Dynamically accurate measurements and constraint-preserving variation can participate in conjecture generation, contradiction, and transitions toward proof. citeturn19view2turn20view2turn22view1 | Empirical confirmation from a diagram is not itself proof, and measurement can sometimes hinder rather than support the proving process. |

The most defensible synthesis is therefore **not** “make every geometry picture perfectly to scale.” It is: **make every relationship that the learner must use as evidence or manipulate as a hypothesis geometrically reliable; allow irrelevant appearance to vary or remain schematic.** Dynamic geometry is effective partly because it operationalizes exactly this distinction: the system can hold selected relationships fixed while visibly changing the rest. The empirical studies on congruence, quadrilateral construction, transformations, conjecturing, and proof consistently make use of that affordance. citeturn20view1turn20view3turn19view1turn19view2

There is also a reason **not** to over-perfect a figure. Fischbein’s analysis emphasizes that the visual image and formal concept can pull reasoning in different directions; Jones describes the educational problem as moving students beyond “because it looks right”; and Hadas et al. show that learners can become convinced of universality through empirical interaction even when what mathematics ultimately requires is deductive explanation. A precise diagram should therefore expose mathematical structure, not substitute visual certainty for proof. citeturn21view3turn20view1turn20view2

Mariotti’s long-term teaching experiment makes the same broader point from another direction: Cabri’s particular features functioned as instruments within teacher-mediated classroom activity aimed at developing theoretical thinking. The value of the figure cannot be separated completely from what the teacher asks learners to notice, articulate, and justify. citeturn22view0

## Gaps, conflicts, and practical boundary conditions

**There is little clean causal evidence comparing “accurately drawn” and “schematically drawn” static figures.** The strongest literature located here concerns dynamic constructions that accurately preserve mathematical constraints. It therefore supports accuracy of **structure and invariants** much more directly than it supports literal scale fidelity in textbook-style drawings. The meta-analysis establishes an overall DGS advantage but expressly synthesizes instructional packages rather than an accuracy manipulation. citeturn21view0

**Metric accuracy is beneficial only when metric information legitimately bears on the task.** González and Herbst provide positive evidence when measurement is central to learning congruence, whereas Olivero and Robutti show that measuring during proving can either support or hinder the transition from spatial-graphical exploration to theoretical reasoning. Thus a ruler-like diagram is useful for generating and testing a metric conjecture, but encouraging students to “read off” a theorem from a figure can work against proof. citeturn20view3turn19view2

**Dynamic confirmation creates its own epistemic risk.** Hadas and colleagues begin from the problem that students may feel a universally quantified geometric claim has been validated simply because it continues to work while they drag a figure. Their use of contradiction was specifically meant to disrupt that empirical conviction and motivate deduction. Accurate figures should therefore be used to ask *what seems invariant and why?*, not to imply that a large number of observed cases proves the theorem. citeturn20view2

**The evidence for projection/view learning is positive but narrow.** Baki et al. found a specific advantage for Cabri 3D on the “views” component of a spatial visualization test, alongside broader advantages of both virtual and physical manipulation over traditional teaching. That supports geometrically coherent 3D-to-2D representations, but it does not tell us which projection errors are most harmful, how much dimensional distortion can be tolerated, or whether an accurate static orthographic drawing is superior to a deliberately schematic one. citeturn19view0

**Area is a notable gap in this review.** I did not find a qualifying original study in the searched literature that cleanly tests whether geometrically accurate area figures—such as exact base-height constructions or area-preserving rearrangements—improve learning *because of that accuracy*. It would be easy to infer mathematically that a task about triangle area needs the relevant base-height or decomposition relationships to be correct, but that is not the same as an empirical learning finding, so I have not promoted an area example into the six evidence-backed situations above.

**Nor does the literature justify a universal fidelity rule.** Different reasoning goals demand different exact properties. For congruence, corresponding distances and angles are critical; for transformations, the mapping parameters and point correspondence are critical; for projection, the view mapping is critical; for a theorem test, every hypothesis must remain satisfied; for proof, by contrast, exact visual appearance is ultimately insufficient. The common design principle is therefore **selective fidelity**: accurately instantiate whatever relations carry the inference and deliberately treat nonessential visual properties as variable or schematic. That conclusion is a synthesis across the cited studies, not the result of a single factorial experiment. citeturn20view3turn19view1turn19view0turn20view2turn21view3

### Decision rule for figure design

For a teacher or curriculum designer, the evidence supports asking: **“Could a learner reach a mathematically wrong conclusion because this particular depicted property is wrong?”** If yes, that property should be constructed accurately. If changing the property leaves the intended theorem, category, transformation, or spatial relation unchanged, it can usually remain schematic—and, in dynamic work, allowing it to vary may actually make the invariant structure clearer. The studies above most strongly support this rule for constraint-based constructions, congruence, transformations, spatial views, conjecture generation, and the transition from empirical testing to proof. citeturn20view1turn20view3turn19view1turn19view0turn19view2turn20view2
