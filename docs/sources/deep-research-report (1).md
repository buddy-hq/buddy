# When Learning Benefits From Examining the Source Itself

## How I judged the evidence

Across the research, the clearest educational case for putting the source itself in front of learners is **not** simply that authentic materials are more engaging. The stronger case is that the source contains information that a summary would suppress: authorship and wording in historical documents, relationships among plotted variables in a scientific figure, spatial structure in a radiologic image or contour map, provenance links on the open web, or scale and axis choices in a graph. Learning improves when instruction makes learners *do something epistemic with those details*—source, trace, compare, infer, explain, or correct them—rather than merely look at the material. This pattern appears across history, science education, spatial learning, digital-information evaluation, and visualization research. citeturn20search8turn21view1turn17view0turn21view4

A crucial qualification runs through all six situations below: **evidence for source-based learning is much stronger than evidence that a particular physical or digital medium is uniquely necessary.** For example, document-based history research supports comparing accounts, but generally does not establish that a scanned facsimile is better than a faithful transcription. Primary-literature research supports reasoning from figures and data, but does not establish that a journal-layout PDF is better than the same figure presented cleanly. Radiology research supports integrating medical images into anatomy teaching, but usually bundles the images with additional instruction. I therefore separate the demonstrated instructional finding from the inference that follows about a specific medium. citeturn20search4turn21view1turn21view2

## Corroborating competing historical documents

**Teaching situation.** Give students two or more accounts of the same historical event that differ in perspective—for example, an official statement, a participant's account, and a contemporary newspaper or image—and require a written judgment that reconciles the disagreement. Before judging the event, students identify who produced each source, when, for whom, and for what apparent purpose; mark wording that affects interpretation; and identify points on which independent sources agree or conflict. This is the document-based activity structure tested in the *Reading Like a Historian* line of research rather than the conventional task of extracting facts from a single textbook narrative. citeturn20search4turn20search8

**What learners must inspect in the original.** Authorship, date, audience, genre, claims, omissions, loaded or uncertain wording, and the relationship between one account and another are the evidence. The pedagogical point is that “what the document says” and “why this particular source says it this way” become separate questions. A paraphrased textbook synthesis can preserve the first while removing much of the second. Nokes, Dole, and Hacker likewise studied instruction in historians' heuristics while students worked with historical texts, including sourcing and corroboration rather than treating every text as an interchangeable container of facts. citeturn20search1

**What they are expected to learn.** The target is disciplinary historical reasoning: sourcing claims, contextualizing them, corroborating across documents, and constructing an interpretation whose certainty is proportional to the evidence. Reisman's curriculum intervention involved 236 eleventh graders in five San Francisco high schools; treatment students outperformed comparison students on historical-thinking measures. Nokes and colleagues studied 246 students from two high schools while varying text/instructional conditions, providing complementary evidence that historical heuristics and multiple-source reasoning can be taught rather than left to emerge spontaneously. citeturn20search8turn20search1

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** The supported finding is **“students can learn historical reasoning by interrogating and reconciling multiple historical texts.”** It is a further inference—not established by these studies—that displaying an archival scan, handwriting, typography, seals, marginalia, or the original page arrangement will improve learning over a complete transcription. Those visual features should therefore be retained when they themselves are evidence—for example, when students must reason about redactions, annotations, newspaper layout, or the relation between image and caption—but the intervention literature does not justify a general “facsimiles are better” rule. The studies also bundle documents with teacher modeling, questions, discussion, and structured comparison, so the causal contribution of document exposure alone cannot be isolated. citeturn20search4turn20search8

Stable original publications: [Reisman, *Cognition and Instruction* (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2011.634081); [Nokes, Dole & Hacker, *Journal of Educational Psychology* (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.492).

## Reasoning from a scientific figure before reading the authors' interpretation

**Teaching situation.** In an undergraduate science course, reveal the Methods and a figure or table from a primary research paper before students read the Results or Discussion. Students identify the independent and dependent variables, controls, comparison groups, axes, units, uncertainty or statistical annotations, and the pattern in the data; they then write the result they believe the figure warrants. Only afterward do they read the authors' prose and compare their interpretation with it. Round and Campbell's “Figure Facts” approach and the C.R.E.A.T.E. model are concrete versions of this data-centered primary-literature pedagogy. citeturn20search2turn21view1

**What learners must inspect in the original.** The indispensable content is the actual evidentiary structure of the figure: what was measured, which values are being compared, the magnitude and variability of differences, labels and legends, and whether the visible data actually support the verbal claim. In C.R.E.A.T.E., primary papers are broken down so students analyze figures and experiments rather than simply accepting the article's conclusions; its name explicitly includes “Analyze and interpret data” and “Think of the next Experiment.” citeturn21view1

**What they are expected to learn.** The immediate goals are experimental reasoning and data interpretation: moving from an observed pattern to a defensible claim, distinguishing results from interpretation, identifying what an experiment can and cannot establish, and proposing a next experiment. Round and Campbell reported that students using the Figure Facts scaffold shifted more attention toward figures and developed stronger data-interpretation performance over the course. C.R.E.A.T.E. studies have also reported gains in students' confidence in reading and analyzing primary literature and changes in views of scientific knowledge and practice. citeturn20search2turn20search6turn21view1

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** The evidence supports **structured reasoning from primary data displays**, especially when a scaffold forces learners to explain each figure instead of skimming it. It does *not* show that an untouched publisher PDF, its journal typography, or even the full paper is educationally superior to presenting the identical figure and caption separately. Indeed, Figure Facts intentionally adds a scaffold, so its positive result is evidence for *guided figure inspection*, not for passive exposure to primary literature. The course studies are also substantially weaker causal evidence than large randomized trials: they are relatively small, embedded in particular biology courses, and combine source reading with repeated practice, discussion, templates, and instructor feedback. citeturn20search2turn21view1

Stable original publications: [Round & Campbell, *CBE—Life Sciences Education* (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.11-07-0057); [Hoskins, Stevens & Nehm, *Genetics* (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.107.071183); [Hoskins, Lopatto & Stevens, *CBE—Life Sciences Education* (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.11-03-0027).

## Mapping anatomy onto real radiologic images

**Teaching situation.** After students learn an anatomical region from an atlas, model, or dissection, give them a radiologic view—most strongly supported in the current evidence by ultrasound—and ask them to locate the same structures, identify orientation and imaging plane, trace boundaries and relationships, and explain how the two-dimensional image corresponds to the three-dimensional body. A productive variation is to alternate between an anatomical representation and an unlabeled radiologic view rather than merely showing a labeled scan during lecture. citeturn21view2

**What learners must inspect in the original.** They need the actual geometry and visual signal of the image: relative position, depth, cross-sectional orientation, boundaries, echogenicity or other modality-dependent appearance, and neighboring structures that make an identification plausible. These are precisely the features lost when the task is reduced to memorizing a verbal description such as “structure A lies anterior to structure B.” Radiologic representations require learners to recognize anatomy under the appearance conditions in which clinicians later encounter it. citeturn21view2

**What they are expected to learn.** A 2025/2026 systematic review by Alexander, Montalbano, and Loukas screened 983 records and included 52 studies comparing radiology-integrated anatomy education with more traditional approaches. Across studies with quantitative knowledge outcomes, the review reported a pooled Cohen's \(d=0.99\), although the estimate was extremely heterogeneous (\(I^2=99.5\%\)). The intended learning is therefore best described conservatively as better anatomical knowledge and the ability to connect conventional anatomy to clinical imaging—not as proof of a uniform large effect across every imaging modality and curriculum. citeturn21view2

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** The source-based conclusion is **radiologic representations can be productively integrated into anatomy learning**. The evidence is much weaker for saying that any particular imaging medium is responsible. Ultrasound appeared in 85% of studies in the review, CT in about 15%, and MRI/X-ray in fewer than 5%; only 21% of included studies used controlled designs, while many relied on attitudinal outcomes. Thus the pooled result cannot establish that CT, MRI, or an “authentic patient scan” is superior to an equivalently informative teaching image, nor can it cleanly separate image inspection from the instructor guidance, clinical context, or active scanning bundled with it. citeturn21view2

Stable original publication: [Alexander, Montalbano & Loukas, *Clinical Anatomy* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ca.70048).

## Tracing a topographic map to reconstruct the terrain

**Teaching situation.** Give novices an unfamiliar contour map and have them trace selected contour lines with a finger or stylus while predicting elevation, steepness, valleys/ridges, or the three-dimensional terrain shape. Ask them to justify each prediction by pointing to a specific contour configuration rather than supplying a verbal answer from memory. A particularly useful comparison is to have students sketch a profile or choose among several possible three-dimensional landforms after tracing the map. citeturn17view0

**What learners must inspect in the original.** Here the source's geometry *is* the content: contour values, spacing, nesting, and the configuration of lines encode a continuous elevation surface. To succeed, learners must understand both the representational code—equal-elevation lines—and how local line patterns combine into a three-dimensional shape. A prose summary such as “this is a steep hill with a valley to the east” removes the very representational translation the learner is supposed to master. citeturn17view0

**What they are expected to learn.** Atit and colleagues conducted two experiments specifically on novice topographic-map comprehension. In the first, pointing and tracing gestures that directed attention to contour lines produced better understanding than text-only instruction and a no-instruction comparison; three-dimensional shape gestures had a more limited advantage. In the second experiment, language directing attention to elevation selectively improved elevation-related performance, while language emphasizing shape improved shape-related items. The authors interpret the results as evidence that directing attention to the relevant lines helps learners decode the representation and infer terrain shape. citeturn17view0

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** This is unusually direct evidence that **attention to exact graphical structure matters**: the instructional manipulation literally directed learners to the contour lines carrying the information. But the study does not show that an authentic government topographic sheet is superior to a simplified instructional map, nor that paper is superior to a screen. In fact, adding irrelevant cartographic complexity could plausibly make novice learning harder; the demonstrated principle is to preserve the representation that must be decoded and guide attention to it. The authors suggest that the principle could generalize to other layered representations such as weather maps, but that generalization is an inference rather than a result directly tested in this study. citeturn17view0

Stable original publication: [Atit, Weisberg, Newcombe & Shipley, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-016-0002-y).

## Tracing an online claim back through its sources

**Teaching situation.** Give students a live webpage making a substantive civic or scientific claim. Rather than asking “Does this page look trustworthy?”, require them to identify the claim and publisher, open new tabs to investigate who is behind the site, locate independent reporting or reference works about the organization, and—when the page cites evidence—follow that citation to the original report, dataset, or research paper and compare what it actually says with the webpage's wording. This is the “lateral reading” family of activities. citeturn21view4

**What learners must inspect in the originals.** Several sources matter simultaneously: the precise claim on the target page; the identity and reputation of the organization making it; the bibliographic trail or links offered as evidence; and the wording/data in the underlying source. An especially important lesson is that visual polish, a `.org` domain, an “About” page, or the mere presence of citations is not itself sufficient evidence of credibility. The strategy deliberately moves learners away from prolonged inspection of a self-authored webpage and toward comparison with independent sources. citeturn21view4

**What they are expected to learn.** Wineburg, Breakstone, McGrew, Smith, and Ortega tested six 50-minute lessons taught by regular high-school teachers across an urban district. In a matched-control design, students in intervention classrooms (\(n=271\)) were compared with students in regular classrooms (\(n=228\)); the intervention group improved significantly more in judging the credibility of digital content. Earlier intervention work by McGrew likewise found that civic-online-reasoning instruction can improve students' online source evaluation. citeturn21view4turn15search0

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** The supported conclusion is **learners become better evaluators when taught to investigate provenance and corroborate a source against the wider information environment**. This is notably different from the claim that “carefully inspecting the original webpage” by itself helps. In fact, lateral-reading research warns against remaining inside an unfamiliar site and overinterpreting its design. The open web is useful because it permits fast cross-source investigation, but the field study does not prove that a live browser is indispensable; a carefully constructed packet containing the same source relationships might teach similar reasoning. Nor does improvement in credibility judgment mean students became universally accurate fact-checkers or that gains necessarily persist indefinitely. citeturn21view4

Stable original publications: [Wineburg et al., *Journal of Educational Psychology* (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000740); [McGrew, *Computers & Education* (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2019.103711).

## Comparing a misleading graph with an accurate rendering of the same data

**Teaching situation.** Present a graph that gives an intuitively compelling but distorted impression—for example, a bar chart with a truncated vertical axis or a pictorial chart whose icons are incorrectly area-scaled. Before explaining the problem, have learners state the conclusion the graph seems to communicate, then inspect the axes, numerical values, intervals, scaling, labels, and graphical marks. Next put a corrected representation of the *same quantities* beside it and require students to explain exactly which design choice changed their impression. Finally, test the principle on a new graph using the same deceptive technique. citeturn19view0turn18search9

**What learners must inspect in the original.** Exact visual encoding is central here. A learner has to compare bar heights with the axis origin, icon area with the value represented, or graphical spacing with numerical intervals. Wijnker and colleagues' 2026 study used accurate and misleading versions of bar graphs, pictorial-area graphs, and pie charts; misleading bar charts used truncated axes, while pictorial charts and pie charts manipulated other graphical conventions. Separately, Rho, Rau, and Zhu experimentally compared 14 forms of misleading visualization and found that several axis manipulations—including inverted axes, irregular intervals, and dual axes—substantially reduced interpretation accuracy, with some distortions affecting even learners with relatively high visualization literacy. citeturn19view0turn18search9

**What they are expected to learn.** The target is critical graph literacy: separating the quantities actually represented from the perceptual impression produced by the design and transferring that scrutiny to a new visualization. In Wijnker and colleagues' mixed-method study, the main quantitative sample comprised 130 vocationally educated young adults. Showing corrected versions reduced the misleading effect, and participants were less misled by new graphs using the same type of distortion; whether the correction retained the original colorful design or used a cleaner version made no detectable difference. citeturn19view0

**Source-based finding versus medium inference.** This is strong evidence that the *structure of the graphical representation itself* can change comprehension, and useful evidence that comparison with a correction can support learning. But the study's charts contained fictional data designed for the experiment, not authentic news graphics. Therefore, the recommendation to use an actual published chart and its underlying dataset is a **pedagogically reasonable inference**, not something that study directly established. The correction study also lacked a simple no-correction arm for all of its transfer comparisons, so its “learning effect” should not be interpreted as the same kind of clean causal estimate as a randomized treatment-versus-no-treatment trial. Rho and colleagues establish that distortions can mislead, but their experiment tested interpretation rather than an instructional intervention. citeturn19view0turn18search9

Stable original publications: [Wijnker et al., *PLOS ONE* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0340100); [Rho, Rau & Zhu, *Computers & Education* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2025.105508).

## What the evidence does and does not establish

Taken together, these literatures support a fairly specific principle: **show the source when the learning goal requires a judgment that can only be warranted by features of the source, and give learners a task that makes those features consequential.** Historical learners have to reconcile who said what; science learners have to derive a claim from displayed data; anatomy learners have to map structures onto clinical imagery; map learners have to decode contour geometry; web learners have to follow provenance across sources; graph learners have to distinguish numbers from their visual framing. In each case, replacing the source with a teacher's verbal conclusion would remove at least part of the reasoning the activity is designed to teach. citeturn20search8turn20search2turn21view2turn17view0turn21view4turn19view0

The evidence does **not** support a blanket rule that “originals are always better.” Most positive studies combine the source with substantial instructional support: sourcing prompts and class discussion in history, Figure Facts or C.R.E.A.T.E. scaffolds in biology, integrated teaching around medical images, gestures and targeted language for maps, explicit lateral-reading lessons online, or side-by-side corrections for graphs. The empirically safer conclusion is that **guided inspection, comparison, and explanation are doing important work alongside the source itself**. Passive authenticity is not what these studies tested. citeturn20search4turn21view1turn17view0turn21view4turn19view0

There is also a recurring **medium gap**. I found substantially less credible experimental evidence comparing a facsimile with an accurate transcription, an original journal figure with a redrawn equivalent, an authentic patient image with a matched instructional image, paper maps with digital maps, or a live webpage with a faithful static capture. Where exact visual form is itself the object of learning—as with contours and misleading graph axes—the case for retaining that form is direct. Where the learning goal is sourcing, corroboration, or reasoning from data, fidelity to the *evidentially relevant information* is better supported than fidelity to every incidental feature of the original medium. citeturn17view0turn18search9turn20search8turn21view1

Finally, transfer and durability remain important gaps. The lateral-reading field experiment demonstrates improvement after a relatively short classroom intervention, the graph-correction study shows near-term transfer to new graphs of the same misleading type, and the topographic experiments demonstrate transfer within closely related map tasks; these do not establish that students will spontaneously invoke the same reasoning months later, across radically different subject matter, or without a prompt. The radiology review likewise reports very high between-study heterogeneity and relatively few controlled studies. Thus, the most defensible use of these activities is **repeated source-based practice across varied examples**, with learners continually required to point to the evidence that warrants their interpretation, rather than assuming that a single encounter with an “original” will create a general critical-thinking skill. citeturn21view4turn19view0turn17view0turn21view2
